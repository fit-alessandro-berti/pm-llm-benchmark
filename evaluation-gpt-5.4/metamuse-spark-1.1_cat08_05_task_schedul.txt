## Major deductions

### 1. Several technical inaccuracies or dubious formulations
These are the biggest reason the score is not much higher.

#### a) Incorrect or misleading “lead time vs planned lead time” formula
> `Lead Time vs. Planned Lead Time = Flow Time - (DueDate - Release Date)`

This is not “planned lead time” unless the due date minus release date is explicitly the promised/planned lead time. Often it is a commercial commitment, not an operational plan. This is a **conceptual error**.

#### b) “Little’s Law violation”
The answer says:
> “This is Lack's Law violation - WIP directly inflates flow time.”

This is a serious flaw:
- It should be **Little’s Law**, not “Lack’s Law.”
- More importantly, **Little’s Law is not something you ‘violate’** in this sense. It is an identity under steady-state assumptions, not a managerial rule being broken.

This is a notable technical blemish in an otherwise sophisticated answer.

#### c) “Blocked” state used incorrectly
It says:
> “Blocked: Downtime due to Breakdown”

That is generally not what **blocked** means in manufacturing/queueing terms. A broken machine is unavailable/down, not blocked. Blocking typically means inability to release output because downstream capacity is unavailable.

#### d) TSP simplification for sequence optimization
> “If we solved a TSP for the observed job pool...”

This is too loose. The sequencing problem here is not simply TSP:
- there are release times,
- due dates,
- precedence constraints,
- potentially parallel/alternative machines,
- hot jobs,
- disruptions.

Using TSP as shorthand is understandable, but presented this way it is **technically oversimplified**.

#### e) Difference-in-differences for disruption impact
Using DID for breakdown impact is presented too casually:
- causal identification assumptions are not discussed,
- parallel trends are implausible in many shop-floor contexts,
- event overlap and selection effects are likely.

As stated, it sounds more rigorous than it really is.

### 3. Some process mining choices are not fully rigorous
#### a) Imputing missing Task End from next Queue Entry
> “We clean: impute missing Task End via next Queue Entry”

This is dangerous and may be wrong:
- next queue entry may be delayed,
- logging may be asynchronous,
- rework/inspection/transport can intervene,
- parallel event timing can break that assumption.

Under a strict standard, this is an overly casual data-engineering assumption.

#### b) “Filter 90% most frequent variants”
For a **high-mix, low-volume job shop**, aggressive variant filtering is risky and may remove exactly the rare but operationally critical cases. The answer does not justify this enough.

#### c) “Process discovery on setup events only” to show sequence dependence
Process discovery is not really the correct tool to determine whether setup duration depends on predecessor state. That is more a **statistical/ML problem** than a control-flow discovery problem.
