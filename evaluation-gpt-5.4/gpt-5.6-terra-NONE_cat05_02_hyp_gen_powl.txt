Why it loses substantial points:

   - The section about “no explicit association between the assigned adjuster and events” is more of a data/modeling-governance concern than an anomaly in the given control-flow structure. It’s a useful audit idea, but it does not directly answer the POWL anomaly question.

2. Some wording is slightly imprecise about concurrency/execution.
   - Saying executions like `R A C / E P N` can occur “concurrently” is a bit loose. In event logs you observe linearizations, not true concurrency. The answer should have been more careful in distinguishing partial-order permissiveness from observed trace order.

3. A few SQL checks are too coarse for the stated purpose.
   - The “notification-skipping frequency” query counts a claim as notified if `N` occurs at any time, even after `C`. That weakens the anomaly detection materially.
   - Several queries rely only on `MIN(timestamp)` per activity. That is acceptable for a first pass, but it can misclassify more complex cases with repeated closes, repeated approvals, or administrative post-close events.
   - The `E-P-E` loop query detects `P` followed by later `E`, but does not fully reconstruct the pattern or prove loop semantics beyond that simple ordering.

4. Some hypotheses are plausible but not tightly tied to verification plans.
   - The answer gives good hypotheses, but it does not always show how to distinguish, for example, “policy change over time” vs. “technical logging gap” vs. “tooling error” with specific comparative queries by date, resource, region, or system actor.

   - The adjuster specialization query assumes mappings like `home_insurance -> home` and `auto_insurance -> auto`, which may be fine illustratively, but it is speculative.
