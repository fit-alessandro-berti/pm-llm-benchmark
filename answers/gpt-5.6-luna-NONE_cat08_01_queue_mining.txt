## 1. Queue Identification and Characterization

### 1.1 Prepare the event log

For each visit, reconstruct an ordered timeline using:

- Visit ID
- Activity
- Start timestamp
- Completion timestamp
- Resource, such as clinician, clerk, room, or equipment
- Patient type, specialty, urgency, appointment time, and other relevant attributes

The data should be checked for:

- Missing starts or completions
- Duplicate events
- Overlapping activities that are logically impossible
- Incorrect timestamp order
- Activities recorded under inconsistent names
- Cases that span multiple days or contain cancellations/no-shows

A complete start-and-completion record allows both service time and waiting time to be estimated.

### 1.2 Define waiting time

For an activity \(A_i\):

\[
\text{Service time}_{A_i}
=
\text{Completion}_{A_i}
-
\text{Start}_{A_i}
\]

If the preceding activity is \(A_{i-1}\), then the inter-activity waiting time is:

\[
\text{Wait}_{A_i}
=
\text{Start}_{A_i}
-
\text{Completion}_{A_{i-1}}
\]

For example, if Nurse Assessment ends at 09:25:10 and the doctor consultation starts at 09:45:55:

\[
\text{Wait before doctor}
=
20\text{ minutes }45\text{ seconds}
\]

This is the time the patient appears to be waiting between those activities, assuming there are no unrecorded activities.

Waiting should be distinguished from:

1. **Arrival-to-registration wait**  
   Arrival time, if available, to registration start.

2. **Queue wait before an activity**  
   Previous completion to next activity start.

3. **Total visit duration**

\[
\text{Visit duration}
=
\text{Final completion}
-
\text{First recorded arrival or activity start}
\]

4. **Active treatment time**  
   Sum of service durations.

5. **Unexplained or uncaptured time**  
   Time not attributable to a recorded activity, including possible movement, paperwork, delays, or missing events.

Where activities occur in parallel, a simple sequential calculation is inappropriate. The analysis should use the completion of the last required predecessor:

\[
\text{Wait for activity}
=
\text{Start of activity}
-
\max(\text{completion times of required predecessors})
\]

For example, a specialist review may require both an ECG and a blood test. The relevant wait begins after the later of those two completions.

### 1.3 Queue metrics

Metrics should be calculated overall and segmented by activity, specialty, resource, day of week, time of day, patient type, urgency, and appointment type.

Important measures include:

- Mean waiting time
- Median waiting time
- Standard deviation and interquartile range
- 90th, 95th, and 99th percentile waiting time
- Maximum waiting time
- Number and percentage of cases experiencing a wait above a threshold
- Queue frequency: number of visits passing through the queue
- Total waiting minutes generated
- Percentage of total visit duration attributable to waiting
- Service-time mean and variability
- Visit throughput per hour or per session
- Resource utilization and idle time
- Rework, repeat tests, or unnecessary transitions
- Abandonment, cancellation, or early departure rates
- Wait by patient segment and urgency level

The 90th percentile is especially useful because averages may hide severe delays. A queue with a 10-minute average but a 75-minute 90th percentile may be more problematic than a consistently moderate queue.

A useful prioritization measure is:

\[
\text{Total queue burden}
=
\text{Number of affected visits}
\times
\text{Average wait}
\]

This identifies queues that affect many patients, not only queues with the longest individual delays.

### 1.4 Identifying critical queues

I would rank queues using a composite assessment based on:

1. **Magnitude**  
   High average, median, or 90th-percentile wait.

2. **Frequency**  
   Large number or percentage of visits affected.

3. **Patient impact**  
   Effect on new patients, urgent patients, elderly patients, or patients requiring multiple services.

4. **Contribution to visit duration**  
   Queue time as a percentage of total visit duration.

5. **Operational importance**  
   Queues that block downstream activities or create cascading delays.

6. **Equity and safety**  
   Queues disproportionately affecting urgent or clinically vulnerable patients.

7. **Controllability**  
   Delays that can realistically be improved through scheduling, staffing, coordination, or redesign.

For example, a doctor consultation queue might be prioritized if it has a 35-minute median wait, a 70-minute 90th percentile, affects 65% of visits, and is responsible for the largest share of total visit delay. A laboratory queue might have a longer maximum wait but affect only a small number of cases and therefore receive a lower initial priority.

A queue heat map by hour and weekday can reveal whether the problem is persistent or concentrated in particular sessions.

## 2. Root Cause Analysis

Queue duration alone does not identify the cause. The analysis should connect waiting patterns to capacity, demand, process variants, and resource behavior.

### 2.1 Resource bottlenecks

For each staff member, room, and equipment resource, calculate:

- Number of activities performed
- Service time
- Utilization
- Concurrent workload
- Idle time
- Queue length immediately before service
- Waiting time associated with that resource
- Overtime and late-session activity
- Performance variation between comparable resources

Examples:

- One cardiologist may have substantially higher downstream waiting than other physicians.
- ECG Room 3 may operate near full capacity while another room is underused.
- A registration clerk may be unavailable during a predictable morning surge.
- A nurse may be pulled into urgent cases, causing routine assessments to accumulate.

Resource-specific analysis can be supported with resource-performance dashboards, utilization analysis, and bottleneck analysis. Resource utilization should be interpreted carefully: 100% utilization may indicate efficiency in the short term but often creates long queues because there is no capacity buffer for variability.

### 2.2 Activity dependencies and handovers

Some queues are caused by poor coordination rather than insufficient resources. Examples include:

- Doctor consultation cannot begin until nurse documentation is completed.
- Specialist review waits for test results even when the patient is physically available.
- Check-out waits because the physician note or billing code is incomplete.
- Patients are sent to a test area without a confirmed slot.
- Handover information is incomplete, requiring repeated clarification.

Process discovery and conformance analysis can identify actual variants, skipped activities, repeated steps, and unexpected ordering. A directly-follows graph can show where patients frequently remain between activities.

### 2.3 Service-time variability

A queue may be caused not by the average service time but by high variation. For example:

- New-patient registration takes much longer than follow-up registration.
- Complex consultations vary significantly by specialty or urgency.
- Certain diagnostic tests have long setup times.
- A small percentage of unusually long cases delays many subsequent patients.

Service-time distributions should therefore be analyzed using medians, percentiles, and coefficients of variation—not just averages. Queueing theory predicts that high variability increases waiting even when average utilization is unchanged.

### 2.4 Appointment scheduling policies

Potential scheduling causes include:

- Too many appointments booked at the beginning of a session
- Identical time slots for patients with different service requirements
- New and follow-up patients scheduled with the same duration
- Diagnostic tests scheduled after consultations without sufficient capacity
- No protected capacity for urgent patients
- Appointments booked according to physician availability but not downstream room or test capacity
- Insufficient allowance for late arrivals or documentation time

Comparing planned appointment time with actual arrival and start times can show whether delays are generated before the patient reaches the clinic. If scheduled demand exceeds hourly service capacity, queues are structurally inevitable.

### 2.5 Patient arrival patterns

The event log can reveal:

- Morning arrival surges
- Late arrivals creating uneven workload
- Simultaneous arrival of multiple specialties
- Day-of-week peaks
- No-show and cancellation patterns
- Urgent cases arriving unpredictably
- Patients requiring multiple services on the same visit

Arrival-rate plots can be compared with service capacity by 15- or 30-minute interval. Queue formation is likely when arrivals consistently exceed effective capacity, even if daily totals appear manageable.

### 2.6 Patient-type and urgency differences

Waiting and service times should be compared for:

- New versus follow-up patients
- Urgent versus normal patients
- Specialty
- Age or accessibility needs, where appropriate
- Single-service versus multi-service visits
- Appointment versus walk-in patients
- Patients requiring diagnostic testing

Averages can be misleading if the case mix changes. A registration queue may appear problematic because it contains a high proportion of new patients, while a physician queue may be driven by urgent cases or complex specialty variants.

### 2.7 Process-mining techniques

Useful techniques include:

- **Process discovery:** Learn the actual patient-flow variants from the event log.
- **Variant analysis:** Compare high-delay and low-delay visit pathways.
- **Performance overlays:** Display waiting time on process maps.
- **Bottleneck analysis:** Identify activities and transitions contributing most to total duration.
- **Resource analysis:** Link delay to staff, rooms, and equipment.
- **Conformance checking:** Detect deviations from intended pathways.
- **Trace clustering:** Group visits with similar patterns, such as routine, complex, urgent, or multi-test visits.
- **Predictive monitoring:** Predict visits likely to experience long waits based on arrival time, patient type, specialty, and current congestion.
- **Social-network analysis:** Examine handovers and dependencies among staff and departments.

A particularly useful comparison is between the worst-performing 10% of visits and the best-performing 50%. Differences in route, resource, arrival time, and service duration often expose practical causes.

## 3. Data-Driven Optimization Strategies

The final interventions should be selected after quantifying the relevant bottlenecks. The following strategies are examples that can be tested using the event log.

### Strategy 1: Capacity-based appointment scheduling

**Target queues:** Registration, nurse assessment, doctor consultation, and diagnostic testing.

**Likely root cause:** Demand is concentrated into time windows, and appointment templates do not reflect the actual service requirements or downstream capacity.

**Approach:**

- Estimate arrival rates and service-time distributions by 15- or 30-minute interval.
- Separate appointment types by expected workload:
  - New patient
  - Follow-up
  - Urgent
  - Consultation plus testing
  - Multiple-diagnostic visit
- Set appointment slots using effective capacity rather than theoretical capacity.
- Stagger arrivals instead of booking large groups at the start of a session.
- Reserve a small percentage of capacity for urgent cases and expected variability.
- Coordinate physician, nurse, room, and diagnostic schedules rather than scheduling each independently.

**Data support:**  
If the event log shows that arrivals exceed service capacity between 8:30 and 10:00, and that this period has the highest queue growth, smoothing arrivals should directly reduce waits.

**Expected impact:**  
A pilot might target a 15–30% reduction in morning registration and consultation waits, although the actual effect should be estimated through simulation or a controlled rollout.

**Implementation:**  
Use discrete-event simulation or queueing models to compare current templates with alternatives before changing the schedule.

---

### Strategy 2: Dynamic resource allocation and cross-training

**Target queues:** Registration, nurse assessment, ECG, laboratory, or check-out, depending on the measured bottleneck.

**Likely root cause:** Resource capacity is mismatched with demand by time of day, while other staff or rooms remain underutilized.

**Approach:**

- Use hourly workload and queue data to create flexible staffing windows.
- Cross-train selected registration staff to support check-out during peaks.
- Assign nurses or technicians to the highest-congestion location temporarily.
- Use a shared pool for ECG or laboratory services where skills permit.
- Open an additional room or service station during measured peak periods rather than for the entire day.
- Balance workload across clinicians where clinically appropriate.

**Data support:**  
Resource-level analysis may show that one ECG room is at 95% utilization from 10:00 to 12:00 while another is idle, or that check-out demand peaks after consultations finish.

**Expected impact:**  
If congestion is driven by a two-hour capacity mismatch, adding targeted peak-period capacity may reduce the affected queue by 20–40%, without adding a full-time resource.

**Potential caution:**  
Staff should not be moved away from another activity whose queue is equally critical. Cross-training must preserve competence and patient safety.

---

### Strategy 3: Parallelize compatible activities

**Target queues:** Delays between consultation, diagnostic testing, specialist review, and check-out.

**Likely root cause:** Activities are performed sequentially even when some can safely occur in parallel.

**Approach:**

- Identify dependencies using the process model.
- Schedule blood tests or ECG testing before the physician consultation when clinically appropriate.
- Allow registration and preliminary data collection to occur in parallel with waiting for a nurse.
- Pre-authorize or pre-complete administrative steps for known procedures.
- Trigger diagnostic appointments directly from the consultation system rather than requiring manual handoffs.
- Start clinician documentation or coding workflows before the patient reaches check-out, where appropriate.

For a patient needing consultation and ECG, the pathway may change from:

\[
\text{Consultation} \rightarrow \text{ECG} \rightarrow \text{Review}
\]

to:

\[
\text{Consultation and ECG in parallel} \rightarrow \text{Review}
\]

**Data support:**  
Variant analysis can identify activities that are consistently sequential but have no clinical dependency, and calculate the time that could potentially be overlapped.

**Expected impact:**  
If parallelization removes 10–15 minutes of avoidable sequential waiting for a significant patient group, total visit duration may fall by 10–20% for those visits.

**Safety constraint:**  
Only clinically independent activities should be parallelized. The intervention must be approved by clinical leadership.

---

### Strategy 4: Introduce real-time queue coordination

**Target queues:** High-variability queues and handoff delays between departments.

**Likely root cause:** Staff lack timely visibility into patient location, test completion, room availability, or downstream congestion.

**Approach:**

- Create a dashboard showing:
  - Patients waiting by stage
  - Current queue age
  - Available staff and rooms
  - Completed tests awaiting review
  - Predicted late cases
- Add automatic alerts when a patient exceeds a stage-specific threshold.
- Use a coordinator during peak periods to redirect patients to available resources.
- Notify the next department when the preceding activity is nearly complete.
- Provide patients with estimated wait updates and route information.

**Data support:**  
Event logs show the timing of handoffs and the point at which a patient becomes available. Excessive gaps after completion of a predecessor often indicate coordination problems.

**Expected impact:**  
A coordination intervention might reduce unproductive handoff delays by 10–25%, particularly where capacity exists but is not synchronized.

---

### Strategy 5: Pre-visit preparation and digital registration

**Target queues:** Registration and check-out.

**Likely root cause:** New-patient administration, insurance verification, consent, and documentation are performed on site and have high service-time variability.

**Approach:**

- Send forms and insurance verification before the visit.
- Separate quick follow-up registration from complex new-patient registration.
- Use self-service or assisted digital check-in where appropriate.
- Prepopulate demographic and clinical information.
- Start check-out preparation before the final consultation ends.
- Use a dedicated exception queue for cases requiring manual review.

**Data support:**  
Compare registration service times for new and follow-up patients. If new-patient service times have a high 90th percentile, pre-visit preparation is likely to have substantial value.

**Expected impact:**  
A 20–40% reduction in registration service time for prepared patients may reduce both registration waits and the downstream arrival surge.

**Consideration:**  
Digital access must not disadvantage patients with limited technology access, language barriers, disabilities, or low digital literacy.

## 4. Trade-offs and Constraints

### 4.1 Cost versus waiting-time reduction

Adding staff or rooms can reduce queues but increase labor and facility costs. The preferred approach is usually:

1. Remove avoidable process delay.
2. Smooth demand through scheduling.
3. Reallocate existing capacity.
4. Add targeted peak-period capacity only where justified.

Cost-effectiveness should be evaluated using:

- Cost per patient-minute saved
- Additional visits enabled per day
- Overtime avoided
- Patient satisfaction improvement
- Revenue or capacity gained
- Effects on staff retention and absenteeism

### 4.2 Shifting the bottleneck

Parallelizing consultation and diagnostic testing may reduce physician waiting but create a laboratory or ECG bottleneck. Every proposed change should therefore be assessed across the entire pathway, not only at the targeted activity.

A process simulation should test whether:

- Queue reductions in one stage increase queues elsewhere
- Peak demand becomes more concentrated
- Check-out becomes overloaded after faster clinical flow
- Capacity buffers remain adequate

### 4.3 Staff workload and burnout

Higher throughput can increase workload intensity. Resource utilization should not be optimized to the point that staff have no recovery, documentation, or contingency time.

Measures should include:

- Overtime
- Break compliance
- Workload distribution
- Staff-reported burden
- Error or rework rates
- Absence and turnover indicators

### 4.4 Care quality and safety

Reducing time must not mean shortening clinically necessary assessments. Quality safeguards include:

- Maintaining minimum consultation and assessment standards
- Monitoring incomplete documentation
- Tracking repeat tests, adverse events, and complaints
- Clinical approval of parallel activities
- Preserving priority access for urgent patients
- Validating that faster processing does not increase diagnostic errors

### 4.5 Equity and prioritization

Urgent patients may appropriately experience different flows, but prioritization rules should be transparent and clinically justified. Performance should be reported separately by patient type and urgency to ensure that overall improvement does not conceal deterioration for a vulnerable group.

### 4.6 Data limitations

The event log may not record:

- Actual patient arrival
- Movement between departments
- Informal waiting
- Phone calls or manual coordination
- Reasons for delay
- Temporary room closures
- Staff breaks
- Clinical complexity

Where possible, enrich the log with appointment, arrival, cancellation, room-status, staffing, and test-order data. Otherwise, conclusions should distinguish measured waiting from unobserved or inferred waiting.

## 5. Measuring Success

### 5.1 Core patient-flow KPIs

Track overall and segmented values for:

- Average total visit duration
- Median total visit duration
- 90th-percentile visit duration
- Average total waiting time
- Median and 90th-percentile waiting time
- Waiting time by process stage
- Percentage of visits exceeding a defined total-duration target
- Percentage of visits with excessive waits at any stage
- Time from arrival to first clinical contact
- Time from consultation to diagnostic completion
- Time from final clinical activity to check-out

### 5.2 Operational KPIs

Monitor:

- Queue length by stage and time interval
- Throughput per hour
- Resource utilization
- Resource idle time
- Overtime
- Room and equipment utilization
- Appointment start-time adherence
- No-show and cancellation rates
- Rework and repeated activities
- Percentage of visits following the intended pathway
- Percentage of test results available when needed for review

### 5.3 Quality and experience KPIs

Waiting-time improvement should be balanced with:

- Patient satisfaction
- Complaints related to delays
- Left-without-being-seen or early-departure rate
- Clinical incidents
- Documentation completeness
- Repeat tests caused by process failure
- Staff satisfaction and workload
- Equity of performance across patient segments

### 5.4 Ongoing process monitoring

The same event-log structure can support continuous monitoring through:

- Daily or weekly process dashboards
- Control charts for waiting times
- Alerts when a queue exceeds a threshold
- Heat maps by day and hour
- Trend analysis by specialty and resource
- Before-and-after comparisons
- Statistical process control
- Monthly variant and conformance reviews
- Predictive alerts for visits likely to become delayed

A practical monitoring cycle is:

1. Establish a four- to six-week baseline.
2. Implement one intervention in a limited pilot area.
3. Compare with the baseline and, where possible, a control clinic or session.
4. Adjust for case mix, urgency, day of week, and arrival pattern.
5. Review both intended and unintended effects.
6. Scale only after sustained improvement is demonstrated.

For example, success might be defined as:

- 20% reduction in median total waiting time
- 15% reduction in 90th-percentile visit duration
- No increase in clinical incidents or repeat tests
- No increase in staff overtime
- Improved patient satisfaction
- No widening of delays for urgent or vulnerable patients

The central principle is to treat the clinic as an interconnected flow system. Queue mining should identify not only the longest waits, but also the capacity, variability, handoffs, and scheduling decisions that generate them. Combining event-log analysis with targeted operational experiments and continuous monitoring provides a sustainable way to improve throughput and patient experience without relying solely on costly increases in staffing or space.
