# Queue Mining Approach for the Multi-Specialty Outpatient Clinic

## 1. Queue Identification and Characterization

### 1.1 Defining and Computing Waiting Time

Because the log contains paired `START`/`COMPLETE` events per activity instance, I can reconstruct each visit as an ordered trace and compute, for every pair of consecutive activities *A  A* within a case:

- **Service time:** `service(A) = complete(A)  start(A)`
- **Waiting time (queue time):** `wait(A  A) = start(A)  complete(A)`

**Definition of "waiting time" in this context:** the elapsed time between the moment a patient is *ready* for the next step (previous activity completed) and the moment the next resource actually begins working on them. It is *not* the same as value-adding time. Note that some inter-activity gaps are *legitimate transfer time* (e.g., specimen transport, room cleaning, lab processing) rather than queueing — I would estimate a baseline transfer time per transition (e.g., 5th percentile of observed gaps) and flag only the excess as true queue delay.

**Derived case-level measures:**
- Total visit duration (first `START`  last `COMPLETE`)
- Total waiting time (sum of inter-activity waits)
- **Value-added ratio** = total service time ÷ total duration (outpatient clinics typically run 20–40%; this is a powerful executive-level KPI)

**Data-quality prerequisites:** handle negative waits (overlapping/parallel events or clock skew across systems), missing `COMPLETE` events, incomplete traces (patients who leave without being seen — important to flag, as they're a satisfaction signal), and define "arrival time" (first event vs. scheduled appointment time — both are useful).

### 1.2 Queue Characterization Metrics

For **each queue** (activity-to-activity transition, segmented by specialty, resource, patient type, urgency, hour-of-day):

| Metric | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Mean, median, std dev of wait | Central tendency and variability (median matters — waits are usually right-skewed) |
| **P90 / P95 wait** | The "bad day" experience driving complaints |
| Max wait | Worst-case outliers for investigation |
| **Queue frequency** (cases/day through this transition) | Exposure — a long wait affecting 5 patients/day  one affecting 200 |
| **% of cases exceeding threshold** (e.g., > 20 min) | Directly tied to satisfaction targets |
| **Total wait-hours per day** = frequency × mean wait | Congestion footprint; combines volume + severity |
| Implied queue length via **Little's Law (L = W)** | Average number of patients physically waiting |
| **Wait-while-idle time** | Portion of the wait during which the *downstream resource was actually free* — separates coordination loss from true capacity shortage (critical for Section 2) |
| Downstream resource utilization  (busy time ÷ available time) | Queue theory predicts waits explode non-linearly as   100% |

### 1.3 Identifying the Most Critical Queues

I would rank queues on a composite criticality score rather than any single measure:

1. **Congestion footprint** (wait-hours/day) — the primary ranking criterion, because it captures both magnitude and frequency.
2. **Severity of tail** (P90/P95) — long-tail waits drive complaints even when averages look acceptable.
3. **Patient impact weighting** — waits experienced by *urgent* patients score higher (clinical risk); waits early in the visit (registration) disproportionately shape perceived experience.
4. **Position in the flow** — a queue upstream of an expensive/scarce resource (doctor, imaging) is more damaging than one upstream of an abundant one, because it starves the bottleneck.

**Justification:** ranking by "longest average wait" alone wastes effort on low-volume edges; ranking by frequency alone chases trivial waits. Example: the *Registration  Nurse* queue may have a modest mean but 100% of patients pass through it, while *Doctor  ECG* may have the longest P90 but only 15% of cases. The footprint metric forces this trade-off into the open. I'd validate the ranking against patient complaint themes before finalizing priorities.

## 2. Root Cause Analysis

### 2.1 Hypothesized Root Causes to Test

- **Resource bottlenecks:** e.g., a single ECG room/technician, or physician utilization running at 95–100% — queue theory guarantees long, volatile waits at that saturation level even if average capacity exceeds average demand.
- **Handover/transfer losses:** the gap between *Registration COMPLETE* and *Nurse Assessment START* is often not a busy-nurse problem but a *notification* problem — nobody knows the patient is ready (measurable via wait-while-idle).
- **Service-time variability:** New-patient consultations likely take ~2× longer than follow-ups. Mixing them on the same resource pool creates blocking and unpredictable downstream arrivals.
- **Appointment scheduling design:** block scheduling (many patients booked at :00) creates arrival surges that no staffing level can absorb smoothly; uniform slot lengths mismatch actual service-time distributions.
- **Arrival pattern misalignment:** demand curves (arrivals/hour) vs. flat staffing/shift patterns; Monday-morning and post-lunch peaks; shift changes mid-peak.
- **Batching behavior:** lab results processed in batches, or a doctor completing consultations in bursts, creates artificial queueing invisible in average utilization figures.
- **Sequential dependencies and rework loops:** the *Consult  Test  re-Consult* loop forces the patient through the doctor queue *twice*; missing-information rework (sent back to registration) adds hidden queues.
- **Priority inversion:** urgent patients queued FIFO behind normal patients.

### 2.2 Process Mining Techniques to Confirm Causes

- **Performance-enhanced process map:** discover the model and overlay mean/median wait on edges and frequency on paths — instantly highlights the hot edges and dominant variants.
- **Variant analysis:** group cases by path (e.g., ConsultECGRe-consult vs. ConsultCheck-out). Compare total duration and wait composition per variant. Expect to find that the diagnostic-loop variant carries 2–3 queues and disproportionate duration.
- **Resource utilization timelines:** plot each resource's busy/idle state over the day. Reveals (a) saturated resources (bottleneck confirmed), (b) idle-during-wait resources (coordination problem, not capacity), (c) batching (activity clustered in bursts).
- **Handover-of-work matrix / social network:** quantify delays associated with specific resource-to-resource handoffs (e.g., ClerkNurse vs. NurseDoctor) to find structurally slow interfaces.
- **Queue discipline inference (queue mining proper):** test whether queues behave FIFO, priority-based, or ad hoc; detect batch processing; estimate *effective* service capacity per resource vs. nominal capacity.
- **Time-of-day / day-of-week heatmaps** of waiting time, cross-referenced with arrival curves and rosters: if P90 waits spike 09:00–11:00 while arrivals peak 08:30–10:30 and staffing is flat, the scheduling/staffing mismatch hypothesis is confirmed.
- **Cohort comparison:** urgent vs. normal, new vs. follow-up, by specialty — checking for priority inversion and for service-time heterogeneity driving variability.
- **Correlation/feature analysis:** regress wait time on concurrent in-clinic load, preceding activity duration, hour, and case attributes to quantify which factors explain the variance.

The key analytical discipline: **distinguish capacity problems (resource busy) from coordination problems (resource idle while patients wait) from demand-shaping problems (avoidable arrival surges)** — because each demands a completely different intervention.

## 3. Data-Driven Optimization Strategies

### Strategy 1 — Demand-Aligned Staffing (Staggered Rosters & Flex Capacity)

- **Targets:** the top congestion-footprint queues (hypothetically pre-Consultation and pre-Registration during the 09:00–11:30 peak).
- **Root cause addressed:** flat staffing vs. peaked demand; utilization near 100% during peaks.

- **Proposal:** stagger shift start times so capacity tracks the measured arrival curve; create a small "flex pool" (cross-trained staff) deployed to whichever queue breaches a threshold; protect peak hours from meetings/admin tasks.
- **Expected impact:** queueing behavior is highly non-linear — reducing peak utilization from ~97% to ~85% can plausibly cut peak-period average waits **30–50%** with *zero net additional hours*, only redistributed ones. Validate with simulation before rollout (see §4).

### Strategy 2 — Redesign the Appointment Template (Smooth Arrivals, Right-Size Slots)

### Strategy 3 — Parallelize and Re-Sequence the Flow (Protocol-Driven Pre-Ordering of Diagnostics)

## 4. Trade-offs and Constraints

- **Bottleneck shifting (the most common failure mode):** accelerating Registration pushes demand onto Nursing; pre-ordering tests increases ECG/lab volume, potentially creating a *new* diagnostic bottleneck. **Mitigation:** model the whole system, not the single queue — feed the discovered process model into a **discrete-event simulation** and stress-test each intervention ("what-if" analysis) before deployment; monitor downstream queues post-implementation.
- **Cost vs. service:** Strategy 1 redistributes rather than adds hours, but flex pools and kiosks have real costs. The balance: express each strategy as **cost per wait-hour eliminated** and fund the cheapest first (typically coordination fixes > scheduling > staffing additions).
- **Staff workload and resilience:** deliberately raising utilization has diminishing, then dangerous, returns — above ~85% utilization with high variability, small disruptions cascade (Kingman dynamics). Target utilization caps, and protect staff breaks in the new rosters, or burnout will recreate the problem through turnover.
- **Quality of care:** shorter waits must not mean shorter, rushed consults or over-testing. Protocol-based pre-ordering requires clinical governance. Guardrail metrics (§5) must be monitored *alongside* flow KPIs; a strategy that improves flow but degrades a guardrail is rejected.
- **Equity:** fast-track/priority lanes can starve normal patients if unmanaged — enforce per-class wait caps. Kiosks/SMS must retain an assisted channel for patients with limited digital access.
- **Balancing method:** a weighted scorecard (flow KPIs, cost, quality guardrails, staff experience) reviewed monthly; phased rollout by specialty with pilot–measure–adjust cycles rather than clinic-wide big-bang change.
