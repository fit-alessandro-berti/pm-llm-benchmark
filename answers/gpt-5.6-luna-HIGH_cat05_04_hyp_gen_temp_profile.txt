## 1. Anomalies in the temporal profile

| Pair | Average | Standard deviation | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| `R  A` | 1 hour | 10 minutes | Relatively stable and plausible. |
| `R  E` | 1 day | 8 hours | Moderate variability; may reflect workload or claim complexity. |
| `R  P` | 25 hours | 1 hour | Very low relative variation, approximately 4%. This may indicate a fixed batch schedule, timestamp rounding, or an artifact in event pairing. |
| `A  C` | 2 hours | 1 hour | Potentially short for a process that should include evaluation, approval, and notification. May represent an expedited path or skipped events. |
| `E  N` | 5 minutes | 1 minute | Very short and highly consistent. Could be automated, but may also indicate missing approval or evaluation events. |
| `E  C` | 1 hour | 50 minutes | Extremely high relative variation. The process may contain multiple closure paths. |
| `P  N` | 7 days | 2 days | Long average delay with substantial variability, suggesting a notification backlog, manual processing, or missing event records. |
| `N  C` | 30 minutes | 5 minutes | Relatively stable and plausible if closure is automated after notification. |

The most important apparent inconsistencies are:

1. **`R  P` is unusually rigid.**  
   A 25-hour average with only a one-hour standard deviation is suspiciously precise.

2. **`P  N` is unusually long.**  
   Seven days between approval and notification is inconsistent with a normally responsive claim process.

3. **`A  C` may indicate skipped activities.**  
   Closing two hours after assignment is difficult to reconcile with the intended sequence unless there is an expedited or rejected-claim path.

4. **`E  N` may be too fast.**  
   Five minutes could be valid for an automated notification, but it may also reflect missing `P` events.

5. **The pairwise averages may not be mutually consistent.**  
   If the intended order is `E  P  N`, then `E  N` should generally be at least as long as `E  P` plus `P  N` for the same execution. The reported averages may have been computed from different subsets of claims, or the event matching logic may be pairing unrelated occurrences.

6. **`E  C` has a very large coefficient of variation.**  
   Its standard deviation is larger than the average, suggesting multiple execution patterns, rework, missing events, or a strongly skewed distribution.

A standard deviation cannot be interpreted fully without the number of observations and the underlying distribution. A small standard deviation based on only a few claims may not be meaningful.

## 2. Possible explanations

### `R  P` is too regular

Possible causes include:

- Approvals are processed by a nightly or daily batch job.
- Approval timestamps are generated using a default SLA or scheduled time.
- Timestamps are rounded to the nearest hour or day.
- The same approval event is repeatedly matched to the wrong receive event.
- Approval is automatically generated for a homogeneous subset of claims.
- The model was built from only a small number of claims.
- Event clocks or application servers are not synchronized.

### `P  N` is long and variable

Possible causes include:

- Notifications are manually queued after approval.
- Notification jobs run only on certain days or during business hours.
- Failed emails or letters are retried later.
- Notification events are logged only when delivery is confirmed.
- Approval and notification are performed by different departments.
- There is a backlog in a customer-communications system.
- Different claim types use different notification channels.
- Some notification events are missing, causing the next recorded notification to appear very late.

### `A  C` is short

Possible causes include:

- Certain claims are rejected or withdrawn immediately after assignment.
- Low-value claims use an abbreviated process.
- A system rule closes claims automatically after assignment.
- Evaluation and approval occurred outside the claims system.
- Required events were not logged.
- A claim was reopened and the assignment and closure events belong to different process attempts.
- The temporal profile pairs the first assignment with a later closure without considering intermediate case lifecycles.

### `E  N` is very short

Possible causes include:

- Notification is automatically generated when evaluation is completed.
- Approval is implicit for certain claim types.
- The `P` event was skipped or recorded in another system.
- The event labeled `E` represents a final evaluation rather than the start of evaluation.
- Notifications are sent before approval due to a workflow defect.
- Event timestamps were generated asynchronously or out of order.

### General data-quality explanations

- Duplicate events for the same activity.
- Missing events.
- Incorrect claim identifiers.
- Different systems writing timestamps in different time zones.
- Delayed ingestion into `claim_events`.
- Multiple executions or reopened claims being treated as one trace.
- Matching every source activity to the first later target activity rather than matching canonical process instances.

## 3. SQL verification queries

The queries below use a three-standard-deviation threshold as an example. The threshold should be changed if a different ZETA factor is used.

### 3.1 Find outliers for all modeled activity pairs

This query matches each source event to the first later target event for the same claim.

```sql
WITH profile(from_activity, to_activity, avg_sec, sd_sec, zeta) AS (
    VALUES
        ('R', 'A', 3600::numeric,   600::numeric,    3::numeric),
        ('R', 'E', 86400::numeric,  28800::numeric,  3::numeric),
        ('R', 'P', 90000::numeric,  3600::numeric,   3::numeric),
        ('A', 'C', 7200::numeric,   3600::numeric,   3::numeric),
        ('E', 'N', 300::numeric,    60::numeric,     3::numeric),
        ('E', 'C', 3600::numeric,   3000::numeric,   3::numeric),
        ('P', 'N', 604800::numeric, 172800::numeric, 3::numeric),
        ('N', 'C', 1800::numeric,   300::numeric,    3::numeric)
),
pairs AS (
    SELECT
        p.from_activity,
        p.to_activity,
        p.avg_sec,
        p.sd_sec,
        p.zeta,
        s.claim_id,
        s.event_id AS from_event_id,
        s.timestamp AS from_ts,
        s.resource AS from_resource,
        t.event_id AS to_event_id,
        t.timestamp AS to_ts,
        t.resource AS to_resource
    FROM profile p
    JOIN claim_events s
      ON s.activity = p.from_activity
    CROSS JOIN LATERAL (
        SELECT
            e.event_id,
            e.timestamp,
            e.resource
        FROM claim_events e
        WHERE e.claim_id = s.claim_id
          AND e.activity = p.to_activity
          AND (e.timestamp, e.event_id) >
              (s.timestamp, s.event_id)
        ORDER BY e.timestamp, e.event_id
        LIMIT 1
    ) t
),
scored AS (
    SELECT
        *,
        EXTRACT(EPOCH FROM (to_ts - from_ts)) AS observed_sec
    FROM pairs
)
SELECT
    s.claim_id,
    c.customer_id,
    c.claim_type,
    s.from_activity,
    s.to_activity,
    s.from_ts,
    s.to_ts,
    s.from_resource,
    s.to_resource,
    ROUND(s.observed_sec, 2) AS observed_sec,
    s.avg_sec,
    s.sd_sec,
    ROUND(
        (s.observed_sec - s.avg_sec) / NULLIF(s.sd_sec, 0),
        2
    ) AS z_score
FROM scored s
JOIN claims c
  ON c.claim_id = s.claim_id
WHERE ABS(s.observed_sec - s.avg_sec) > s.zeta * s.sd_sec
ORDER BY
    s.from_activity,
    s.to_activity,
    ABS(s.observed_sec - s.avg_sec) DESC;
```

This produces claim-level anomalies and allows the results to be inspected by claim type, customer, and resource.

If the model was created using only the first occurrence of each activity per claim, the query should use `DISTINCT ON (claim_id, activity)` instead of matching every source event to a later target event.

### 3.2 Investigate unusually rigid `R  P` intervals

```sql
WITH receive_approve AS (
    SELECT
        r.claim_id,
        r.timestamp AS receive_ts,
        p.timestamp AS approve_ts,
        p.resource AS approval_resource,
        EXTRACT(EPOCH FROM (p.timestamp - r.timestamp)) AS delay_sec
    FROM claim_events r
    CROSS JOIN LATERAL (
        SELECT
            e.event_id,
            e.timestamp,
            e.resource
        FROM claim_events e
        WHERE e.claim_id = r.claim_id
          AND e.activity = 'P'
          AND (e.timestamp, e.event_id) >
              (r.timestamp, r.event_id)
        ORDER BY e.timestamp, e.event_id
        LIMIT 1
    ) p
    WHERE r.activity = 'R'
)
SELECT
    rp.claim_id,
    c.customer_id,
    c.claim_type,
    rp.receive_ts,
    rp.approve_ts,
    rp.approval_resource,
    rp.delay_sec,
    rp.delay_sec - 90000 AS deviation_from_model_sec
FROM receive_approve rp
JOIN claims c
  ON c.claim_id = rp.claim_id
WHERE rp.delay_sec < 90000 - 3 * 3600
   OR rp.delay_sec > 90000 + 3 * 3600
ORDER BY ABS(rp.delay_sec - 90000) DESC;
```

To check whether approvals are scheduled at a small number of fixed offsets:

```sql
SELECT
    ROUND(
        EXTRACT(EPOCH FROM (p.timestamp - r.timestamp)) / 3600.0,
        1
    ) AS delay_hours,
    COUNT(*) AS claim_count
FROM claim_events r
JOIN LATERAL (
    SELECT e.timestamp, e.event_id
    FROM claim_events e
    WHERE e.claim_id = r.claim_id
      AND e.activity = 'P'
      AND (e.timestamp, e.event_id) >
          (r.timestamp, r.event_id)
    ORDER BY e.timestamp, e.event_id
    LIMIT 1
) p ON TRUE
WHERE r.activity = 'R'
GROUP BY 1
ORDER BY 1;
```

A strong concentration around exactly 24 or 25 hours would support a batch-processing or timestamp-generation hypothesis.

### 3.3 Find excessively delayed `P  N` transitions

The model's three-standard-deviation upper boundary is:

```text
604800 + (3 × 172800) = 1,123,200 seconds
```

That is 13 days.

```sql
WITH approval_notification AS (
    SELECT
        p.claim_id,
        p.timestamp AS approve_ts,
        p.resource AS approval_resource,
        n.timestamp AS notify_ts,
        n.resource AS notification_resource,
        EXTRACT(EPOCH FROM (n.timestamp - p.timestamp)) AS delay_sec
    FROM claim_events p
    CROSS JOIN LATERAL (
        SELECT
            e.event_id,
            e.timestamp,
            e.resource
        FROM claim_events e
        WHERE e.claim_id = p.claim_id
          AND e.activity = 'N'
          AND (e.timestamp, e.event_id) >
              (p.timestamp, p.event_id)
        ORDER BY e.timestamp, e.event_id
        LIMIT 1
    ) n
    WHERE p.activity = 'P'
)
SELECT
    pn.claim_id,
    c.customer_id,
    c.claim_type,
    pn.approve_ts,
    pn.notify_ts,
    pn.approval_resource,
    pn.notification_resource,
    ROUND(pn.delay_sec / 86400.0, 2) AS delay_days
FROM approval_notification pn
JOIN claims c
  ON c.claim_id = pn.claim_id
WHERE pn.delay_sec > 604800 + 3 * 172800
ORDER BY pn.delay_sec DESC;
```

To identify likely queue or resource effects:

```sql
WITH approval_notification AS (
    SELECT
        p.claim_id,
        p.resource AS approval_resource,
        n.resource AS notification_resource,
        EXTRACT(EPOCH FROM (n.timestamp - p.timestamp)) AS delay_sec
    FROM claim_events p
    CROSS JOIN LATERAL (
        SELECT e.timestamp, e.event_id, e.resource
        FROM claim_events e
        WHERE e.claim_id = p.claim_id
          AND e.activity = 'N'
          AND (e.timestamp, e.event_id) >
              (p.timestamp, p.event_id)
        ORDER BY e.timestamp, e.event_id
        LIMIT 1
    ) n
    WHERE p.activity = 'P'
)
SELECT
    c.claim_type,
    pn.approval_resource,
    pn.notification_resource,
    COUNT(*) AS transition_count,
    ROUND(AVG(pn.delay_sec) / 86400.0, 2) AS average_delay_days,
    ROUND(
        STDDEV_SAMP(pn.delay_sec) / 86400.0,
        2
    ) AS delay_sd_days,
    COUNT(*) FILTER (
        WHERE pn.delay_sec > 604800 + 3 * 172800
    ) AS excessive_delay_count
FROM approval_notification pn
JOIN claims c
  ON c.claim_id = pn.claim_id
GROUP BY
    c.claim_type,
    pn.approval_resource,
    pn.notification_resource
HAVING COUNT(*) >= 5
ORDER BY excessive_delay_count DESC, average_delay_days DESC;
```

### 3.4 Find claims closed shortly after assignment without intermediate events

This query treats two hours as the “immediate closure” threshold.

```sql
WITH assignment_close AS (
    SELECT
        a.claim_id,
        a.event_id AS assignment_event_id,
        a.timestamp AS assignment_ts,
        a.resource AS assignment_resource,
        c.event_id AS close_event_id,
        c.timestamp AS close_ts,
        EXTRACT(EPOCH FROM (c.timestamp - a.timestamp)) AS delay_sec
    FROM claim_events a
    CROSS JOIN LATERAL (
        SELECT
            e.event_id,
            e.timestamp
        FROM claim_events e
        WHERE e.claim_id = a.claim_id
          AND e.activity = 'C'
          AND (e.timestamp, e.event_id) >
              (a.timestamp, a.event_id)
        ORDER BY e.timestamp, e.event_id
        LIMIT 1
    ) c
    WHERE a.activity = 'A'
)
SELECT
    ac.claim_id,
    cl.customer_id,
    cl.claim_type,
    ac.assignment_ts,
    ac.close_ts,
    ac.assignment_resource,
    ROUND(ac.delay_sec / 60.0, 2) AS delay_minutes
FROM assignment_close ac
JOIN claims cl
  ON cl.claim_id = ac.claim_id
WHERE ac.delay_sec <= 2 * 3600
  AND NOT EXISTS (
      SELECT 1
      FROM claim_events x
      WHERE x.claim_id = ac.claim_id
        AND x.activity IN ('E', 'P', 'N')
        AND (x.timestamp, x.event_id) >
            (ac.assignment_ts, ac.assignment_event_id)
        AND (x.timestamp, x.event_id) <
            (ac.close_ts, ac.close_event_id)
  )
ORDER BY ac.delay_sec;
```

This distinguishes genuine expedited closures from closures where the intermediate events exist but were simply not matched by the temporal-profile logic.

### 3.5 Find very rapid `E  N` transitions and missing approvals

The model's three-standard-deviation lower boundary is:

```text
300 - (3 × 60) = 120 seconds
```

```sql
WITH evaluation_notification AS (
    SELECT
        e.claim_id,
        e.event_id AS evaluation_event_id,
        e.timestamp AS evaluation_ts,
        e.resource AS evaluation_resource,
        n.event_id AS notification_event_id,
        n.timestamp AS notification_ts,
        n.resource AS notification_resource,
        EXTRACT(EPOCH FROM (n.timestamp - e.timestamp)) AS delay_sec
    FROM claim_events e
    CROSS JOIN LATERAL (
        SELECT
            x.event_id,
            x.timestamp,
            x.resource
        FROM claim_events x
        WHERE x.claim_id = e.claim_id
          AND x.activity = 'N'
          AND (x.timestamp, x.event_id) >
              (e.timestamp, e.event_id)
        ORDER BY x.timestamp, x.event_id
        LIMIT 1
    ) n
    WHERE e.activity = 'E'
)
SELECT
    en.claim_id,
    c.customer_id,
    c.claim_type,
    en.evaluation_ts,
    en.notification_ts,
    en.evaluation_resource,
    en.notification_resource,
    ROUND(en.delay_sec, 2) AS delay_seconds,
    EXISTS (
        SELECT 1
        FROM claim_events p
        WHERE p.claim_id = en.claim_id
          AND p.activity = 'P'
          AND (p.timestamp, p.event_id) >
              (en.evaluation_ts, en.evaluation_event_id)
          AND (p.timestamp, p.event_id) <
              (en.notification_ts, en.notification_event_id)
    ) AS approval_recorded_between
FROM evaluation_notification en
JOIN claims c
  ON c.claim_id = en.claim_id
WHERE en.delay_sec <= 300
ORDER BY en.delay_sec;
```

Claims with a short `E  N` delay and `approval_recorded_between = false` are candidates for skipped approval, missing events, or an alternative workflow.

### 3.6 Check the intended sequence for missing or out-of-order events

```sql
WITH first_events AS (
    SELECT
        claim_id,
        MIN(timestamp) FILTER (WHERE activity = 'R') AS r_ts,
        MIN(timestamp) FILTER (WHERE activity = 'A') AS a_ts,
        MIN(timestamp) FILTER (WHERE activity = 'E') AS e_ts,
        MIN(timestamp) FILTER (WHERE activity = 'P') AS p_ts,
        MIN(timestamp) FILTER (WHERE activity = 'N') AS n_ts,
        MIN(timestamp) FILTER (WHERE activity = 'C') AS c_ts
    FROM claim_events
    GROUP BY claim_id
)
SELECT
    claim_id,
    r_ts,
    a_ts,
    e_ts,
    p_ts,
    n_ts,
    c_ts,
    CASE
        WHEN r_ts IS NULL
          OR a_ts IS NULL
          OR e_ts IS NULL
          OR p_ts IS NULL
          OR n_ts IS NULL
          OR c_ts IS NULL
            THEN 'missing_activity'
        WHEN NOT (
            r_ts < a_ts
            AND a_ts < e_ts
            AND e_ts < p_ts
            AND p_ts < n_ts
            AND n_ts < c_ts
        )
            THEN 'out_of_order'
        ELSE 'ordered'
    END AS sequence_status
FROM first_events
WHERE r_ts IS NULL
   OR a_ts IS NULL
   OR e_ts IS NULL
   OR p_ts IS NULL
   OR n_ts IS NULL
   OR c_ts IS NULL
   OR NOT (
        r_ts < a_ts
        AND a_ts < e_ts
        AND e_ts < p_ts
        AND p_ts < n_ts
        AND n_ts < c_ts
   );
```

This is useful for determining whether the unusual temporal averages are caused by genuine process behavior or incomplete traces.

### 3.7 Correlate `R  P` anomalies with claim type and adjuster

The schema does not contain a direct `adjuster_id` on `claim_events`. The query below assumes that the `resource` on the assignment event contains the numeric adjuster ID. If it contains a name or another identifier, the join condition must be changed.

```sql
WITH receive_approve AS (
    SELECT
        c.claim_id,
        c.claim_type,
        r.timestamp AS receive_ts,
        p.timestamp AS approve_ts,
        a.resource AS assignment_resource,
        ad.name AS adjuster_name,
        ad.region,
        EXTRACT(EPOCH FROM (p.timestamp - r.timestamp)) AS delay_sec
    FROM claims c
    JOIN LATERAL (
        SELECT e.event_id, e.timestamp
        FROM claim_events e
        WHERE e.claim_id = c.claim_id
          AND e.activity = 'R'
        ORDER BY e.timestamp, e.event_id
        LIMIT 1
    ) r ON TRUE
    JOIN LATERAL (
        SELECT e.event_id, e.timestamp
        FROM claim_events e
        WHERE e.claim_id = c.claim_id
          AND e.activity = 'P'
          AND (e.timestamp, e.event_id) >
              (r.timestamp, r.event_id)
        ORDER BY e.timestamp, e.event_id
        LIMIT 1
    ) p ON TRUE
    LEFT JOIN LATERAL (
        SELECT e.resource
        FROM claim_events e
        WHERE e.claim_id = c.claim_id
          AND e.activity = 'A'
          AND (e.timestamp, e.event_id) >
              (r.timestamp, r.event_id)
          AND (e.timestamp, e.event_id) <
              (p.timestamp, p.event_id)
        ORDER BY e.timestamp, e.event_id
        LIMIT 1
    ) a ON TRUE
    LEFT JOIN adjusters ad
      ON ad.adjuster_id =
         CASE
             WHEN a.resource ~ '^[0-9]+$'
             THEN a.resource::integer
         END
)
SELECT
    claim_type,
    assignment_resource,
    adjuster_name,
    region,
    COUNT(*) AS claim_count,
    ROUND(AVG(delay_sec) / 3600.0, 2) AS average_delay_hours,
    ROUND(STDDEV_SAMP(delay_sec) / 3600.0, 2) AS observed_sd_hours,
    COUNT(*) FILTER (
        WHERE delay_sec < 90000 - 3 * 3600
           OR delay_sec > 90000 + 3 * 3600
    ) AS model_outlier_count
FROM receive_approve
GROUP BY
    claim_type,
    assignment_resource,
    adjuster_name,
    region
HAVING COUNT(*) >= 5
ORDER BY model_outlier_count DESC, average_delay_hours DESC;
```

This can reveal whether the anomaly is concentrated among:

- A particular adjuster.
- A particular region.
- A particular claim type.
- A specific assignment or approval resource.

Because `claims` has no region or adjuster column, regional analysis is only possible if `claim_events.resource` can reliably be mapped to `adjusters`.
