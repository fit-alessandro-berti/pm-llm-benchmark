## 2. Attribute-based root-cause analysis

### A. Complexity is the clearest driver

| Complexity | Cases | Typical lead time | Observed behavior |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Low | 2001, 2004 | ~1.5 hours | No document requests; rapid approval and payment |
| Medium | 2002 | ~26 hours | One document request; overnight wait before approval |
| High | 2003, 2005 | ~48–77 hours | Multiple document requests; long waits before approval/payment |

There is a strong relationship between **higher complexity** and longer duration:

- Low-complexity cases have a direct flow:  
  **Submit  Evaluate  Approve  Pay  Close**
- Medium- and high-complexity cases enter a rework/documentation loop:  
  **Evaluate  Request Additional Documents  further waiting / repeated requests  Approve**

#### Evidence
- **2002, Medium:** 1 request for additional documents.
- **2003, High:** 2 document requests.
- **2005, High:** 3 document requests.

The repeated “Request Additional Documents” activity is a major signal of missing information, incomplete submissions, or difficult validation requirements. It adds both operational effort and elapsed waiting time, likely including customer response time.

### B. Repeated document requests are the direct process-level bottleneck

| Case | Complexity | Number of document requests | Lead time |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| 2001 | Low | 0 | 1 h 30 m |
| 2004 | Low | 0 | 1 h 25 m |
| 2002 | Medium | 1 | 25 h 55 m |
| 2003 | High | 2 | 48 h 20 m |
| 2005 | High | 3 | 77 h 5 m |

This trend is especially clear: more document-request cycles correspond to longer overall case duration.

#### Important waiting intervals
- **Case 2002:** request at Apr 1 14:00; approval at Apr 2 10:00  
  - About **20 hours** waiting after the request.
- **Case 2003:** second request at Apr 1 17:00; approval at Apr 2 16:00  
  - About **23 hours** waiting after the second request.
- **Case 2005:** third request at Apr 3 15:00; approval at Apr 4 10:00  
  - About **19 hours** waiting after the third request.

These long waits are likely not entirely staff processing time; they may represent time waiting for claimants to submit documents, documents to be verified, or cases to return to an approval queue.

### C. Region B appears slower, but complexity is a confounding factor

| Region | Cases | Lead times |
|---|---|---|
| A | 2001, 2003 | 1.5 h, 48.3 h |
| B | 2002, 2004, 2005 | 25.9 h, 1.4 h, 77.1 h |

At first glance, Region B has more delayed cases: **2002 and 2005**. However, the data does not support concluding that Region B itself is the primary cause:

- Region B also contains **2004**, a low-complexity case completed in only **1 hour 25 minutes**.
- Region A contains **2003**, a high-complexity case that took more than **48 hours**.

Therefore, the strongest explanation is **complexity and documentation rework**, not region alone.

That said, Region B may still deserve investigation because:
- It includes the longest case, **2005**.
- Both of its non-low-complexity cases are delayed.
- It could have local queueing, staffing capacity, customer-document turnaround, or workflow-policy issues.

More cases would be needed to distinguish a genuine regional effect from the fact that Region B happens to have more complex cases in this small sample.

This does **not** prove that Adjuster_Lisa is inefficient:

However, her involvement in the two delayed Region B cases indicates areas worth checking:

## 3. Likely explanations and mitigation actions

### Root cause 1: High-complexity claims create repeated documentation loops

**Explanation:**  
High-complexity claims likely require more evidence, validation, policy interpretation, or external information. In this log, high-complexity cases repeatedly request additional documents, causing long waiting periods and rework.

**Mitigations:**
- Introduce a **complex-claim intake checklist** that identifies required documents at submission.
- Use guided digital forms that make mandatory evidence clear before submission.
- Provide customer-specific document packages rather than sending one request at a time.
- Assign high-complexity claims to specialized teams early.
- Add automated reminders and status tracking for outstanding claimant documents.
- Set escalation rules when documents are not received within a target time.

### Root cause 2: Sequential document requests instead of consolidated requests

**Explanation:**  
Cases 2003 and 2005 have multiple requests for additional documents. This implies that all missing requirements may not have been identified during the first review. Each additional request introduces another customer-response and verification cycle.

**Mitigations:**
- Require adjusters to perform a complete initial evidence review before contacting the claimant.
- Use rules or checklists based on claim type and complexity to identify all likely required documents.
- Create quality checks for high-complexity cases before the first request is sent.
- Track the “first-pass completeness” rate: percentage of cases resolved with only one document request.

### Root cause 3: Possible Region B queueing or operational capacity issues

**Explanation:**  
Region B contains two delayed cases, including the longest case, but it also processes a low-complexity claim quickly. Therefore Region B is a possible contributing factor, not a proven root cause.

**Mitigations:**
- Compare Region B’s workload, staffing, pending-document queue, and approval queue with Region A.
- Measure cycle time by region while controlling for complexity.
- Check whether Region B has slower customer response times, different document standards, or more manual handoffs.
- Balance workloads across regions or route complex cases to the team with the lowest queue.

### Root cause 4: Delays after approval in complex cases

**Explanation:**  
For Case 2005, payment takes around four hours after approval, noticeably longer than the simple cases. This may reflect additional payment controls, manual checks, or finance queue delays for high-value/complex claims.

**Mitigations:**
- Separate approval-to-payment time as its own performance metric.
- Automate payment initiation once required approval conditions are met.
- Use risk-based controls so only truly exceptional claims need additional manual payment review.
- Establish service-level targets for payment after approval.

---

## Overall conclusion

The primary root cause of long case duration is **claim complexity**, especially when it leads to **multiple additional-document requests** and long waiting periods between those requests and approval.

- **Case 2005** is the most serious performance issue: high complexity, three document requests, and a long overall duration.
- **Case 2003** has the same pattern at a smaller scale: high complexity and two document requests.
- **Case 2002** shows that even medium complexity can cause substantial delay once a document request is introduced.

Resource and region should be investigated, particularly **Adjuster_Lisa’s complex-case workload** and **Region B’s queues**, but the supplied data most strongly supports a complexity/documentation-rework explanation rather than attributing delays solely to a specific person or region.
