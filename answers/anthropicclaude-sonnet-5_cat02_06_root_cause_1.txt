## 2. Root Cause Analysis

### a) Escalation to Level2 Agent
- Cases 102 and 105 both include an **Escalate to Level2 Agent** step, and both are the slowest cases.
- After escalation, there is a significant wait before the next "Investigate Issue" step:
  - Case 102: Escalate (11:30)  Investigate (14:00) = **2.5 hours**
  - Case 105: Escalate (10:00, Day 1)  Investigate (14:00, Day 2) = **28 hours**
- This suggests the **hand-off to the specialized team is not immediate** — Level2 agents may have queued workloads, lack of real-time notification, or are only available during certain hours.

### b) Overnight/After-Hours Gaps
- Even in **non-escalated** Case 104, there's a long gap between "Investigate Issue" (13:00) and "Resolve Ticket" (08:00 next day) — a **19-hour gap**, almost entirely overnight.
- Case 102 exhibits the same pattern: Investigate (14:00)  Resolve (09:00 next day) = **19 hours**.
- Case 105 has **two** such overnight gaps, compounding delay dramatically.
- This points to a **systemic pattern**: whenever an activity finishes late in the day, the next step doesn't happen until the next business day — indicating **no after-hours coverage or shift-based bottlenecks**, rather than an issue specific to escalation.

### c) Delay Before Investigation Start
- In Case 104, there's a **3.5-hour gap** between "Assign to Level-1 Agent" (09:30) and "Investigate Issue" (13:00), even without escalation — suggesting **agent workload backlog** or slow ticket pickup.
- Similar (though shorter) delays appear in Case 102 pre-escalation.

## 3. Insights & Recommendations

**Key finding:** The primary driver of long cycle times is not the process structure itself (all cases follow logical steps), but **waiting time between activities**, driven by two intertwined factors:
1. **Escalation hand-off inefficiency** — tickets sent to Level-2 agents sit idle before being picked up.
2. **Lack of after-hours/24-7 processing** — any ticket whose investigation or resolution step falls near end-of-day gets pushed to the next business day, adding 15–20+ hours regardless of actual complexity.

**Recommendations:**

1. **Improve escalation SLAs:** Establish maximum response-time targets for Level-2 agents (e.g., pickup within 30 minutes) and monitor actual escalation queue times.
2. **Address off-hours bottlenecks:** Consider a rotating on-call/after-hours team or automated triage bot to prevent tickets from sitting idle overnight when investigation or resolution is pending.
3. **Ticket aging alerts:** Implement automated alerts/escalation triggers when a ticket has been in "Assigned" or "Escalated" status beyond a defined threshold (e.g., 1–2 hours), prompting proactive follow-up.
4. **Root-cause tracking for escalations:** Since escalated tickets (102, 105) are consistently the slowest, consider examining whether escalation criteria are too broad (causing unnecessary escalations) or whether Level-2 capacity needs to be increased.
5. **Workload balancing for Level-1 agents:** The gap between assignment and investigation start (as in Case 104) suggests uneven agent workload — consider automated ticket routing or workload dashboards to reduce idle assignment time.

**Overall,** the data suggests that **cycle-time variability is driven less by task complexity and more by process idle time — particularly overnight gaps and escalation hand-off delays.** Tackling these waiting periods (via staffing, SLAs, and automated monitoring) would likely yield the greatest reduction in overall resolution time.
