The XOR creates two possible traces after preliminary scoring:

- `...  C  D  E  F`
- `...  C  E  F`

The model itself does not encode a score change; `D` is only a transition labeled `CheckLocalAffiliation`. However, under the stated business interpretation, completing `D` produces a small score uplift. In that case, the XOR is not merely a process alternative—it creates unequal treatment paths.

### How the bias arises

If `D` is selected primarily for local residents or members of favored community groups, those applicants receive an advantage that otherwise similar applicants do not. For example:

- A local applicant may receive an uplift and be approved, while an otherwise identical non-local applicant follows the `skip` branch and is rejected.
- Borderline applications may be especially affected because a small uplift can change the final decision or loan terms.
- The benefit may be larger than intended if it affects both the preliminary score and the underwriter’s subsequent manual review.
- “Local affiliation” or membership in a “known community group” may act as a proxy for race, ethnicity, nationality, religion, socioeconomic status, neighborhood, or social connectedness.

Thus, even if locality or group membership is not itself a legally protected characteristic, the rule can create indirect disparate treatment or disparate impact.

### Why non-protected status does not eliminate the concern

Fairness is not limited to whether the favored category appears on a statutory list. An unexplained advantage for one group can still be:

- **Arbitrary**, if affiliation is not demonstrably related to repayment ability.
- **Exclusionary**, because applicants outside the favored network cannot obtain the same benefit.
- **Opaque**, since applicants may not know why two similar applications followed different paths.
- **Self-reinforcing**, since favored applicants receive more approvals and better terms, creating data that may later appear to justify the policy.

Depending on the jurisdiction and lending context, the practice could also raise fair-lending concerns if the affiliation criterion is a proxy for a protected characteristic. Conversely, a locality-based program might be legally permissible in a narrowly defined community-development context, but that would require a legitimate objective, careful design, and legal review.

### Fairness implications for the final decision

The effect should be assessed not only through approval rates, but also through:

- loan pricing and terms;
- rejection and error rates;
- the frequency with which each group enters the `D` branch;
- outcomes for borderline cases;
- outcomes across intersectional groups;
- whether comparable applicants receive comparable treatment.

A reasonable safeguard would be to make the local-affiliation check available under clearly defined, neutral criteria and avoid an automatic uplift merely for belonging to a particular community. If the information has genuine predictive value, its contribution should be empirically validated, calibrated, explainable, and applied consistently. Counterfactual testing—holding the application constant while changing only local affiliation—can reveal whether the XOR branch changes the outcome unfairly.

In short, the POWL structure exposes a potential fairness risk: an optional branch before manual review can encode a systematic advantage. The process should therefore distinguish between collecting relevant information and granting preferential treatment based on affiliation.
