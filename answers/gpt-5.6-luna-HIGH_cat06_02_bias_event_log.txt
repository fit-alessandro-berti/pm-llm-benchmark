## Where bias appears

### 1. Explicit preference for one community affiliation

The scoring engine awards **+10 points to members of the Highland Civic Darts Club**:

- C001: 710  720
- C004: 690  700

Applicants with no listed affiliation receive no adjustment:

- C002: 720  720
- C003: 715  715
- C005: 740  740

This is differential treatment based on **community-group membership**, which is not obviously related to creditworthiness. It favors people who belong to a particular organization and disadvantages otherwise comparable applicants who do not.

The adjustment is not merely recorded as a recommendation; it is carried through manual review and into the final decision process. Therefore, the preference is embedded in the operational workflow rather than being an isolated reviewer judgment.

### 2. Geographic or social-proxy effects

There is no explicit points adjustment for `LocalResident = TRUE`; for example, C002 is local but receives no bonus. However, both applicants receiving the community bonus are local residents and affiliated with the same group. Consequently, the community rule may operate as an indirect proxy for:

- geographic location;
- access to a particular local organization;
- social networks or community familiarity;
- length of residence or local social capital.

People who are new to the area, live outside the relevant geography, cannot participate in the club, or belong to other groups may be disadvantaged even if their financial qualifications are similar.

### 3. Outcomes are not explained by the raw score alone

Several comparisons demonstrate the potential effect:

- **C001 vs. C003:** C001 has a raw score of 710 and is approved after receiving the +10 community adjustment. C003 has a higher raw score of 715, receives no adjustment, and is rejected.
- **C004 vs. C003:** C004 has a raw score of only 690 and is approved after the community adjustment, while C003 has 715 and is rejected.
- **C001 vs. C002:** Both finish at 720 and are approved, but C001 reaches 720 through a community bonus whereas C002 reaches it through its underlying score.

These outcomes suggest that affiliation can materially change eligibility or approval outcomes, particularly for applicants near a decision boundary. C005 shows that a high enough score can overcome the lack of an affiliation, so the disadvantage is not absolute; it is most consequential for borderline applicants.

The approval of C004 at an adjusted score of 700 also indicates that the process is not governed by a simple transparent cutoff. Either additional criteria or manual discretion is involved. That inconsistency increases the risk that community information influences outcomes in ways that are difficult to explain or audit.

## Role of manual review

The log does not prove that the individual underwriters changed decisions because of community status. However, reviewers were exposed to:

- the community affiliation;
- the positive adjustment;
- the adjusted score.

This can create anchoring or confirmation effects. The absence of a recorded reason for the adjustment or for the final decision also makes it difficult to determine whether reviewers applied a consistent standard. A rules engine can automate a biased policy, while manual review can reinforce it without leaving a clear audit trail.

## Fairness and equity implications

The process raises both **disparate-treatment** and **disparate-impact** concerns:

- Affiliated applicants receive a direct advantage.
- Unaffiliated applicants are not formally penalized, but they are disadvantaged relative to those receiving the bonus.
- The rule may favor established local residents and people with access to a particular social organization.
- It may exclude applicants who lack the time, resources, geography, or social connections needed to obtain that affiliation.
- A person with similar or better underlying creditworthiness may receive a worse outcome simply because they lack the preferred community characteristic.

The log is small and does not include other protected characteristics, so it cannot establish broader demographic discrimination or quantify disparity rates. Nevertheless, the explicit +10 rule and the contrasting outcomes provide clear evidence of a potentially inequitable decision mechanism.

A fairer process would require a documented, job-related justification for the community adjustment, testing whether it predicts legitimate outcomes beyond the underlying score, reviewing outcomes for affiliated and unaffiliated applicants, and ensuring that manual reviewers apply consistent criteria without relying on unnecessary group or geographic information.
