This DECLARE model is a formalization of a highly controlled, compliance-driven loan origination lifecycle. With `support = 1.0` and `confidence = 1.0` for every rule, the miner is claiming these patterns held in *every* trace in the log. That is a very stringent process - nothing is optional. Here is how it reads in real-life terms:

### 1. The Mandatory Backbone of the Process

These are existence and positional constraints. They define what *must* and *must never* happen.

**`init[Receive_Application]` + `existence[Receive_Application]`**
Every case starts with A. In business terms: no shadow pipeline. You cannot create a loan file, do a credit check, or move money without a formal intake. This is fundamental for KYC and auditability - every dollar has an originating request.

**`existence[Gather_Additional_Documents]`**
C must occur in every case. Even for prime borrowers, the bank is required to collect income statements, tax returns, ID. This is not a conditional path. This reflects a regulatory requirement to document ability-to-repay under e.g., CFPB/ATR, Basel III.

**`exactly_one[Preliminary_Credit_Check]`**
B must happen once and only once per loan. This is crucial business logic:
* You cannot have zero checks - that's reckless lending.
* You cannot have two checks - each hard credit pull costs money, impacts the applicant's credit score, and may trigger Fair Credit Reporting Act issues. Exactly one enforces efficiency and consumer protection.

**`absence[Proceed_Without_Compliance]`**
Z never happens in 100% of cases. This is the explicit compliance kill-switch. The model codifies that bypassing compliance is not just an exception, it is a forbidden activity. Auditors want to see this.

### 2. Sequencing and Dependency - The Controlled Lifecycle

This is where DECLARE shapes the order.

**`response[A -> B]` and `succession[B -> C]` + `chainprecedence[Authorize_Contract_Terms -> Preliminary_Credit_Check]`**
If A happens, B must eventually happen after it. And B and C are in succession: if B happens, C must happen after, and C cannot happen before B.
Real life: `Receive -> Credit Check -> Gather Docs`. You don't waste time collecting documents from someone who fails the initial screen. The chainprecedence rule as encoded - `Authorize_Contract_Terms chainprecedes Preliminary_Credit_Check` - is written as "if Preliminary_Credit_Check occurs, Authorize_Contract_Terms must have occurred immediately before it". In the given dictionary encoding where key=source, target=target, this is logically impossible in a sane loan process. If we interpret the intention as the reverse [B chainprecedes F], it would mean F cannot happen without a credit check right before it, which is also too strict. This points to either a miner artifact or an over-constrained model: the log wants a credit check to be tied tightly to authorization.

**`altsuccession[C -> E]` and `precedence[E -> F]` and `coexistence[C <-> F]`**
This is the Quality Assurance core.
* `coexistence`: C and F co-occur. If you gathered documents you must authorize, and vice-versa. Since C is mandatory, F becomes mandatory by implication.
* `altsuccession`: Each C must be followed by E, and each E must be preceded by C, with no other E between two C's without a C in between. No QA review without fresh documents, no document gathering without a follow-up QA.
* `precedence[E -> F]`: You cannot `Authorize_Contract_Terms` before `Quality_Assurance_Review` has happened. Classic four-eyes principle. QA is a gate.

**`chainsuccession[E -> D]` and `chainresponse[D -> G]`**
* `chainsuccession: Quality_Assurance_Review -> Assemble_Loan_Offer_Package`: If both occur, E must be *immediately* followed by D. No intermediate activity allowed.
* `chainresponse: Assemble_Loan_Offer_Package -> Transfer_Funds`: If you assemble the offer, you must disburse funds *in the very next step*.

In practice this is extremely strict and unrealistic - normally you would have `D -> E -> F -> G`. Here it says QA -> Assemble -> Transfer as a tight, atomic block: review, package, disburse immediately. The business goal is to prevent tampering: once QA passes, no one can alter the offer before money moves.

**`responded_existence[D -> E]`**
If you assemble an offer, a QA review must exist somewhere in the trace. This is the weaker version of the above, guaranteeing quality even if the tight chaining is relaxed.

### 3. The Disbursement and Notification Closure

**`altresponse[G -> H]` and `altprecedence[H -> G]`**
These two together create an `altsuccession[G <-> H]`:
If G occurs, H must occur after it before another G can occur, and if H occurs, G must have occurred before it with no other H in between.
Interpretation: Every fund transfer must be paired with a customer notification, 1-to-1. No double disbursement without informing the customer, and no notification saying "money sent" without an actual transfer. This is transparency and Reg E / UDAAP compliance.

**`nonsuccession[H -> B]`**
If `Notify_Customer` happens, `Preliminary_Credit_Check` cannot happen after it. Once you have told the customer the decision, you cannot go back and run another credit check. The decision is final.

**`nonchainsuccession[F -> H]`**
`Notify_Customer` cannot *immediately* follow `Authorize_Contract_Terms`. You must have an intermediate step - likely Transfer_Funds - between final authorization and communication. Prevents telling a customer it's approved before money is actually available.

### 4. The Contradiction that Reveals a Compliance Truth

**`noncoexistence[G, A]`**
This rule says `Transfer_Funds` and `Receive_Application` can never co-exist in the same case. But `existence[A]` says A must exist in every case. Logically, this model is unsatisfiable - if A is mandatory, G can never happen. Yet G is part of the fund disbursement chain.

In a real log, this would mean one of two things:
1.  The discovery algorithm overfitted with 1.0 support. There were actually no successful disbursements in the log used to mine this - all applications are still pending review, which explains why A and G never co-exist.
2.  It is a data quality flag: the system that records applications and the system that records disbursements are disconnected.

This contradiction itself is valuable: it shows how DECLARE with perfect confidence can expose a process that is *controlled but not completing* - a bank that is excellent at intake and review but never actually gets to disburse.

### What Business Goals Create This Model?

A model this rigid does not come from an agile fintech. It comes from:

*   **Regulatory Compulsion:** KYC/AML, Fair Lending, Ability-to-Repay, GDPR for document handling. Every step must be logged to prove to regulators you didn't discriminate and you verified income.
*   **Risk Mitigation & Loss Avoidance:** Exactly-one credit check, mandatory document collection, mandatory QA -> Authorization chain is classic credit risk control.
*   **Auditability and Non-Repudiation:** Init, absence, and chain constraints make the process linear and defensible. You can prove to an internal auditor: "We never notifed without transferring, we never authorized without QA".
*   **Quality and Reputational Control:** The responded_existence and altsuccession loops prevent offer packages from being built on stale documents.

In short, it is a lifecycle designed for: **Intake > Vet once > Evidence > Independent QA > Package > Immediate Disbursement > Immediate Notification > Final Authorization** - with zero tolerance for compliance bypass.
