**1. Analyzing Historical Scheduling Performance and Dynamics**

Process mining on the MES event logs provides an objective, data-driven reconstruction of what actually happened on the shop floor, independent of the intended or planned schedule. Treat each Job ID (Case ID) as a process case. Apply process discovery algorithms such as the Inductive Miner, Heuristics Miner, or Fuzzy Miner (via tools such as ProM, Disco, Celonis, or pm4py) to the control-flow perspective. This yields process maps and variant collections that reveal the actual sequences of operations (Cutting → CNC Milling → Lathing → …) executed by jobs, including frequencies, deviations from nominal routings, and the high variability inherent in a high-mix job shop.

Simultaneously perform resource-perspective discovery: group events by Machine ID and order them chronologically. This reconstructs the exact sequence of jobs processed on every machine, exposing how work was interleaved and where contention or idle gaps occurred.

Key performance metrics are extracted as follows:

- **Job flow times, lead times, and makespan distributions**: Case duration is computed as the timestamp of the final event (e.g., last Task End or implicit completion) minus the Job Released timestamp. Distributions (mean, median, 90th/95th percentiles, histograms, box-plots) quantify central tendency and tail behavior. Lead time is analogous; makespan can be derived for identifiable cohorts of concurrent jobs.

- **Task waiting times (queue times)**: Intervals from Queue Entry events to the subsequent Setup Start or Task Start on the same resource are measured for every activity–machine combination. Aggregates (mean/median waiting per work center), full distributions, and visualizations such as dotted charts (cases ordered by start time on the y-axis, time on the x-axis, colored by activity) or the performance spectrum reveal queue dynamics, concurrency, and periods of congestion.

- **Resource (machine and operator) utilization**: For each Machine ID, productive time is the sum of actual Task Durations; setup time is the sum of (Setup End − Setup Start); breakdown time is taken from Breakdown Start/End events; idle time is residual calendar gaps. Utilization is expressed as (productive + setup) / available time (or excluding planned downtime). Operator utilization follows the same logic using Operator ID. Time-based heatmaps or shift profiles expose imbalances.

- **Sequence-dependent setup times**: This is central. For each machine, sort its events by timestamp and identify consecutive jobs (Task End of job \(i\) followed by Setup Start or Task Start of job \(i+1\)). Extract the observed setup duration. Enrich each pair with attributes of the preceding and succeeding jobs (material type, geometry class, tooling family, or the explicit “Previous job” note in the log; join with MES/ERP master data if needed). Aggregate into an empirical setup-time matrix (or conditional distributions) keyed by attribute pairs, or train a predictive model (regression or tree-based) that estimates setup duration given the from–to attributes. Total setup time as a percentage of capacity and its contribution to overall flow time are then quantified.

- **Schedule adherence and tardiness**: For every completed case, compare the actual completion timestamp against the Order Due Date attribute. Compute percentage of tardy jobs, mean tardiness \(\max(0, C_j - d_j)\), total and weighted tardiness (weights from Order Priority), and the full lateness distribution. Planned-versus-actual task duration columns directly measure estimation bias and variance.

- **Impact of disruptions**: Isolate Breakdown Start/End events and Priority Change events. For cases whose execution overlaps a breakdown or is affected by a hot-job insertion, measure excess waiting and flow-time inflation relative to matched non-disrupted periods (difference-in-differences or stratified comparison). Cascade effects are visible by tracing delayed downstream Queue Entry and Task Start timestamps.

These analyses are multi-perspective (control-flow, organizational/resource, time, and data attributes) and produce both visual artifacts (annotated process maps, dotted charts, utilization profiles, setup heatmaps) and quantitative distributions that form the factual baseline of current performance.

**2. Diagnosing Scheduling Pathologies**

The metrics above supply direct evidence of systemic inefficiencies:

- **Bottleneck resources**: Machines that simultaneously exhibit high utilization (>80–90 %) and long average queue times / high frequency of “Waiting for resource” are flagged as bottlenecks (classic bottleneck analysis in process-mining tools). Their impact is quantified by the fraction of total job flow time spent waiting at those resources and by regression of overall lead time or tardiness on bottleneck queue length. Specialized machines (certain CNC mills, heat-treatment equipment) typically dominate.

- **Poor task prioritization**: Comparative/variant analysis splits the log into on-time versus tardy cases, or High-priority versus Medium/Low-priority cases. If high-priority or near-due-date jobs still show substantial waiting times, or if queue-composition analysis reveals frequent selection of lower-priority jobs when higher-priority ones are present, the local FCFS/EDD mix is failing. Correlation between priority score and sojourn time is weaker than expected.

- **Suboptimal sequencing inflating setups**: Using the mined setup matrix, the observed cumulative setup time on a machine is compared with a lower-bound sequence (greedy nearest-neighbor or an optimized ordering of the same job set). Large ratios indicate waste. Reconstructing concurrent queues shows periods in which dissimilar jobs were sequenced while similar jobs waited, confirming myopic local decisions.

- **Starvation of downstream resources**: Multi-resource timeline or dotted-chart alignment reveals intervals in which flexible downstream machines are idle while upstream bottleneck queues are non-empty (or vice versa). Lost productive capacity is the integral of such idle time that could have been avoided by better flow control.

- **Bullwhip-like WIP variability**: The number of concurrently active cases (global or stage-wise WIP) is tracked over time from release and completion events. High variance, or progressive amplification of queue-length fluctuations along the process (visible in the performance spectrum), is linked to priority swings, batching effects, and disruption recovery.

These pathologies are not anecdotal; they are statistically evidenced by stratified distributions, process comparisons (e.g., Earth-Mover’s Distance between variant sets), and attribute-influenced enhancement of the discovered models.

**3. Root Cause Analysis of Scheduling Ineffectiveness**

The diagnosed pathologies stem primarily from the mismatch between the complexity of the environment and the simplicity of the current control logic:

- Static, local dispatching rules (FCFS mixed with EDD) are fundamentally myopic. They ignore remaining work, sequence-dependent setup costs, downstream congestion, and global due-date risk. In a high-mix shop with unique routings this produces cascading inefficiencies.
- Absence of real-time holistic visibility means each work center decides without knowledge of machine availability, queue lengths, or predicted remaining times elsewhere.
- Task-duration and setup-time estimates are either inaccurate (planned versus actual discrepancies are large) or unused; historical distributions and conditional dependencies are ignored.
- Sequence-dependent setups are not systematically minimized; changeovers are accepted as a byproduct of priority or arrival order.
- Coordination across work centers is absent, allowing starvation and overload to coexist.
- Disruption handling is purely reactive and local (a hot job simply jumps the queue; a breakdown simply idles the machine), generating priority inversions and unrecovered capacity loss.

Process mining differentiates scheduling-logic failures from pure capacity shortages or inherent process variability. Utilization profiles over time show whether high tardiness/WIP occurs even when average utilization is moderate and idle time exists (logic problem) or only when resources are saturated (capacity problem). Variance decomposition separates residual task-time variability (after conditioning on job attributes, machine, and operator via decision mining or regression) from excess flow-time variance caused by queueing; the latter is scheduling-induced. Comparative analysis of disruption-free versus disruption-rich periods, and of high-load versus low-load windows, isolates the marginal contribution of each factor. Decision-point analysis and attribute-influence measures further quantify how much waiting-time variance is explained by the actual selection decisions (previous job attributes, priority of chosen versus alternative jobs) versus exogenous load. If performance remains poor in periods of available capacity and low inherent variability, the root cause is the scheduling logic itself.

**4. Developing Advanced Data-Driven Scheduling Strategies**

Three complementary, process-mining-informed strategies address the pathologies directly.

**Strategy 1 – Multi-factor dynamic dispatching with setup and downstream awareness**  
When a machine becomes free, every waiting job receives a composite priority index (an extension of Apparent Tardiness Cost with Setups – ATCS – enriched by mined data):

\[
\pi_j = w_1\cdot\frac{1}{\text{CR}_j} + w_2\cdot\text{PriorityScore}_j + w_3\cdot\frac{1}{\widehat{s}_{ij}} + w_4\cdot\text{DownstreamFactor}_j + w_5\cdot\text{RemainingWorkRatio}_j
\]

where \(\text{CR}_j\) is the critical ratio (time to due date divided by remaining processing time estimated from the process model), \(\widehat{s}_{ij}\) is the predicted sequence-dependent setup from the current job \(i\) (looked up or regressed from the mined setup matrix), and the downstream factor reflects current or predicted congestion on subsequent machines in the job’s routing. Weights are tuned by offline simulation or by replaying historical logs under alternative weightings and measuring resulting tardiness/WIP.  

Process-mining inputs: the setup matrix and conditional duration models, correlation analysis identifying which factors most strongly predict tardiness, and discovered remaining-path statistics. This directly mitigates poor prioritization, myopic sequencing, and starvation. Expected impact: 20–40 % reduction in mean and percentile tardiness (especially for high-priority jobs), 15–30 % lower total setup time, smoother WIP, and more balanced utilization.

**Strategy 2 – Predictive/proactive scheduling using stochastic models**  
Historical actual durations are fitted to conditional distributions (log-normal or gamma, stratified or regressed on machine, operator, job attributes). Predictive process monitoring models (e.g., XGBoost or LSTM on partial traces) estimate remaining cycle time and due-date attainment probability for every active job. These predictions feed rolling-horizon re-prioritization, proactive capacity reservation for emerging bottlenecks, and early warning of disruption-induced cascades (using mined MTBF/MTTR patterns). When a bottleneck is forecast to overload, upstream release can be throttled or flexible jobs re-routed.  

Process-mining inputs: duration and setup distributions, process models for remaining-path prediction, and historical disruption impact analysis. This attacks unpredictable lead times, cascading delays, and reactive-only disruption handling. Expected impact: substantially more accurate due-date quotations, earlier intervention that reduces tardiness under disruption, lower WIP variability, and higher effective throughput.

**Strategy 3 – Bottleneck-focused setup minimization via intelligent sequencing and similarity batching**  
Bottlenecks identified by process mining receive special treatment. A full empirical setup-transition matrix is maintained. Waiting jobs are re-sequenced (greedy insertion heuristics in real time; genetic algorithms or ATSP approximations for longer queues) to minimize cumulative setups subject to due-date and priority constraints. Jobs are also clustered by the attributes that drive setup time so that similar jobs can be grouped. The optimized sequence overrides the ordinary dispatching rule only at the critical resources.  

Process-mining inputs: bottleneck ranking, the setup matrix, historical sequence analysis that quantifies avoidable setup waste, and counterfactual re-sequencing of past machine logs to estimate savings. This targets the amplification of bottleneck impact by changeovers and the associated WIP accumulation. Expected impact: 20–50 % reduction in setup time percentage at bottlenecks, increased effective capacity, shorter queues, and secondary improvements in flow time and tardiness.

The three strategies are designed to be hybridized: Strategy 2 predictions feed the index of Strategy 1 and the constraints of Strategy 3; Strategy 3 acts as a local optimizer at detected bottlenecks while Strategy 1 governs the rest of the shop.

**5. Simulation, Evaluation, and Continuous Improvement**

Discrete-event simulation (SimPy, AnyLogic, Arena, or FlexSim) is parameterized directly from the process-mining outputs: inter-arrival distributions from Job Released events, routing probabilities or exact paths from discovered models, processing-time distributions (conditioned on features), the full sequence-dependent setup matrix or predictive function, breakdown MTBF/MTTR distributions, and hot-job arrival characteristics. The simulation implements the shop as a network of resources with state-dependent changeovers and the candidate dispatching/sequencing logic.

Critical validation step: first replicate historical KPIs under the baseline FCFS/EDD rules; only after the model matches observed flow times, tardiness, setup percentages, and utilization does it become a credible test-bed. Scenarios include nominal load matching the past year, high load (+20–30 % arrivals or larger jobs), elevated disruption rates, and product-mix shifts. Multiple replications with warm-up and long horizons yield statistically comparable distributions of the key KPIs (mean/percentile flow time and lead time, percentage tardy, mean and weighted tardiness, average/max WIP, setup-time fraction, utilization breakdown). Sensitivity analysis on prediction error, setup-matrix accuracy, and weight settings quantifies robustness. This permits risk-free ranking of strategies and parameter tuning before any live deployment.

For continuous improvement, process mining is embedded as a live service on the MES event stream. Real-time dashboards display annotated process maps, current bottleneck indicators, live WIP, predicted tardiness for open jobs, and utilization gauges. KPI trend lines and threshold alerts detect degradation. Concept-drift detectors monitor changes in variant frequencies, duration distributions, or setup averages. Periodically (or on alert) the models, matrices, and strategy parameters are re-mined/re-optimized from recent data; new rule variants can run in shadow mode for empirical validation. The improved predictive capabilities also feed more accurate due-date quotations to customers, reducing future pressure. The result is a closed-loop system that continuously adapts the scheduling logic to evolving shop-floor realities, sustaining and compounding the initial gains.
