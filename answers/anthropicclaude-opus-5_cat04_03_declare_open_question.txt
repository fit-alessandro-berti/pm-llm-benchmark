## 1. Reading the model before interpreting it

Two preliminary remarks, because they change everything that follows.

**(a) Direction conventions matter.** In this dictionary form, each constraint is stored as `{activation: {..., 'target': X}}`. For *forward-looking* templates (`response`, `chainresponse`, `succession`, `altresponse`, `chainsuccession`) the key is the earlier activity and the target the later one. For *backward-looking* templates (`precedence`, `altprecedence`, `chainprecedence`) the key is the **later** activity and the target is the **required predecessor** ("X is preceded by Y"). We can verify this internally: the model contains both `altresponse: G  H` (funds then notification) and `altprecedence: H  G`. These are only mutually consistent if `altprecedence(H, G)` reads "H is preceded by G" — i.e., they jointly form an alternating succession between disbursement and customer notification. So I apply that convention throughout.

**(b) Every constraint has support = confidence = 1.0.** That is not a "strong model"; it is a *suspicious* model. Either the log was tiny/idealised, or the model was hand-authored and the metrics stamped on afterwards. Real discovered loan models show 0.8–0.99 support with visible exception paths (withdrawn applications, rejected applicants, re-work loops). A wall of 1.0s means the model has no tolerance for the ordinary messiness of banking operations — an important point I return to in §6.

## 3. Constraint-by-constraint: what control does it implement?

**Cardinality / existence controls**

- `existence(A)` + `init(A)` — nothing happens without a formally registered application. This kills "shadow underwriting", verbal pre-approvals, and back-dating: the case must *begin* with intake, which fixes the start of the audit trail and the statutory clocks (e.g., adverse-action / decision deadlines).
- `existence(C)` — evidence-based lending. No file may be decided purely on the bureau score; supplementary documentation is mandatory (income verification, affordability substantiation).
- `exactly_one(B)` — the sharpest and most interesting cardinality rule. Not "at least one" (no unassessed applicants) and not "many" (no repeated bureau pulls). Business reasons for the upper bound: (i) each hard credit inquiry damages the applicant's score, and repeated pulls are a consumer-protection issue; (ii) bureau calls are billed per query; (iii) most importantly, **re-scoring is score-shopping** — if staff could re-run the check until a favourable result appeared, the decision becomes manipulable and unauditable. "Exactly one" makes the credit decision anchored to a single, timestamped, reproducible assessment.
- `absence(Z)` — zero-tolerance control bypass.

**Ordering / flow controls**

- `response(A  B)` — every intake must eventually be assessed. This is the anti-"application left in a drawer" rule; combined with SLAs it becomes a fair-treatment control (no silent discrimination via selective neglect).
- `succession(B  C)` — bidirectional: after the credit check documents *must* be gathered, and documents may *only* be gathered after the check. The second half is the economically meaningful one: don't burden the customer (or the ops team) with heavy document collection before the cheap pre-screen has been passed. It also prevents "documents first, score later" reverse engineering of a file.
- `altsuccession(C  E)` — every document-gathering episode must be followed by a QA review *before another gathering episode can start*, and every QA must be preceded by a gathering episode. This enforces a **strict collect  review cycle**: you cannot pile up three rounds of documents and review them once, and you cannot review a file that has not been freshly evidenced. It is the classic "each iteration is inspected" quality gate.
- `chainsuccession(E  D)` — QA is *immediately* followed by offer assembly and offer assembly is *immediately* preceded by QA. Zero-gap adjacency here means: the offer is priced on a file whose state has just been validated, and nothing (no data edit, no re-negotiation, no override) can slip between the review and the offer. This is the strongest possible "no tampering window" guarantee.
- `chainresponse(D  G)` — the assembled package is immediately followed by disbursement. Interpreted charitably, this expresses "once the final package is assembled the file is complete and payment executes automatically" — straight-through processing with no manual step between offer and payment.
- `precedence(E, F)` / `coexistence(C, F)` / `chainprecedence(F, B)` — the authorisation cluster: contract authorisation requires that a QA review has taken place (`precedence`), authorisation and document gathering always accompany each other (`coexistence`, i.e. no authorised deal without a documented file and no documented file left unauthorised), and every authorisation is *directly* preceded by the preliminary credit check (`chainprecedence`).
- `altresponse(G  H)` + `altprecedence(H  G)` — an alternating pairing of disbursement and notification: every disbursement is followed by exactly-interleaved customer notification, and every notification refers back to a disbursement. Operationally: **the customer is always told, and told once per event**, no silent money movements, no duplicate/ orphan notifications.
- `responded_existence(D  E)` — if an offer package exists anywhere in the case, a QA review exists somewhere in the case (order-free). This is a *redundant safety net* on top of `chainsuccession(E, D)`: even if the chain rule were relaxed during model maintenance, the "no un-reviewed offer" invariant would survive. Deliberate defence-in-depth in constraint sets is good practice.

**Negative (prohibitive) controls**

- `nonsuccession(H, B)` — once the customer has been notified, no credit check may follow. In other words the decision is final: you cannot notify and then quietly re-assess creditworthiness on the same case. Re-assessment requires a *new* case (and a new application). This protects both the audit trail and the customer.
- `nonchainsuccession(F, H)` — authorisation must never be *immediately* followed by customer notification. Something must intervene. Practically this forbids "we signed it, we told them" without the intermediate execution/verification steps (and it prevents pre-announcing terms as approved before downstream checks complete).
- `noncoexistence(G, A)` — funds transfer and application intake never occur in the same case. **This is where the model breaks.**

## 4. The hidden defect: the model is unsatisfiable

Take the constraints at face value and reason forward:

1. `init(A)` and `existence(A)`  **A occurs in every trace**.
2. `noncoexistence(G, A)`  since A always occurs, **G can never occur**.
3. `chainresponse(D  G)`  every D is immediately followed by a G. Since G is impossible, **D can never occur**.
4. `chainsuccession(E  D)`  every E is immediately followed by a D. Since D is impossible, **E can never occur**.
5. `altsuccession(C  E)`  every C requires a later E. Since E is impossible, **C can never occur**.
6. But `existence(C)`  **C must occur**. Contradiction.

(The same collapse arrives independently through `precedence(E, F)` + `coexistence(C, F)`, and `altprecedence(H, G)` additionally makes H impossible.)

So **no event log trace can satisfy this model** — yet every constraint is reported with support 1.0, which is only possible if the metrics were fabricated, computed on an empty/vacuous basis, or the constraints were assembled from different sources without a global consistency check. This is the single most important practical finding: a DECLARE model can be individually plausible constraint-by-constraint and globally impossible, and only a satisfiability check (SAT/automaton product) catches it.

**What was probably intended.** `noncoexistence` was almost certainly the wrong template for the sentence *"funds must never be disbursed before the application is received."* That sentence is `precedence(G, A)` — "G is preceded by A" — or its negative twin `nonsuccession(G, A)` ("A never after G"). With that single substitution the model becomes consistent, and it admits essentially one canonical happy path:

```
A    B    F    C    E    D    G    H
Receive  Credit check  Authorize terms  Gather docs  QA  Assemble offer  Disburse  Notify
```

Check it: init ; exactly_one(B) ; B immediately precedes F (`chainprecedence`) ; F precedes E (`precedence`) ; CF coexist ; BC succession ; CE altsuccession ; E immediately followed by D ; D immediately followed by G ; GH alternating ; F not immediately followed by H ; no B after H ; no Z .

**But look at that path critically** — it exposes a *second*, semantic problem. `chainprecedence(F, B)` forces **Authorize_Contract_Terms to sit immediately after the preliminary credit check, before documents are collected and before QA**. That is business nonsense and, in a regulated lender, a finding: terms would be authorised on the strength of a bureau score alone, with the evidence file and the compliance review happening afterwards. The domain-sensible model is the mirror image: **authorisation should be chain-*preceded* by QA** (`chainprecedence(F, E)`), and disbursement should be preceded by authorisation (`precedence(G, F)` — currently *nothing in the model requires F before G*, which is a genuine control gap: money can move without the contract being authorised, as long as F happens somewhere in the case).

A corrected constraint set would look roughly like:

```
init(A); existence(A, C, E, F); absence(Z); exactly_one(B)
response(A  B); succession(B  C); altsuccession(C  E)
chainsuccession(E  D)                 # QA immediately before offer assembly
chainprecedence(F, E) or precedence(F, D)   # authorise only after review/offer
precedence(G, F)                       # NEW: no disbursement without authorisation
precedence(G, A)                       # replaces noncoexistence(G, A)
altsuccession(G  H)
nonsuccession(H, B); absence(Z)
```

with the canonical path `A  B  C  E  D  F  G  H`.

## 5. How the (repaired) rule interplay produces control and transparency

- **Anchoring**: `init(A)` + `existence(A)` fix a single, non-forgeable start of the audit trail; `precedence(G, A)` guarantees money never precedes the record that justifies it. Together they make the case reconstructable end-to-end — the core of any audit or regulatory examination.
- **Non-manipulability of the decision**: `exactly_one(B)` + `nonsuccession(H, B)` bracket the credit assessment: one assessment, and no re-assessment after the customer has been told. The decision basis is immutable.
- **Evidence before judgement**: `succession(B  C)` + `existence(C)` ensure documented substantiation; `altsuccession(C  E)` guarantees each evidence round is inspected.
- **No tamper windows**: the *chain* constraints are the strongest tool in DECLARE and are used exactly where a gap would be dangerous — between review and offer assembly. Adjacency in the log means no unlogged intervention could have occurred.
- **Segregation of duties made visible**: `precedence(*, E)`-style rules encode the four-eyes principle at the process level. DECLARE cannot express *who* performs a step, so the natural extension is to pair these constraints with resource rules ("the resource of E  the resource of D/F").
- **Customer transparency**: the alternating `G  H` pairing guarantees every money movement has exactly one corresponding communication — the process-level realisation of disclosure duties.
- **Bypass detection**: `absence(Z)` turns a cultural expectation ("don't override controls") into a machine-checkable assertion over the log.

## 6. Plausible business and regulatory drivers

- **Credit risk & prudential rules** (Basel III/CRD, internal ratings): scoring must be performed, once, on documented data, and be reproducible — hence `exactly_one(B)` and the mandatory document collection.
- **AML/KYC and sanctions** (FATF, EU AMLD, BSA): identity and source-of-funds evidence before disbursement; disbursement traceable to an application.
- **Consumer credit and fair-lending law** (US: ECOA/Reg B, TILA/Reg Z, Fair Credit Reporting Act; EU: Consumer Credit Directive): every application gets assessed (`response(A  B)`), decisions and terms are disclosed (`G  H`), no gratuitous repeated hard inquiries (`exactly_one(B)`), and adverse-action timelines run from a fixed intake event (`init(A)`).
- **Internal control / financial reporting** (SOX §404, delegated credit authority frameworks): authorisation must exist for every disbursed loan and must follow a compliance review; no payment without an authorising act.
- **Operational-risk and fraud controls**: chain constraints prevent the classic insider fraud pattern of altering a file *between* the review and the offer, or paying out to an account substituted after approval.
- **Privacy / data minimisation** (GDPR): collect documents only after the pre-screen makes them relevant — `succession(B  C)`'s backward half is literally a data-minimisation control.
- **Model risk management** (SR 11-7 style): one scoring execution per case, logged, so decisions can be replayed and challenged.

## 7. Practical caveats if this model were deployed

1. **Run a satisfiability check before publishing.** As shown, this model admits no trace. Any DECLARE model destined for conformance checking or run-time monitoring should be compiled to an automaton and tested for non-emptiness, and ideally for *vacuity* (constraints trivially satisfied because their activation never fires).
2. **Chain constraints are brittle in real logs.** Real cases contain "note added", "customer called", "SLA escalation", "case reassigned" events. `chainsuccession(E  D)` will fail on the first such interleaving. Either the log must be filtered to a control-relevant alphabet, or these should be weakened to `succession`/`altsuccession`.
3. **The model has no exception paths.** No rejection, no withdrawal, no cancellation, no re-work loop — yet `existence(C)`, `existence(A)` and the disbursement chain effectively assert *every* application is funded. In reality most loan processes have 30–70% non-funded cases. A realistic model would decompose per outcome (approved / declined / withdrawn) or replace hard `existence` rules with high-support-but-not-1.0 ones and add `not_coexistence(Transfer_Funds, Decline_Application)`.
4. **The control gap matters more than the redundancy.** Redundant constraints (`responded_existence(D  E)` on top of `chainsuccession(E  D)`) are harmless defence-in-depth. The missing `precedence(G, F)` — no disbursement without authorisation — is a real, auditable weakness. When reviewing a declarative control model, always ask which *pairs* of critical activities are left unordered.
5. **Metrics should be earned, not asserted.** Report support (fraction of traces/activations satisfying) and confidence separately, plus activation counts, so a reviewer can distinguish "always true" from "never tested".

**Bottom line:** the model tells a coherent compliance story — single anchored intake, one immutable credit assessment, evidence-then-review cycles, a tamper-proof reviewoffer adjacency, paired disbursement/notification, and zero tolerance for control bypass — and it maps cleanly onto prudential, AML and consumer-credit obligations. But as literally specified it is logically empty (`noncoexistence(Transfer_Funds, Receive_Application)` versus mandatory intake), and its authorisation step is sequenced implausibly early while the crucial "no funds without authorisation" rule is absent. Those three fixes turn an appealing narrative into an enforceable control model.
