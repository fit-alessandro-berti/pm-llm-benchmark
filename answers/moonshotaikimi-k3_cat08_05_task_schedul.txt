## 4. Advanced Data-Driven Scheduling Strategies

### Strategy 1 — Calibrated Dynamic Dispatching: ATCS+ with Downstream Awareness

**Core logic.** Replace FCFS/EDD with a **composite index rule** evaluated at every machine-free event. The foundation is the **Apparent Tardiness Cost with Setups (ATCS)** rule, which is purpose-built for sequence-dependent setups:

I_j(t, i) = (w_j / p_j) × exp( max(d_j  p_j  t, 0) / (k·p) ) × exp( s_ij / (k·s) )

where w = priority weight, p = predicted processing time, s_ij = predicted setup given the *current* machine occupant i, and k, k are scaling parameters. I extend it with a **downstream-load term** (favor jobs whose next operation feeds a near-starved machine; penalize jobs heading to an already-congested center) and a **bottleneck-feeding override** (jobs destined for the current constraint get expedited upstream — a drum-buffer-rope flavor).

**How process mining informs it.**
- p and s_ij come from the mined duration distributions and setup matrix (not the biased MES planning values).
- k, k and the downstream-term weight are **tuned by simulation** (Section 5) per load regime — e.g., heavier setup aversion (lower k) when the bottleneck is saturated, heavier due-date pressure during high-tardiness periods. This yields a small set of **context-dependent parameter sets**, switched automatically based on mined shop-state thresholds.
- Priority weights w are calibrated from mined expedite costs (Section 1.2) so a "hot job" insertion is rationally priced.

**Pathologies addressed:** priority inversions (2.1b), setup churn (2.1c), starvation (2.1d).
**Expected impact:** strong reduction in *weighted* tardiness (ATCS-class rules are well-documented to dominate EDD under SDST); reduced setup burden at bottlenecks; modest WIP reduction via downstream throttling. Limitation: still locally greedy — which motivates Strategies 2 and 3.

### Strategy 2 — Predictive, Rolling-Horizon Finite-Capacity Scheduling

**Core logic.** Move from dispatching (reactive, local) to **predictive finite-capacity scheduling** on a rolling horizon:

1. **Predictive layer:**
 - **Task duration models:** gradient-boosted or quantile-regression models per activity, with features from the log (machine, operator, job attributes, routing position). Quantile outputs (p50/p80) let the scheduler choose its risk posture.
 - **Remaining-flow-time prediction per in-flight job** (predictive process monitoring on trace prefixes)  continuously updated, realistic customer promise dates. This directly attacks *unpredictable lead times*.
 - **Breakdown risk:** mine MTBF/MTTR distributions per machine; if sensor/telemetry data exists, graduate to failure-risk scoring. At minimum, model stochastic availability in the schedule.

2. **Optimization layer:** a **constraint-programming / MILP job-shop solver with sequence-dependent setups** builds a finite-capacity schedule over a 1–2 week horizon, freezing only the near-term window (e.g., next shift) to limit nervousness.

3. **Re-optimization triggers (event-driven):** breakdown start, hot-job arrival, or predicted tardiness of a high-value order crossing a threshold  re-solve for the affected horizon segment. Mined time-to-recover distributions set realistic rescheduling assumptions.

4. **Proactive alerting:** jobs with predicted tardiness probability > threshold are flagged *days* in advance, enabling escalation (overtime, outsourcing, renegotiation) instead of post-hoc apology.

**How process mining informs it:** every parameter — durations, setup matrices, routing variant probabilities, breakdown models, even realistic shift calendars — is mined rather than assumed. Conformance data (planned vs. actual) quantifies how much buffer the schedule needs and where.

**Pathologies addressed:** unpredictable lead times, poor disruption response, lack of coordination, estimation bias.
**Expected impact:** materially better due-date reliability and quote accuracy; faster, cheaper disruption recovery; reduced schedule nervousness vs. daily replanning. Cost: data/model maintenance and solver infrastructure — the reason Strategy 1 remains as a fallback layer.

### Strategy 3 — Setup-Family Campaigning at the Bottleneck (plus targeted SMED)

**Core logic.**
1. **Family discovery:** use the clustered setup families from Section 1.2 (groups of jobs whose pairwise setups are minimal).
2. **Campaign formation:** at the bottleneck machine(s), form **dynamic batches** of same-family jobs. Batch release is urgency-gated: a batch runs when either (a) it reaches a target size, or (b) its oldest member's slack falls below a threshold — this bounds the tardiness cost of waiting for batchmates.
3. **Sequencing:** within a family, run EDD; across families, choose the next family by cheapest transition × urgency (a small combinatorial problem solved by CP or insertion heuristics each cycle).
4. **Structural improvement:** feed the mined "worst transition" list (expensive, frequent family switches) to a **SMED program** — engineering out the top transitions yields permanent capacity gains no algorithm can match.

**How process mining informs it:** families, transition costs, batch-size thresholds, and slack gates all come from the mined setup matrix and historical trade-off analysis between batching delay and setup savings.

**Pathologies addressed:** setup waste (2.1c), bottleneck capacity loss, and indirectly WIP/lead time.
**Expected impact:** when setups consume 15–30% of bottleneck load, family campaigning typically reclaims a double-digit share of that capacity — equivalent to buying machine time at zero capex. Risk (tardiness from batch-waiting) is bounded by the slack gate and validated in simulation.

**Complementary layers (brief):** (i) **Release control** (CONWIP/workload control): cap total shop WIP at the mined Little's-Law-optimal level — releasing jobs only as others finish; this alone often cuts flow-time p90 substantially at zero throughput cost. (ii) A **reinforcement-learning dispatching agent** trained in the Section-5 simulator is a credible next-generation successor to Strategy 1 once the digital twin is validated.

## 5. Simulation, Evaluation, and Continuous Improvement

### 5.1 Discrete-Event Simulation (DES) Testbed

**Parameterization — everything mined, nothing assumed:**
- **Arrivals:** job release process from `Job Released` events (inter-arrival distributions, mix probabilities, seasonal patterns, priority/due-date assignment logic).
- **Routings:** empirical routing-variant distribution (a Markov model over work-center transitions from the clustered archetypes).
- **Processing/setup times:** fitted or empirical distributions per activity/machine, including the full **setup transition matrix** and operator effects.
- **Disruptions:** MTBF/MTTR per machine from breakdown events; hot-job injection process from priority-change events.
- **Calendars:** shifts, breaks, planned maintenance from resource availability reconstruction.

**Validation:** replay a historical 3-month window and statistically compare simulated vs. actual KPI distributions (flow time, per-station queues, utilization; e.g., via distribution tests and within-tolerance error bounds). Only a validated twin is used for policy testing.

**Experimental design:**

| Scenario dimension | Levels |
|---|---|
| Scheduling policy | Baseline (current FCFS/EDD mix) / S1 / S2 / S3 / combinations (e.g., S1+S3, S2+release control) |
| Load | Normal / +15% demand surge / bottleneck at 95%+ |
| Disruption intensity | Baseline MTBF / 2× breakdowns / 3× hot-job rate |
| Mix | Current / shifted mix (new families, unseen transitions — robustness check) |
| Estimation quality | Mined-accurate vs. current biased planning values (isolates the value of better data alone) |

**Metrics:** mean & weighted tardiness, service level, flow time (mean, p90), WIP, bottleneck utilization, setup share, schedule stability (nervousness after disruptions). **Statistics:** 30 replications per cell, warm-up exclusion, common random numbers for variance reduction, confidence intervals and pairwise comparisons — no policy is declared a winner on a single run. A key secondary output is **robustness**: the best average performer may be brittle under disruptions; prefer policies with flat degradation curves.

### 5.2 Continuous Monitoring and Adaptation Framework

1. **Streaming process mining layer.** Land MES events continuously into the mining platform; maintain live dashboards of the Section-1 KPIs: tardiness trend, flow-time percentiles, per-station WIP vs. CONWIP cap, utilization decomposition, inversion rate, setup share at the active bottleneck.

2. **Control charts + drift detection.** Statistical process control on KPIs; **concept-drift detectors** (e.g., ADWIN/Page–Hinkley on duration distributions, setup matrices, and routing probabilities) to automatically flag when the mined models underpinning Strategies 1–3 go stale — e.g., new materials change the setup matrix, or a new operator cohort shifts duration distributions.

3. **Deviation alerting.** Conformance of live execution vs. the finite-capacity schedule: persistent alignment deviations (skipped/reordered tasks) trigger investigation — is the schedule unrealistic, or is the floor circumventing it (a governance signal in itself)?

4. **Periodic recalibration cadence.** Monthly: re-tune rule parameters and re-fit duration/setup models. Quarterly: re-validate the DES twin against fresh history; re-run the policy tournament to confirm the deployed strategy still dominates as mix and demand evolve.

5. **Governed rollout loop.** Deploy changes as controlled experiments (pilot work center or shadow-mode A/B), measure against mined baselines, institutionalize via a monthly S&OP-style review that owns the pathologies-to-policy linkage.

This closes the loop: **mine  diagnose  redesign  simulate  deploy  monitor  re-mine** — converting scheduling from a static, intuition-driven function into a continuously learning system grounded in the shop's own behavioral data.
