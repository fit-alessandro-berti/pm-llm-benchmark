In this POWL model, the bias is introduced in this XOR:

```
XOR( CheckLocalAffiliation [D], skip )
```

### How the bias works

1.  **Two different paths to ManualReview:** Every case must go through `A -> LOOP(B,G) -> C -> E -> F`, but between `C` and `E` there is a choice.
2.  **Only one path gets enrichment:** Cases that go through `D` receive what you describe as a "subtle score uplift". Cases that take `skip` go to `E` with only `C`.
3.  **Non-uniform advantage:** `D = Check if applicant is a local resident and member of a known community group`. So membership in that community group becomes a positive feature. Two applicants who are identical on `B` = DataValidation and `C` = PreliminaryScoring will have different scores at `E` and therefore different outcomes at `F` = FinalDecision, solely based on local affiliation.

In a loan process, even a small uplift at that point propagates to approval rate and terms - interest, limit, collateral requirement - at `F`.

### Why it is a fairness problem even if the group is not legally protected

In the US, discrimination in credit is regulated by ECOA and Fair Lending - it prohibits discrimination on the basis of race, color, religion, national origin, sex, marital status, age, etc. "Local resident and member of a known community group" is not itself a protected attribute.

However, it still creates three problems:

**1. Procedural unfairness:** A fair lending model should be based on creditworthiness - ability and willingness to repay. Local affiliation is not predictive of credit risk, but is used to inflate the score. This violates the principle of merit-based decision making.

**2. Proxy / Disparate Impact:** In practice, local residence + community membership is highly correlated with protected attributes like race, national origin, and familial/social origin. If that community group is, for example, predominantly of a certain ethnicity, religion, or socioeconomic background, then `D` acts as a proxy. Even without intent to discriminate, US regulators apply *disparate impact* doctrine: a facially neutral practice that disproportionately disadvantages a protected group is unlawful if it is not justified by business necessity.

**3. Equity and feedback loop:** Giving terms advantage to an in-group concentrates capital in that group. Those applicants then build better credit history, get better future terms, and can sponsor other members. Applicants on the `skip` branch systematically get worse terms, higher default risk due to worse terms, and are then scored worse in future. It creates a two-tier system.

From a process mining / fairness measurement perspective, you would see this as:

* **Violation of demographic parity and equal opportunity:** `P(F=Approved | D taken) > P(F=Approved | skip taken)` for same `C` distribution
* **Lack of justification:** The uplift cannot be explained by risk variables in `B` and `C`
* **Opacity:** Because it is modeled as an XOR with a silent skip, it can be hidden in conformance checking as an "optional enrichment" rather than an explicit scoring rule, making it harder to audit.

**Mitigation if this were a real process:**

> Do not include `D` in the scoring path. If local affiliation data is collected for other reasons, e.g. KYC, keep it separate from scoring. If you want to keep it, you need to demonstrate business necessity, audit for correlation with protected attributes, measure approval/terms gap conditioned on `C`, and remove the unconditional uplift. The fair version would be: `A -> LOOP(B,G) -> C -> E -> F`.
