# 1. Analyzing Historical Scheduling Performance and Dynamics

The first objective is to create a decision-grade representation of what actually happened—not merely what the routing master or original schedule said should happen. This becomes the analytical foundation for scheduling improvements and a calibrated shop-floor digital twin.

## 1.1 Event-log preparation and process reconstruction

### A. Create a multi-object event model

A conventional case-centric log should use the job as the primary case:

- **Case:** Job/order ID
- **Activity:** Queue entry, setup, processing, inspection, transfer, rework, completion
- **Lifecycle:** Start/end, suspend/resume, cancel
- **Resources:** Machine, operator, tooling, work center
- **Attributes:** Priority, due date, material, part family, geometry, quantity, routing, setup family, planned duration

However, the MES data also contains events whose natural object is a machine, operator, or maintenance incident. An **object-centric process mining** model is therefore preferable. It links:

- Jobs to operations
- Operations to machines
- Operations to operators
- Setups to preceding and succeeding jobs
- Breakdowns to affected machines and operations
- Priority changes to all remaining operations of a job

Each operation should have a unique operation-instance ID. Without this, repeated activities such as two grinding steps in the same job can be incorrectly merged.

### B. Normalize timestamps and calendars

Before analysis:

1. Correct duplicate, missing, and out-of-order events.
2. Match start/end events into intervals.
3. Account for shifts, weekends, planned maintenance, and overtime.
4. Identify interrupted and resumed tasks.
5. Distinguish planned downtime from breakdowns.
6. Verify that machine and operator assignments do not create impossible overlaps.
7. Preserve schedule versions, if available, to distinguish the original schedule from later revisions.

The schedule history is important: actual completion versus due date measures delivery performance, but true **schedule adherence** requires knowing the planned start, planned finish, and planned sequence as they existed at each schedule revision.

### C. Reconstruct two complementary process views

**Job-centric view**

For every job, reconstruct:

- Release
- Queue entry at each operation
- Setup start/end
- Processing start/end
- Transfer or blocking
- Rework and route deviation
- Completion

Process discovery methods such as directly-follows graphs, Inductive Miner models, and performance-annotated Petri nets can reveal actual routes and loops. Variant clustering should be used instead of one large “spaghetti” model, grouping jobs by product family, complexity, routing, or material.

**Resource-centric view**

For every machine, sort operation and setup intervals chronologically to reconstruct the actual sequence:

\[
\ldots \rightarrow JOB\text{-}6998 \rightarrow setup \rightarrow JOB\text{-}7001 \rightarrow \ldots
\]

This view is essential for sequence-dependent setup analysis, utilization, contention, and identifying avoidable machine idle periods.

Conformance checking—token replay or alignment against the planned routing—can identify:

- Skipped operations
- Unplanned rework
- Use of nonstandard machines
- Operations performed in the wrong sequence
- Jobs that remain in queues without valid routing progress

## 1.2 Performance measures

### A. Job flow time, lead time, and makespan

Definitions should be standardized:

\[
\text{Shop-floor flow time}_j = C_j-R_j
\]

where \(R_j\) is release time and \(C_j\) is production completion.

If order receipt is available:

\[
\text{Customer lead time}_j = C_j-O_j
\]

where \(O_j\) is order acceptance time.

Flow time should be decomposed into:

\[
\text{Flow time}
=
\text{processing}
+\text{setup}
+\text{queueing}
+\text{transport}
+\text{blocking}
+\text{downtime delay}
\]

For every job family and priority class, report:

- Mean, median, 75th, 90th, and 95th percentile
- Standard deviation and coefficient of variation
- Distribution by routing length and complexity
- Percentage of total flow time spent processing versus waiting
- Conditional distributions for on-time and late jobs

“Makespan” can be measured in two ways:

- **Job route makespan:** first operation start to final operation end
- **Production-horizon makespan:** completion time of the last job in a daily, weekly, or campaign cohort

Performance spectrum and dotted-chart analyses can expose periods when many jobs progress slowly or become synchronized into WIP waves.

### B. Queue and waiting times

For operation \(o\):

\[
W_{jo} =
\text{Setup or processing start}_{jo}
-
\text{Queue entry}_{jo}
\]

Waiting should be analyzed by:

- Machine and work center
- Shift and day of week
- Queue length at arrival
- Priority
- Part family and routing
- Downstream or upstream congestion
- Machine state at arrival
- Breakdown periods
- Setup family of the preceding job

Useful measures include:

- Mean and percentile queue time
- Percentage of jobs waiting beyond a threshold
- Queue-time contribution to overall lateness
- Time-weighted average and maximum queue length
- Arrival-to-service variability
- Aging of the oldest waiting operation

WIP at time \(t\) is the number of released but incomplete jobs or operations. The time integral of WIP provides average WIP, and Little’s Law can be used as a consistency check:

\[
\overline{WIP} \approx \text{Throughput rate}\times \text{Average flow time}
\]

### C. Resource utilization

For each machine \(m\), partition the scheduled calendar into mutually exclusive states:

- Productive processing
- Setup
- Planned maintenance
- Unplanned breakdown
- Blocked
- Idle while eligible work exists
- Starved because no eligible work is available

Then calculate:

\[
U_m^{prod} =
\frac{\text{productive processing time}}
{\text{scheduled available time}}
\]

\[
U_m^{setup} =
\frac{\text{setup time}}
{\text{scheduled available time}}
\]

\[
U_m^{occupied} =
\frac{\text{processing}+\text{setup}}
{\text{scheduled available time}}
\]

Idle time needs interpretation. A machine with no work because an upstream bottleneck failed is starved; a machine with an eligible queue but no assignment indicates a dispatching, labor, tooling, or data issue.

Equivalent calculations should be performed for operators, while accounting for operators who supervise multiple machines concurrently.

### D. Sequence-dependent setup analysis

For every setup on machine \(m\), associate:

- Previous job and setup family \(a\)
- Next job and setup family \(b\)
- Material, tooling, dimensions, and process parameters
- Operator and shift
- Length of the preceding idle period
- Actual setup duration

This produces a transition-level setup matrix:

\[
\hat{s}_{m,a,b}
=
E[S \mid m,\text{previous family}=a,\text{next family}=b]
\]

The analysis should report:

- Mean and percentile setup time for each \(a\rightarrow b\) transition
- Variability and sample size
- Same-family versus cross-family setup differences
- Operator and shift effects
- Transitions associated with frequent setup overruns
- Total setup time attributable to actual job sequencing

Because high-mix environments produce sparse transition data, raw averages are insufficient. A hierarchical or regularized prediction model can combine machine, material, tooling, and job-feature effects, allowing estimates for rare or unseen transitions.

The observed sequence can also be compared with a feasible setup-minimizing sequence for the same jobs. The difference estimates the **avoidable setup burden** rather than merely reporting high setup time.

### E. Schedule adherence and tardiness

For job \(j\):

\[
L_j=C_j-D_j
\]

\[
T_j=\max(0,C_j-D_j)
\]

where \(D_j\) is the due date, \(L_j\) is lateness, and \(T_j\) is tardiness.

Track:

- Percentage of jobs completed on time
- Mean and median tardiness
- 90th and 95th percentile tardiness
- Maximum tardiness
- Weighted tardiness:

\[
\sum_j w_j T_j
\]

where \(w_j\) reflects contractual priority or penalty

- Tardiness by customer, route, family, and priority
- Early completion, since excessive earliness may increase finished-goods inventory

If schedule snapshots exist, operation-level adherence should include:

- Actual minus planned start
- Actual minus planned completion
- Sequence adherence on each machine
- Number and magnitude of schedule revisions
- Schedule stability or “nervousness”

Machine sequence adherence can be measured using rank correlation or the number of inversions between planned and actual sequences.

### F. Disruption impact

Breakdowns, urgent jobs, and priority changes should be connected to affected jobs through the resource and temporal views.

For a breakdown:

1. Identify interrupted operations.
2. Identify jobs waiting for the failed machine.
3. Trace downstream jobs and machines that became starved.
4. Compare predicted completion immediately before the event with eventual completion.
5. Measure recovery time—the interval until queues and throughput return to normal.

For urgent-job insertion:

- Measure the hot job’s lead-time benefit.
- Identify displaced jobs.
- Quantify additional setup, tardiness, and schedule changes caused to those jobs.
- Determine whether reserved capacity would have reduced disruption.

Event studies, matched historical periods, and difference-in-differences analyses can compare similar workload conditions with and without disruptions. A no-disruption simulation provides a stronger counterfactual. Process-mining associations alone should not be treated as causal without such controls.

# 2. Diagnosing Scheduling Pathologies

The diagnostics should identify not only where delays occur, but also whether they are avoidable and how they propagate through the system.

## 2.1 Bottleneck and shifting-bottleneck analysis

A bottleneck is not simply the machine with the highest average utilization. Evidence should combine:

- Sustained high productive-plus-setup utilization
- High and growing queues
- Long waiting-time contribution to job lead time
- High workload-to-capacity ratio in due-date windows
- Starvation of downstream resources
- Throughput sensitivity when the resource loses capacity
- Frequency with which the resource lies on the realized critical path of late jobs

Time-window analysis may reveal a **shifting bottleneck**: for example, milling during one product mix and heat treatment during another.

The impact can be quantified by attributing each job’s delay to waiting intervals. If 40% of late-job flow time is consistently spent waiting for a particular machine family, that is stronger evidence than utilization alone.

## 2.2 Poor prioritization and priority inversions

The log can reveal cases where:

- A low-priority job starts while a high-priority eligible job waits.
- A job with negative slack is delayed behind jobs with ample slack.
- Jobs with fewer routing alternatives wait while flexible jobs occupy specialized machines.
- Hot jobs are inserted without considering the tardiness imposed on displaced jobs.

At every dispatch decision, reconstruct the eligible queue and compare the selected job with alternatives. Calculate:

- Percentage of priority inversions
- Extra waiting imposed on urgent or near-due jobs
- Difference in tardiness risk between selected and bypassed jobs
- Aging and starvation of repeatedly bypassed jobs

Variant analysis should compare on-time and late jobs after controlling for route length, workload, due-date tightness, and job complexity. Otherwise, late jobs may simply appear different because they are inherently more difficult.

## 2.3 Suboptimal setup sequencing

Evidence includes:

- Frequent alternation between setup families
- High-cost transitions followed shortly by a reversal
- Same-family jobs waiting while an expensive cross-family setup is performed
- Excessive setup share on the bottleneck
- Setup time exceeding the processing time saved by prioritizing a particular job

For each historical queue snapshot, a constrained resequencing calculation can estimate the minimum feasible setup time while respecting due-date and priority guardrails. The gap between actual and benchmark setup time measures scheduling inefficiency.

## 2.4 Downstream starvation and upstream blocking

A downstream machine is starved when it is available but has no eligible operation because upstream work has not arrived. Process mining can link the idle interval to upstream queues, breakdowns, or dispatch choices.

Conversely, an operation may be complete but unable to transfer because downstream storage or capacity is unavailable. This creates blocking and may reduce effective capacity upstream.

Useful evidence includes:

- Starved hours by resource
- Upstream event responsible for each starvation episode
- Correlation between upstream batch releases and downstream idle/busy cycles
- Percentage of machine idle time occurring despite high total shop WIP

High total WIP combined with starvation at critical machines is a strong signal that work is in the wrong place, not that the shop lacks work.

## 2.5 WIP amplification and workload waves

Local rules can create a manufacturing bullwhip effect:

1. A work center releases a large group of jobs.
2. The downstream center becomes overloaded.
3. Jobs accumulate and age.
4. That center later releases another large wave.
5. The next resource alternates between starvation and overload.

This can be detected through:

- Time-series plots of arrivals, departures, and WIP by work center
- Coefficients of variation of WIP and release quantities
- Cross-correlation and lag analysis between neighboring work centers
- Queue-length control charts
- Dotted charts showing synchronized job movement

## 2.6 Additional likely pathologies

Process mining may also expose:

- Imbalance among technically interchangeable machines
- Excessive rework or route deviations
- Waiting for operators, tools, or material rather than machines
- Premature release of jobs that cannot progress through the bottleneck
- Local utilization maximization that increases global flow time
- Excessive schedule churn following every disruption

These findings should be reported as hypotheses supported by quantified evidence, since the hypothetical snippet alone is insufficient to conclude which pathologies actually dominate.

# 3. Root Cause Analysis of Scheduling Ineffectiveness

## 3.1 Likely root causes

### Static local dispatching in a dynamic system

FCFS and local EDD do not account for:

- Remaining routing and processing time
- Current and future bottleneck queues
- Alternative machine eligibility
- Sequence-dependent setup cost
- Breakdown risk
- Downstream congestion
- The impact of a local choice on total job tardiness

EDD can prioritize a near-due job whose remaining route is short while ignoring another job that must still pass through two congested bottlenecks. FCFS protects fairness but may process jobs with abundant slack before jobs at high lateness risk.

### Inadequate real-time state visibility

If dispatchers lack accurate information about machine state, queue length, tooling, operator availability, and job progress, decisions are made from stale assumptions. A schedule may assign work to a machine that is down or to an operation whose material is not ready.

### Inaccurate duration and setup estimates

Using one deterministic standard time for a highly variable task causes:

- Unrealistic completion projections
- Overloaded machine schedules
- False slack calculations
- Frequent schedule revisions
- Systematic underestimation for difficult families, shifts, or operators

Setup estimates may be especially inaccurate if they ignore the preceding job.

### Poor coordination among work centers

Local optimization can cause one center to maximize utilization by producing jobs that the downstream center cannot accept. The result is higher WIP without higher throughput.

### Weak disruption response

Unplanned events may trigger ad hoc expediting rather than structured rescheduling. Hot jobs can receive absolute priority without evaluating displaced tardiness or setup penalties. Breakdown recovery may not consider alternate machines, preventive maintenance risk, or downstream consequences.

## 3.2 Separating logic, capacity, and variability problems

Process mining should be combined with workload analysis and counterfactual optimization.

### A. Scheduling-logic gap

Replay the same historical job arrivals, capacities, durations, and disruptions under:

- Actual dispatching
- An optimized or improved dispatching policy

If tardiness and setup time improve substantially without adding capacity, the dominant issue is scheduling logic.

Examples of scheduling evidence include:

- Idle machine while eligible work exists
- Avoidable setup transitions
- Priority inversions
- Poor allocation among alternative machines
- High WIP despite non-bottleneck capacity

### B. Capacity gap

Calculate effective workload-to-capacity ratios by machine and due-date window, including setup and downtime:

\[
\rho_m =
\frac{\text{required processing and setup workload}}
{\text{effective available capacity}}
\]

If \(\rho_m\) persistently approaches or exceeds 1, queues are structurally unavoidable. If even a finite-capacity optimized schedule cannot meet due dates, capacity, staffing, maintenance, or due-date acceptance policies must change.

### C. Variability gap

Compare simulations with:

1. Deterministic median durations and no failures
2. Stochastic task durations
3. Stochastic durations plus breakdowns
4. Full model including urgent jobs

The KPI degradation between these scenarios estimates the impact of variability and disruption.

Mixed-effects or variance-decomposition models can further separate duration variation attributable to:

- Job family
- Machine
- Operator
- Shift
- Material
- Setup transition
- Random residual variation

A practical decomposition is:

- **Logic gap:** baseline versus improved policy on identical instances
- **Capacity gap:** current capacity versus added capacity under the same optimized policy
- **Variability gap:** deterministic versus stochastic operation times
- **Disruption gap:** normal versus breakdown/hot-job scenarios

This prevents unnecessary capital purchases when better scheduling would solve the problem, while also preventing unrealistic expectations from scheduling when capacity is genuinely insufficient.

# 4. Advanced Data-Driven Scheduling Strategies

The strategies below are complementary. A strong target architecture would combine dynamic dispatching, predictive finite-capacity scheduling, setup optimization, and controlled job release.

## Strategy 1: Multi-factor dynamic dispatching

### Core logic

At each machine availability or queue-change event, rank only operations that are truly eligible: predecessors complete, material and tooling available, operator available, and machine qualified.

For eligible job \(j\) on machine \(m\), estimate:

- Due-date slack
- Predicted remaining processing and queue time
- Probability of late completion
- Customer priority or penalty
- Sequence-dependent setup from the current machine state
- Downstream congestion
- Whether the job feeds a bottleneck
- Routing flexibility
- Current waiting age

An interpretable score could be:

\[
Score_{jm} =
\alpha P_j
+\beta Pr(C_j>D_j)
+\gamma B_j
+\delta A_j
-\eta \hat{s}_{prev,j,m}
-\theta Q^{downstream}_j
-\kappa F_j
\]

where:

- \(P_j\): priority or penalty weight
- \(Pr(C_j>D_j)\): predicted lateness risk
- \(B_j\): urgency to feed a bottleneck
- \(A_j\): aging term to prevent starvation
- \(\hat{s}_{prev,j,m}\): expected setup time
- \(Q^{downstream}_j\): downstream congestion
- \(F_j\): penalty for consuming a specialized resource when flexible alternatives exist

An Apparent Tardiness Cost with Setups formulation can also be used as an interpretable starting point:

\[
ATCS_j =
\frac{w_j}{p_j}
\exp\left(
-\frac{\max(slack_j,0)}{k_1\bar p}
\right)
\exp\left(
-\frac{s_{prev,j}}{k_2\bar s}
\right)
\]

It should be extended with predicted remaining-route time and downstream load rather than relying only on the current operation.

### Process-mining inputs

- Learned waiting-time and remaining-cycle-time models
- Empirical setup transition model
- Bottleneck and starvation analysis
- Historical priority-inversion patterns
- Machine eligibility and alternative-routing behavior
- Congestion and breakdown states

Weights should be tuned through simulation or Bayesian optimization, not selected solely from observational correlations. Different weights may be appropriate for bottleneck and non-bottleneck machines.

### Pathologies addressed

- Poor local prioritization
- High-priority job delays
- Excessive setups
- Downstream overload
- Starvation of critical resources
- Repeated bypassing of older jobs

### Expected impact

- Lower weighted tardiness and fewer extreme delays
- More predictable lead times
- Lower setup burden than EDD alone
- Better flow to bottlenecks
- Moderate WIP reduction

Guardrails should include maximum waiting time, priority protection, and limits on schedule churn.

## Strategy 2: Predictive rolling-horizon finite-capacity scheduling

### Core logic

Create a rolling schedule over an operational horizon—for example, the next shift to several days—using CP-SAT, mixed-integer programming, constraint programming, or a suitable scheduling heuristic.

The model should include:

- Operation precedence
- Machine eligibility and alternatives
- Machine and operator calendars
- Sequence-dependent setup
- Non-preemptive or preemptive operation constraints
- Maintenance windows
- Tooling and labor constraints
- Release dates and due dates
- Hot-job reserve capacity
- Started-work and frozen-horizon constraints

A multi-objective function can minimize:

\[
\sum_j w_jT_j
+\lambda_1\sum setup
+\lambda_2\sum flow\ time
+\lambda_3 WIP
+\lambda_4 overtime
+\lambda_5 schedule\ changes
\]

The schedule should be recalculated when:

- An operation finishes materially early or late
- A breakdown occurs
- A hot job arrives
- Lateness risk crosses a threshold
- A bottleneck queue deviates significantly from forecast

A short freeze window prevents constant resequencing of work already staged or about to begin.

### Predictive models

Task-duration models should produce distributions or quantiles rather than only averages. Predictors can include:

- Machine and operation
- Material and dimensions
- Quantity and complexity
- Job family
- Operator and shift
- Tool condition
- Previous setup family
- Historical rework risk

Machine-failure models can estimate failure hazard, mean time to repair, and likely downtime by machine condition or usage. If sensor data are unavailable, MES breakdown histories can still provide empirical hazard and repair-time models.

Scenario-based robust scheduling then evaluates each candidate schedule across multiple sampled duration and breakdown scenarios. Jobs with high penalty can be scheduled with risk buffers based on upper quantiles rather than means.

### Process-mining inputs

- Conditional processing-time distributions
- Actual route variants
- Rework probabilities
- Setup transition distributions
- Failure and repair patterns
- Queue-delay models
- Schedule-adherence history

### Pathologies addressed

- Unrealistic deterministic plans
- Poor coordination between work centers
- Reactive rather than anticipatory rescheduling
- Failure to account for bottleneck risk
- Excessive schedule instability

### Expected impact

- Higher due-date reliability
- Lower lead-time variance
- Earlier warning of infeasible due dates
- Better machine-load balance
- More controlled responses to breakdowns and urgent jobs

---

## Strategy 3: Setup-family batching and optimized sequencing

### Core logic

Use the mined setup-transition matrix to form setup families and optimize the sequence at setup-intensive or bottleneck machines.

Within a rolling queue:

1. Cluster jobs by material, tooling, fixture, geometry, or operating parameters.
2. Estimate transition setup cost for each possible job pair.
3. Sequence jobs using CP-SAT, dynamic programming, or a traveling-salesperson-style heuristic.
4. Impose due-date and maximum-wait constraints.
5. Reoptimize when a high-risk job or disruption arrives.

The objective is not pure setup minimization. It should balance setup cost and tardiness:

\[
\min
\left[
\sum s_{i,j,m}
+\mu\sum w_jT_j
+\nu\sum waiting_j
\right]
\]

Dynamic batching guardrails are essential:

- Do not wait indefinitely for a larger family batch.
- Release a batch when the oldest job reaches an age limit.
- Break a family campaign if another job faces unacceptable lateness risk.
- Reserve emergency insertion points for genuinely urgent work.

### Process-mining inputs

- Transition-specific setup times
- Setup variance and confidence intervals
- Frequency of family changes
- Due-date consequences of historical batching
- Operator and shift effects
- Common high-cost transitions

The analysis can also identify setup tasks suitable for pre-staging or external preparation, such as gathering tools while the previous operation is running.

### Pathologies addressed

- Excessive changeovers
- Avoidable setup consumption at bottlenecks
- Capacity loss caused by poor sequencing
- Frequent sequence reversals

### Expected impact

- Lower total setup time
- Higher effective bottleneck capacity
- Higher throughput
- Reduced queue time where setup is a major capacity consumer

Poorly controlled batching can increase WIP and tardiness, so setup optimization must remain subordinate to due-date risk.

---

## Strategy 4: Bottleneck-driven release and WIP control

### Core logic

Introduce a CONWIP or Drum-Buffer-Rope-style release policy.

- The current bottleneck provides the **drum** schedule.
- A time or job buffer protects the bottleneck from starvation.
- New jobs are released only when WIP tokens are available or when projected bottleneck supply falls below target.
- Non-bottleneck resources are scheduled to support bottleneck flow rather than maximize local utilization.

The bottleneck and buffer targets can change dynamically as product mix changes.

Hot jobs should consume reserved emergency capacity or explicitly displace a lower-penalty job, rather than entering the system without WIP consequences.

### Process-mining inputs

- Time-varying bottleneck identification
- WIP and lead-time relationship
- Starvation and blocking episodes
- Queue-time distributions
- Route-specific bottleneck arrival times
- Historical effects of release surges

### Pathologies addressed

- Excessive premature job release
- WIP waves
- Long and unpredictable queues
- Bottleneck starvation despite high shop WIP
- Local utilization optimization

### Expected impact

- Significant WIP reduction
- Shorter and more stable lead times
- Better bottleneck protection
- More reliable completion estimates

Some non-bottleneck utilization may decline. That is acceptable if throughput and due-date performance improve; maximizing every machine’s utilization is not a valid system-level objective.

# 5. Simulation, Evaluation, and Continuous Improvement

## 5.1 Process-mining-calibrated discrete-event simulation

A discrete-event simulation should reproduce:

- Job arrivals and release patterns
- Actual routing distributions and rework loops
- Machine alternatives and qualification rules
- Conditional processing-time distributions
- Sequence-dependent setup
- Operator, tool, and shift constraints
- Transfer and blocking behavior
- Planned maintenance
- Breakdown frequency and repair duration
- Hot-job arrivals and priority changes
- Existing and proposed scheduling logic

Correlations should be preserved. For example, a complex job may have long durations at several operations; independently sampling each task would understate job-level variability.

### Validation

Before testing alternatives, validate the model against a held-out historical period:

- Throughput
- WIP by work center
- Queue-time distributions
- Machine-state percentages
- Setup hours
- Flow-time distribution
- Tardiness
- Breakdown recovery patterns

Historical replay is useful: feed the simulator the actual job arrivals and disruption times, then confirm that the baseline dispatching policy approximately reproduces observed outcomes.

## 5.2 Experimental scenarios

Compare the current rules, each proposed strategy, and combined strategies under:

1. **Normal historical demand**
2. **High load:** for example, 90%, 100%, and 110% of effective capacity
3. **Tighter due dates**
4. **Higher product-mix complexity**
5. **Increased hot-job frequency**
6. **Frequent short breakdowns**
7. **Rare but long bottleneck outages**
8. **Higher duration variability**
9. **Operator absenteeism or reduced shifts**
10. **Alternative-machine or added-capacity scenarios**
11. **Changes in setup-family mix**
12. **Peak customer-priority periods**

Use multiple replications and common random numbers so that policies are compared under the same stochastic conditions. Report confidence intervals, not only point estimates.

### Evaluation KPIs

- On-time delivery rate
- Mean and 95th-percentile tardiness
- Weighted tardiness
- Mean and 95th-percentile lead time
- Average and peak WIP
- Throughput
- Setup hours and setup share at bottlenecks
- Productive, setup, idle, breakdown, and starved time
- Hot-job performance and displaced-job penalty
- Schedule changes per shift
- Overtime
- Fairness and maximum waiting time

The result should be a Pareto analysis. For example, one strategy may minimize tardiness but create excessive schedule churn, while another provides slightly less improvement with much greater operational stability.

## 5.3 Deployment approach

A controlled rollout should follow:

1. **Shadow mode:** Generate recommendations without controlling the shop.
2. **Dispatcher review:** Record accepted and rejected recommendations and reasons.
3. **Pilot:** Deploy on one bottleneck or product family.
4. **Guarded automation:** Automate low-risk decisions with manual override.
5. **Scale-up:** Extend to the rest of the shop after KPI validation.

Schedule versions, overrides, and override reasons should be logged as new process-mining data.

## 5.4 Continuous monitoring and adaptation

Create an ongoing event pipeline from the MES to an object-centric process-mining and scheduling layer.

### Operational monitoring

Daily or shift-level dashboards should track:

- Due-date risk and predicted completion
- Queue length and aging
- WIP by route stage
- Bottleneck utilization and starvation
- Setup transitions
- Breakdown status
- Schedule adherence
- Hot-job displacement effects

### Process-conformance monitoring

Automatically detect:

- New routing variants
- Unexpected rework
- Operations executed on nonstandard machines
- Setup events missing from the log
- Long inactive periods
- Increased dispatcher overrides
- Deviations between planned and actual sequences

### Statistical and model-drift monitoring

Use control charts, CUSUM, change-point detection, population-stability measures, or distribution tests to detect changes in:

- Processing-time distributions
- Setup-transition times
- Product mix
- Arrival rate
- Breakdown frequency and repair duration
- Queue-time relationships
- Prediction calibration
- Bottleneck location

Trigger retraining or policy retuning when drift exceeds defined thresholds.

### Champion–challenger management

Maintain:

- A production “champion” scheduling policy
- One or more challenger policies evaluated in simulation and shadow mode
- Versioned data, models, weights, and constraints
- Periodic reoptimization of dispatch weights and WIP caps
- Human approval for material policy changes

## Overall recommendation

The strongest target solution is a layered system:

1. **Process mining** supplies accurate state reconstruction, bottleneck diagnosis, setup models, and predictive features.
2. **Predictive rolling-horizon scheduling** creates a coordinated finite-capacity plan.
3. **Dynamic dispatching** handles minute-to-minute deviations.
4. **Setup-aware sequencing** protects bottleneck capacity.
5. **Controlled release** limits WIP and stabilizes flow.
6. **Discrete-event simulation** validates changes before deployment.
7. **Continuous process mining** detects drift and closes the improvement loop.

This approach shifts Precision Parts Inc. from local, reactive dispatching to a system-wide, predictive, and adaptive scheduling capability while preserving the flexibility required in a high-mix, low-volume job shop.
