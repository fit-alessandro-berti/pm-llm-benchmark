## 1. Queue Identification and Characterization

### 1.1 Prepare and validate the event log

Before calculating queues, the event log should be converted into activity instances, with one row per activity occurrence:

- Visit/case ID
- Activity and specialty
- Start timestamp
- Completion timestamp
- Resource, room, or equipment
- Patient type and urgency
- Ideally: appointment time, actual arrival time, cancellation/no-show status, and staff schedules

For repeated activities, such as a second doctor review after an ECG, each occurrence needs a sequence number so that START and COMPLETE events are paired correctly.

Data-quality checks should include:

- Missing START or COMPLETE events
- Completion before start
- Duplicate events
- Clock or time-zone inconsistencies
- Implausibly long service times
- Visits spanning multiple days
- Activities recorded under inconsistent names
- Overlapping activities that may be valid, such as laboratory processing while another activity occurs

These issues should be flagged rather than automatically “corrected,” since a negative inter-activity time may indicate legitimate parallel work rather than bad data.

### 1.2 Define service time and waiting time

For an activity \(i\):

\[
ServiceTime_i = Complete_i - Start_i
\]

For consecutive activities \(A\) and \(B\) in the same patient visit:

\[
InterActivityDelay_{A \rightarrow B} = Start_B - Complete_A
\]

For example, using visit V1001:

- Registration completes at 09:08:45
- Nurse Assessment starts at 09:15:20

Therefore:

\[
Wait_{Registration \rightarrow Nurse} = 6\text{ minutes }35\text{ seconds}
\]

Similarly:

- Nurse Assessment completes at 09:25:10
- Doctor Consultation starts at 09:45:55

The observed delay is 20 minutes 45 seconds.

This is an **observed inter-stage waiting time**. It is not always a pure queueing delay. It may include:

- Walking or transport time
- Clinical preparation
- Waiting for results or prerequisites
- Handover delay
- Intentional observation
- Actual waiting for a scarce resource

The event log alone can identify where delays occur, but interpreting them as queues requires understanding when the patient became “ready” for the next activity.

If activity \(B\) requires multiple predecessors, such as both nurse assessment and blood results, its ready time should be:

\[
ReadyTime_B = \max(Complete_{required\ predecessors})
\]

Then:

\[
QueueTime_B = Start_B - ReadyTime_B
\]

This avoids treating time spent completing a legitimate prerequisite as queueing.

For negative values caused by overlapping activities, I would determine whether the overlap is valid. I would not simply convert all negative values to zero without investigation.

### 1.3 Visit-level decomposition

For each visit, calculate:

- **Total visit duration:** arrival or first activity start to check-out completion
- **Total service/touch time:** sum of activity service times, adjusted for overlap
- **Total observed waiting time:** sum of valid inter-stage waits
- **Flow efficiency:**

\[
FlowEfficiency = \frac{TouchTime}{TotalVisitDuration}
\]

A low flow-efficiency value indicates that most of the patient’s visit is non-service time.

If actual patient arrival time is unavailable, the pre-registration wait cannot be measured reliably. Likewise, if the log ends at check-out completion, post-care administrative delays are outside the measured process.

### 1.4 Queue-level metrics

For each transition, such as Registration  Nurse or Doctor  ECG, and for each destination activity, I would calculate:

| Metric | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Mean waiting time | Overall average delay, but sensitive to outliers |
| Median waiting time | Typical patient experience |
| 75th, 90th, and 95th percentiles | Tail performance and reliability |
| Maximum wait | Extreme cases requiring investigation |
| Standard deviation/IQR | Variability and predictability |
| Queue frequency | Number and percentage of visits using the transition |
| Excessive-wait rate | Percentage exceeding a clinically or operationally defined threshold |
| Total waiting hours | Wait multiplied by volume; measures total burden |
| Contribution to visit duration | Percentage of total visit delay attributable to the queue |
| Queue length over time | Patients ready but not yet started |
| Arrival and service rates | Demand-capacity relationship |
| Overtaking/priority rate | Whether later arrivals are served before earlier ones |
| Abandonment or incomplete-visit rate | If captured |

A retrospective queue-length estimate for activity \(B\) can be constructed as:

\[
Q_B(t) = \#\{patients: ReadyTime_B \leq t < StartTime_B\}
\]

Queue metrics should be segmented by:

- Specialty and physician
- Day of week and time of day
- New versus follow-up patients
- Urgency
- Appointment type
- Diagnostic pathway
- Resource, room, and equipment
- Scheduled versus walk-in visits
- On-time versus late arrivals, if available

### 1.5 Identify the most critical queues

Critical queues should not be selected solely by the longest mean. A queue with a 45-minute average affecting 20 patients may have less organizational impact than a 15-minute wait affecting 5,000 patients.

I would prioritize queues using four dimensions:

1. **Magnitude:** median and p90 waiting time
2. **Volume:** number and percentage of visits affected
3. **Total burden:** total patient waiting hours
4. **Clinical and experiential risk:** urgency, missed tests, abandonment, or delayed treatment

A practical priority score could combine normalized measures:

\[
PriorityScore =
w_1(TotalWaitHours) +
w_2(P90Wait) +
w_3(SLAFailureRate) +
w_4(ClinicalRisk) +
w_5(PatientVolume)
\]

The final selection should include:

- High-volume queues that dominate total patient waiting
- Long-tail queues with severe p90/p95 delays
- Queues disproportionately affecting urgent or vulnerable patients
- Queues that trigger downstream delays, such as late doctor consultations causing missed diagnostic slots

A Pareto chart of waiting hours by transition often reveals the small number of queues responsible for most of the total delay.

## 2. Root Cause Analysis

### 2.1 Resource bottlenecks

Potential problems include:

- Too few nurses during the morning arrival peak
- A specialist who is available only during limited periods
- Diagnostic rooms or equipment shared across specialties
- Staff breaks coinciding with peak demand
- Physicians scheduled continuously without allowances for complex cases
- A resource being technically available but blocked by room unavailability

Resource utilization should be inferred from service intervals and, where possible, compared with rostered availability:

\[
Utilization_r =
\frac{BusyTime_r}{AvailableScheduledTime_r}
\]

Interpretation is important:

- **High queue and high utilization:** likely capacity bottleneck
- **High queue and low apparent utilization:** likely coordination, batching, room, skill, or data problem
- **High utilization and low queue:** efficient but potentially fragile
- **Long queues only at certain hours:** time-specific demand-capacity mismatch

Maximum utilization is not the objective. Queues tend to increase rapidly as utilization approaches 100%, especially when arrivals and service times are variable.

### 2.2 Activity dependencies and handovers

A patient may finish nursing assessment but not be communicated as ready for the doctor. Other examples include:

- Doctor waiting for nurse documentation
- Technician waiting for an order to be entered
- Patient physically waiting in one area while the receiving department is unaware
- Results completed but not routed to the appropriate specialist
- Rooms not marked clean or available

A performance-annotated process map can separate:

- Time spent in activities
- Time spent between activities
- Resource handoffs
- Rework or backtracking

If long delays occur mainly across handoffs rather than within activities, adding capacity may not solve the problem.

### 2.3 Variability in service times

Average service time can hide substantial variation. New patients may require 30-minute consultations while follow-ups require 10 minutes. Mixing them into identical appointment slots causes delays to accumulate.

For each activity, calculate service-time distributions and coefficients of variation:

\[
CV = \frac{StandardDeviation}{Mean}
\]

Analyze whether long service times are associated with:

- New versus follow-up patients
- Specific specialties or clinicians
- Urgency
- Additional diagnostics
- Certain times of day
- Particular process variants
- Rework or missing information

A highly variable service-time distribution often creates long queue tails even when average capacity appears adequate.

### 2.4 Appointment scheduling policies

Potential issues include:

- Many patients booked at the top of the hour
- Identical slot lengths for clinically different visit types
- No allowance for urgent add-ons
- Separate scheduling of diagnostics that does not align with the consultation
- Overbooking based on an assumed no-show rate that is not time- or patient-specific
- Physicians beginning clinic later than the first appointment
- Complex patients concentrated in the same session

Testing these causes requires appointment time, booking type, no-show, cancellation, and lateness data. If these data are not currently in the event log, they should be joined from the scheduling system.

Useful measures include:

- Scheduled time versus actual arrival
- Scheduled time versus consultation start
- Number of simultaneous arrivals per time interval
- Slot duration versus actual service-time distribution
- Backlog carried from one appointment slot to the next

### 2.5 Patient arrival patterns

Demand should be analyzed in 15- or 30-minute intervals. Relevant patterns include:

- Morning arrival spikes
- Patients arriving much earlier than scheduled
- Walk-in surges
- Monday or post-holiday peaks
- Seasonal demand
- Late-arriving patients being inserted into an already full queue

Queue-length heat maps can compare:

- Patients becoming ready for service
- Activities started
- Resources actively working
- Backlog at the end of each interval

This distinguishes insufficient daily capacity from capacity available at the wrong times.

### 2.6 Patient and pathway differences

Variant analysis can identify common paths such as:

- Registration  Nurse  Doctor  Check-out
- Registration  Nurse  Doctor  ECG  Doctor Review  Check-out
- Registration  Blood Test  Doctor  X-Ray  Review  Check-out

For each variant, calculate frequency, total duration, waiting time, and rework. Long duration may result from the legitimate complexity of a variant, while excessive waiting within the variant indicates an operational problem.

Comparisons should be risk-adjusted. New, urgent, or clinically complex patients should not be judged against simple follow-up visits without controlling for case mix.

### 2.7 Process-mining methods to pinpoint causes

I would use:

1. **Directly-follows and process discovery maps**  
   Display transition frequency and mean/p90 delays.

2. **Performance overlays**  
   Color activities by service time and edges by waiting time.

3. **Variant analysis**  
   Compare common, fast, slow, and rework-heavy pathways.

4. **Resource and social-network analysis**  
   Identify overloaded resources, repeated handovers, and resources associated with unusually long delays.

5. **Conformance checking**  
   Detect departures from intended pathways, skipped steps, unnecessary repeats, and incorrect sequencing.

6. **Queue reconstruction**  
   Estimate queue lengths, arrival rates, service rates, overtaking, and periods of backlog accumulation.

7. **Cohort and regression analysis**  
   Estimate which factors predict long waits while controlling for specialty, patient type, time, resource, and complexity.

8. **Simulation or digital-twin analysis**  
   Test staffing and scheduling alternatives before implementation.

The analysis establishes strong operational associations, but causal claims should be validated through controlled pilots or phased implementation.

## 3. Data-Driven Optimization Strategies

Actual percentage reductions should be estimated from the clinic’s baseline distributions and simulation. The values below are reasonable pilot targets, not guaranteed outcomes.

### Strategy 1: Align staffing and resource schedules with time-varying demand

**Targets:**  
Registration  Nurse, Nurse  Doctor, and queues for ECG, X-ray, or blood collection during identified peak periods.

**Root cause addressed:**  
Capacity is available over the day but does not align with when patients become ready. Breaks, shift boundaries, or specialist availability may coincide with peak demand.

**Data support:**  

- Queue-length heat maps show when backlog begins and clears.
- Resource calendars show whether staff are active during those periods.
- Arrival and service-rate analysis quantifies the size and duration of the mismatch.
- Resource pooling analysis shows whether another unit has idle cross-trained staff.

**Actions:**

- Shift selected staff start and break times by 30–60 minutes.
- Use staggered nurse and clerk schedules.
- Cross-train clerks for registration and check-out.
- Create a small flex role during predictable peaks.
- Pool equivalent diagnostic rooms or nursing staff instead of assigning them rigidly to individual physicians.
- Reserve limited “surge” capacity for urgent cases.

**Expected impact:**  
A simulation might target a 20–30% reduction in p90 waits during peak intervals without adding full-time positions. The exact target should be accepted only if simulated throughput, overtime, and care-quality constraints remain satisfactory.

---

### Strategy 2: Redesign appointment templates by visit type and variability

**Targets:**  
Nurse  Doctor and Doctor  Diagnostic Test queues, particularly in specialties with accumulating delay later in the session.

**Root cause addressed:**  

- Identical slots for new and follow-up visits
- Appointment bunching
- Overbooking
- No buffer for complex or urgent patients
- Diagnostic demand not coordinated with consultation schedules

**Data support:**  

- Service-time distributions by patient type show appropriate slot needs.
- Delay curves show whether the clinic progressively falls behind.
- Appointment-versus-start analysis identifies chronic lateness.
- Variant analysis estimates the probability that each appointment type will need ECG, X-ray, or laboratory work.

**Actions:**

- Use different templates for new, follow-up, procedure, and complex visits.
- Avoid booking many patients at the same time.
- Distribute complex visits through the session.
- Place a short recovery buffer after a block with highly variable service times.
- Reserve urgent add-on slots rather than interrupting every scheduled queue.
- Coordinate likely diagnostic demand with available diagnostic capacity.
- Use no-show prediction cautiously to determine overbooking rather than applying one rate to all patients.

**Expected impact:**  
The objective could be a 15–25% reduction in doctor-start p90 delay and a reduction in the percentage of sessions ending more than a specified number of minutes late. A simulation should verify that improved punctuality does not reduce throughput excessively.

---

### Strategy 3: Segment and streamline registration and check-out

**Targets:**  
Arrival  Registration, Registration  Nurse, and Check-out queues.

**Root cause addressed:**  
New and follow-up patients may require different processing times, yet share one queue. Staff may spend time entering information that could have been collected before arrival.

**Data support:**  

- Service-time distributions show whether new-patient registration has a substantially longer mean and variance.
- Queue analysis shows whether short follow-up transactions are trapped behind complex cases.
- Rework events identify missing insurance, referral, consent, or demographic information.

**Actions:**

- Offer pre-visit digital registration and insurance verification.
- Send reminders to complete missing forms before arrival.
- Establish a fast path for patients with complete information.
- Use a pooled queue rather than separate clerk-specific lines.
- Introduce virtual or automated check-out for visits requiring only standard instructions.
- Preserve staff-assisted options for patients with accessibility, language, or digital-literacy needs.

**Expected impact:**  
If pre-registration materially reduces service-time variability, a pilot could target a 25–40% reduction in median registration wait for eligible patients and a lower p90 for all patients. Results must be stratified to ensure that non-digital patients are not disadvantaged.

---

### Strategy 4: Parallelize or pre-stage clinically appropriate activities

**Targets:**  
Doctor  ECG, Doctor  Blood Test, Diagnostic Test  Specialist Review, and other serial diagnostic waits.

**Root cause addressed:**  
Activities that do not always require a completed physician consultation may be performed sequentially by convention rather than clinical necessity.

**Data support:**  

- Variant analysis identifies predictable diagnostic combinations.
- Frequent pathways show which patient categories almost always receive the same tests.
- Long waits on serial paths quantify the potential reduction in total duration.
- Conformance analysis shows whether some teams already use a faster, safe variant.

**Actions:**

- Use approved standing protocols to order common pre-visit tests.
- Complete ECG or laboratory collection before consultation when clinically appropriate.
- Run independent administrative and clinical-preparation tasks in parallel.
- Pre-stage rooms and equipment for predictable procedures.
- Route completed results electronically to the responsible clinician immediately.

**Expected impact:**  
If a diagnostic queue is removed from the critical path, the visit-duration reduction can approach the eliminated queue time. For example, eliminating a 15-minute serial delay for 60% of a pathway would reduce average pathway duration by approximately nine minutes, assuming no new downstream bottleneck.

This strategy requires clinical governance; process efficiency must not drive unnecessary testing.

---

### Strategy 5: Implement real-time queue orchestration and handover alerts

**Targets:**  
Registration  Nurse, Nurse  Doctor, Test  Specialist Review, and Check-out.

**Root cause addressed:**  
Patients or results are ready, but the receiving resource is unaware. Work is dispatched manually, staff maintain separate lists, or rooms are not visibly released.

**Data support:**  

- Long waits occur despite idle receiving resources.
- Delays are concentrated at handovers.
- Cases wait longer with certain combinations of departments or resources.
- Queue discipline analysis shows inconsistent selection or excessive overtaking.

**Actions:**

- Create a shared real-time “patient ready” board.
- Automatically notify the next unit when prerequisites are complete.
- Use first-ready/clinical-priority dispatch rules.
- Trigger escalation when a wait exceeds a threshold.
- Display room and equipment status.
- Provide estimated delay information to patients.

**Expected impact:**  
This should primarily reduce avoidable handover latency and variability. A reasonable pilot target would be a 20% reduction in median handover delays and a larger reduction in the number of extreme waits, subject to baseline results.

## 4. Trade-offs and Constraints

### 4.1 Bottleneck displacement

Reducing registration time may move a larger arrival wave to the nurse queue. Accelerating physician consultations may overload diagnostics or check-out.

Therefore, each intervention should be evaluated at the whole-pathway level, not only on the targeted queue. Discrete-event simulation can test whether the bottleneck moves and whether total visit duration actually improves.

### 4.2 Cost versus wait reduction

Additional permanent staff may reduce waits but increase costs and possibly create idle time outside peaks. Lower-cost alternatives should be tested first:

1. Schedule and break changes
2. Resource pooling and cross-training
3. Appointment-template changes
4. Digital coordination
5. Limited flex staffing
6. Permanent capacity only when sustained demand justifies it

The relevant financial measure is not only staffing cost but also cost per completed visit, overtime, abandonment, rework, and potential lost capacity.

### 4.3 Staff workload and sustainability

High utilization can reduce short-term queues but increase:

- Burnout
- Overtime
- Errors
- Absenteeism
- Turnover
- Reduced ability to absorb urgent cases

Staff utilization should therefore be optimized, not maximized. Interventions should include staff feedback and workload monitoring.

### 4.4 Care quality and clinical autonomy

Shortening service time is not automatically desirable. A lower consultation duration may reflect rushed care.

Guardrails should include:

- No increase in rework or repeat visits
- No rise in incident or complaint rates
- Adequate consultation duration for complex patients
- No inappropriate diagnostic pre-ordering
- Priority protection for urgent patients
- Clinical approval of redesigned pathways

### 4.5 Equity and patient experience

Digital pre-registration may disadvantage older patients, patients with disabilities, or those with limited language or technology access. Fast lanes can also create perceptions of unfairness.

Alternatives such as staff-assisted kiosks, interpreters, and telephone completion should remain available. KPI reporting should be stratified by patient group.

### 4.6 Balancing objectives

I would use a constrained optimization approach:

**Primary objectives**

- Reduce total waiting time
- Reduce p90 visit duration
- Improve reliability and satisfaction

**Constraints**

- Maintain clinical-quality indicators
- Stay within staffing and overtime budgets
- Avoid excessive utilization
- Maintain urgent-patient access
- Avoid worsening outcomes for patient subgroups

Rather than selecting the intervention with the lowest average wait, management should choose a Pareto-efficient option that gives substantial wait reduction without unacceptable cost or quality effects.

## 5. Measuring Success

### 5.1 Core KPIs

#### Patient-flow KPIs

- Mean, median, and p90 waiting time for each critical queue
- Percentage exceeding queue-specific wait standards
- Total waiting time per visit
- Median and p90 total visit duration
- Total waiting hours per 100 visits
- Flow efficiency
- Queue length by time of day
- Throughput per session or day
- Percentage of visits completed within target duration
- Abandonment or incomplete-visit rate

#### Operational KPIs

- Resource utilization by interval
- Overtime hours
- Sessions starting and ending on time
- Service-time variability
- Patients served per staff hour
- Room and equipment utilization
- Handover delay
- Rework and repeated-activity rate
- Cost per completed visit

#### Quality and experience KPIs

- Patient satisfaction, especially ratings of waiting and communication
- Complaints related to delays
- Clinical incident rates
- Unplanned repeat visits attributable to process or care issues
- Diagnostic or documentation errors
- Staff workload and satisfaction
- Equity gaps by patient type, urgency, age, language, or accessibility status

Averages should always be accompanied by tail measures. A reduced mean with an unchanged p90 may mean that the intervention helped easy cases but not patients with the worst experience.

### 5.2 Evaluation design

Before deployment, establish a baseline of at least several representative weeks, adjusted for:

- Specialty
- Day and time
- Patient type
- Urgency
- Seasonal volume
- Visit complexity

Then implement changes through a pilot, such as one specialty or selected clinic sessions. Evaluation options include:

- Before-and-after analysis with case-mix adjustment
- Comparison with an unchanged specialty or clinic
- Difference-in-differences analysis
- Stepped-wedge implementation
- Randomized appointment-template testing where feasible

Success criteria should be defined in advance. For example:

- At least 20% reduction in p90 Nurse  Doctor wait
- At least 10% reduction in p90 total visit duration
- No increase in overtime or cost per visit above an agreed threshold
- No deterioration in clinical-quality measures
- No worsening of wait times for urgent or digitally excluded patients

### 5.3 Ongoing process monitoring

The same lifecycle event-log structure can support a continuously refreshed dashboard. It should provide:

- Performance-annotated process maps
- Queue heat maps by time, specialty, and resource
- Daily and weekly p50/p90 control charts
- Alerts for excessive queue length or wait
- Variant and rework monitoring
- Resource-utilization trends
- Patient-group comparisons

Statistical process control should distinguish a sustained improvement from normal variation. Alerts can be generated when:

- A p90 wait exceeds its control limit
- Queue length remains above a threshold for multiple intervals
- Backlog is not cleared by session end
- A new slow process variant becomes common
- A subgroup’s performance deteriorates

Periodic reviews should compare current process behavior with the redesigned target process. If improvements fade, the clinic should investigate schedule drift, staffing changes, increasing demand, or workarounds.

## Recommended implementation sequence

1. Validate and enrich the event log.
2. Build the performance process map and queue inventory.
3. Prioritize queues using volume, tail delay, total burden, and clinical risk.
4. Analyze time, resource, patient, and pathway drivers.
5. Simulate candidate interventions.
6. Pilot low-cost scheduling, pooling, and coordination changes first.
7. Monitor total pathway outcomes and quality guardrails.
8. Scale only interventions that produce sustained, risk-adjusted improvement.

This approach ensures that the clinic does not merely reduce one visible queue, but improves end-to-end patient flow while protecting cost, staff sustainability, equity, and quality of care.
