## 2. Root-cause analysis

### Complexity

Complexity is the strongest apparent driver:

- Low-complexity cases:
  - 2001: 1 h 30 m
  - 2004: 1 h 25 m
- Medium-complexity case:
  - 2002: 25 h 55 m
- High-complexity cases:
  - 2003: 48 h 20 m
  - 2005: 77 h 05 m

Both high-complexity cases require repeated **Request Additional Documents** events:

- Case 2003: two requests
- Case 2005: three requests

These requests introduce substantial waiting time before approval. This suggests that claim complexity leads to incomplete submissions, more investigation, and repeated customer or internal follow-up.

### Additional-document requests

The largest delays occur around document collection:

- Case 2002 waits from the first request on Apr 1 at 14:00 until approval on Apr 2 at 10:00.
- Case 2003 has repeated requests on Apr 1, followed by approval only on Apr 2.
- Case 2005 has three requests spread across Apr 1–3, with approval not occurring until Apr 4.

Thus, the document-request loop is a major bottleneck. The process appears to wait for documents rather than progressing through parallel review or using a clear escalation mechanism.

### Region

Region B contains cases 2002, 2004, and 2005:

- 2004 is fast, so Region B alone does not explain poor performance.
- 2002 and 2005 are slow, but both also have medium or high complexity.

Region A contains cases 2001 and 2003:

- 2001 is fast.
- 2003 is slow because of high complexity and repeated document requests.

Therefore, there is **no clear standalone regional effect** in this small sample. Region B may have operational differences—such as customer response times, staffing, or approval queues—but the data is insufficient to establish that conclusively.

### Resources

Several resource-related patterns are visible:

- **Adjuster_Lisa** handles cases 2002, 2004, and 2005.
  - 2004 is fast, while 2002 and 2005 are slow.
  - This suggests the delay is more likely related to claim complexity and document collection than to Lisa alone.
- **Adjuster_Mike** handles cases 2001 and 2003.
  - 2001 is fast; 2003 is slow.
  - Again, complexity appears more influential than the adjuster.
- **Manager_Bill** approves cases 2003 and 2005, both high-complexity cases.
- **Manager_Ann** approves cases 2001, 2002, and 2004.
  - Case 2002 is delayed before approval, but the data does not show a major approval delay once the documents are available.

There may be an approval-capacity issue involving **Manager_Bill**, but the sample is too small to prove this. In particular, the approval delay may simply reflect that the claims were not ready for approval until the required documents were received.

## 3. Likely explanations and mitigation actions

### Explanation 1: High-complexity claims need more evidence

High-complexity claims are more likely to be incomplete or require detailed validation. This causes repeated document requests and long waiting periods.

**Mitigations:**

- Use a complexity-based checklist at submission.
- Identify required documents upfront.
- Add automated validation for missing or inconsistent information.
- Route high-complexity claims immediately to specialized adjusters.
- Use a single consolidated document request instead of multiple incremental requests.

### Explanation 2: Waiting for documents is unmanaged

The repeated requests in cases 2003 and 2005 indicate that the document-collection stage lacks effective follow-up and escalation.

**Mitigations:**

- Provide customers with a portal showing all outstanding documents.
- Send automated reminders and deadlines.
- Track the age of each pending request.
- Escalate unresponsive cases after a defined period.
- Allow adjusters to review documents as they arrive rather than waiting for the entire package.

### Explanation 3: Possible approval bottlenecks

High-complexity cases require approval by **Manager_Bill**, and both such cases have long total durations. This may indicate limited capacity or specialized approval requirements.

**Mitigations:**

- Establish backup approvers.
- Set service-level targets for approval.
- Use approval queues and workload balancing.
- Permit delegated approval within defined risk thresholds.
- Measure time separately for “awaiting documents” and “awaiting approval.”

### Explanation 4: Regional or resource effects may be masked by complexity

The log does not show a consistent region or individual-resource effect because both Region A and B, and both adjusters, handle both fast and slow cases. More data is needed to distinguish resource or regional performance from claim complexity.

Useful follow-up analysis would compare:

- Average duration by region, controlling for complexity.
- Average duration by adjuster and manager.
- Time spent in each activity and waiting state.
- Number of document requests per case.
- Customer response time to document requests.
- Approval queue time after all documents are complete.

## Overall conclusion

The clearest root cause is **claim complexity**, particularly the repeated additional-document cycle. Cases **2002, 2003, and 2005** are performance outliers, with **2005** being the most severe. Region and resource assignment may contribute through staffing or queue effects, but the current data does not establish them as primary causes. The most effective improvements would target early document completeness, automated follow-up, complexity-based routing, and monitoring of approval and document-waiting times.
