## 1. Process Discovery and Conformance Checking

### 1.1 Build a cohesive event log

The first step is to turn the raw multi-system data into one or more process-mining-ready event logs. Process mining requires, at minimum:

- **Case ID** — the process instance being analyzed
- **Activity name** — what happened
- **Timestamp** — when it happened

For Speedy Parcels, a single case notion is unlikely to answer every question. A practical approach is to maintain two connected perspectives:

1. **Vehicle-day case log**  
   - Case ID: `Vehicle ID + operational date`, e.g., `V12-20241205`
   - Best for route execution, depot operations, traffic delays, vehicle utilization, unscheduled stops, and maintenance interruptions.

2. **Package-delivery case log**  
   - Case ID: `Package ID` or `Package ID + delivery attempt`
   - Best for on-time delivery, failed deliveries, time-window compliance, reattempts, and customer-service performance.

A third optional view can be created for **route-run cases**, where each planned route assigned by dispatch is a case. This helps compare the actual route execution with the dispatch plan.

#### Example standardized event log

| Case ID | Event timestamp | Activity | Vehicle | Driver | Package | Stop ID | Route ID | Location | Planned/Actual | Additional attributes |
|---|---:|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| V12-20241205 | 07:30:40 | Route Assigned | V12 | D105 | — | — | R-102 | Depot | Planned | 35 planned stops |
| V12-20241205 | 07:45:22 | Depart Depot | V12 | D105 | — | — | R-102 | Depot | Actual | odometer/fuel level |
| V12-20241205 | 08:10:55 | Traffic Delay / Low Speed | V12 | D105 | — | — | R-102 | road segment | Actual | speed 5 km/h |
| P9876 | 08:25:10 | Arrive Customer | V12 | D105 | P9876 | S1 | R-102 | customer address | Actual | time window |
| P9876 | 08:28:30 | Delivery Success | V12 | D105 | P9876 | S1 | R-102 | customer address | Actual | proof of delivery |
| V12-20241205 | 11:05:00 | Unscheduled Maintenance Stop | V12 | D105 | — | — | R-102 | roadside/workshop | Actual | warning light |
| V12-20241205 | 16:50:20 | Arrive Depot | V12 | D105 | — | — | R-102 | Depot | Actual | remaining packages |

### 1.2 Data integration and preprocessing

The source data must be normalized before analysis.

#### A. Standardize timestamps

All systems should use:

- One common time zone
- A consistent timestamp format
- A documented timestamp meaning: event occurrence time, device-recorded time, upload time, or system processing time

For example, handheld scanner events may be uploaded after the actual scan, while GPS events may arrive in near real time. The analysis should use the closest available “event occurred” timestamp, not merely the ingestion time.

#### B. Standardize activities

Events from different systems may describe the same operational action differently. Create an activity taxonomy, for example:

- Shift Start
- Route Assigned
- Vehicle Loaded
- Depart Depot
- Travel Segment Started
- Traffic Delay Detected
- Arrive Customer
- Parking/Search for Access
- Delivery Success
- Delivery Failed—Customer Unavailable
- Delivery Failed—Access Issue
- Depart Customer
- Refuel
- Unscheduled Stop
- Maintenance/Repair Start
- Maintenance/Repair End
- Return to Depot
- Shift End

GPS events are especially granular and must usually be transformed into meaningful business activities rather than stored as millions of raw location pings.

#### C. Derive events from GPS telemetry

GPS records can be converted into operational events using business rules and geofences:

- **Depart depot:** vehicle exits depot geofence.
- **Arrive customer:** vehicle enters customer geofence and remains at low speed.
- **Depart customer:** vehicle exits customer geofence.
- **Traffic delay:** sustained low speed below a threshold, such as below 10 km/h, outside a known stop or depot zone.
- **Idle event:** ignition on, speed near zero for longer than a threshold.
- **Unplanned stop:** prolonged stationary period outside planned stops, depot, refueling sites, or approved break zones.
- **Route deviation:** vehicle moves beyond a defined distance from the planned corridor.
- **Maintenance interruption:** GPS stop combined with maintenance or warning-light records.

Thresholds must be calibrated. For example, five minutes of zero speed on a narrow city street may indicate a parking/searching-for-access problem, while the same event at a customer location could be normal service time.

#### D. Add planned-route attributes

The dispatch system should provide:

- Planned stop sequence
- Planned start and finish times
- Planned travel times between stops
- Planned delivery time windows
- Planned package counts, weights, and volume
- Assigned vehicle and driver
- Vehicle capacity and expected load utilization
- Route/territory identifier

These become event and case attributes used for conformance checking and performance segmentation.

#### E. Join maintenance records

Maintenance events need to be matched by vehicle and time interval. They should distinguish:

- Scheduled maintenance
- Unscheduled repair
- Breakdown or warning event
- Roadside assistance
- Workshop visit
- Vehicle unavailable time
- Repair duration and reason code

This makes it possible to determine whether a breakdown merely occurred during the day or actually disrupted delivery execution.

### 1.3 Key data challenges

Likely challenges include:

1. **Different granularity across systems**  
   GPS may produce events every few seconds; scanner events occur only at delivery milestones; dispatch data may represent plans rather than execution.

2. **Inconsistent identifiers**  
   Vehicle, driver, package, route, address, and stop identifiers may differ across systems or be missing.

3. **Timestamp synchronization problems**  
   Device clocks may drift, scanner events may be uploaded late, and some GPS records may be delayed.

4. **Missing or duplicate events**  
   A driver may forget a scan, scan at the wrong time, or scan a package twice. GPS outages may create apparent gaps in travel.

5. **Geocoding quality**  
   Customer addresses, GPS points, and planned stop coordinates may not align exactly, especially in apartment complexes or dense urban areas.

6. **Case-definition ambiguity**  
   A vehicle-day view is useful for fleet operations, but individual packages may cross days or undergo multiple failed attempts. Both vehicle-level and package-level analysis are required.

7. **Separating normal stops from problem stops**  
   A stationary van may be servicing a customer, waiting in traffic, taking a break, searching for parking, refueling, or experiencing a breakdown. Context is essential.

8. **Privacy and workforce considerations**  
   Driver-level analysis must be governed carefully. The goal should be operational improvement, safety, and coaching—not simplistic surveillance or punitive ranking without accounting for route difficulty.

### 1.4 Discover the actual delivery process

Once the event log is prepared, process discovery can reveal how delivery operations actually occur rather than how dispatch assumes they occur.

Useful process-mining techniques include:

- **Inductive Miner:** Produces structured, understandable models and handles infrequent behavior relatively well.
- **Heuristics Miner:** Useful where real operational behavior is noisy and highly variable.
- **Directly-Follows Graphs (DFGs):** Particularly effective for operational dashboards because they show the dominant transitions and their frequencies.
- **Process maps with performance overlays:** Display median/mean durations on each transition, such as “Depart Depot  Arrive Customer” or “Arrive Customer  Delivery Success.”

A discovered vehicle-day process might resemble:

`Start Shift  Route Assigned  Depart Depot  Travel  Arrive Customer  Delivery Success/Failure  Depart Customer  Travel  ...  Return Depot  End Shift`

But the actual model may expose meaningful variants such as:

- `Travel  Traffic Delay  Arrive Customer`
- `Arrive Customer  Long Idle/Parking Search  Delivery Success`
- `Arrive Customer  Delivery Failed  Reattempt Scheduled`
- `Travel  Unscheduled Stop  Maintenance/Repair  Resume Route`
- `Delivery Failed  Return to Depot with Undelivered Package`
- `Route Deviation  Extra Travel  Arrive Customer`

The analysis should not focus only on the “happy path.” The key value is to quantify exceptional paths, their frequency, and their operational impact.

### 1.5 Compare actual performance with planned routes: conformance checking

The dispatch plan can be represented as a reference model or expected sequence for each route. Conformance checking compares actual event traces against that expected route plan.

Relevant methods include:

- **Alignment-based conformance checking:** Measures deviations between actual and planned event sequences.
- **Token replay:** Evaluates how well actual events can be replayed through the planned process model.
- **Sequence conformance:** Compares planned stop order with actual stop order.
- **Temporal conformance:** Compares planned versus actual arrival, departure, travel, and service times.
- **Geospatial conformance:** Measures distance or time spent outside the planned route corridor.
- **Rule-based compliance checks:** Useful where a full process model is unnecessary, for example, “all planned stops should have a delivery attempt,” or “no customer arrival should occur outside permitted time windows unless authorized.”

#### Deviations to identify

1. **Stop sequence deviations**
   - Customer stops visited in a different order from the plan.
   - High-frequency route resequencing.
   - Stops skipped and later revisited.
   - Delivery attempts made after returning to an area already visited.

2. **Unplanned stops**
   - Long idle periods outside planned delivery locations.
   - Unplanned refueling.
   - Maintenance stops.
   - Excessive breaks or waiting periods, while allowing for legitimate operational breaks.

3. **Route deviations**
   - Significant distance traveled outside the planned route.
   - Repeated detours around particular road segments.
   - Backtracking and loop patterns.
   - Unplanned zone crossings.

4. **Timing deviations**
   - Late route departure from depot.
   - Actual travel times far exceeding planned travel times.
   - Long service time at certain stop types or geographies.
   - Arrival outside customer time windows.
   - Late return to depot and overtime.

5. **Capacity and loading deviations**
   - Routes with too many packages, excessive volume, or impractical sequence design.
   - Vehicles returning with many undelivered packages despite apparent route completion.
   - Capacity underutilization that creates more routes than necessary.

6. **Maintenance-related deviations**
   - Routes interrupted by vehicle faults.
   - Repeated vehicle-specific disruptions.
   - Delays following warning-light events or maintenance-related stops.

The output should be a ranked deviation report by route, territory, vehicle, driver, day of week, time of day, and customer segment.

## 2. Performance Analysis and Bottleneck Identification

### 2.1 Core KPIs

The KPI framework should combine customer service, route productivity, vehicle efficiency, and reliability.

| KPI | Definition / Calculation | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| On-Time Delivery Rate | Successful deliveries completed within promised time window ÷ eligible deliveries | Measures customer punctuality |
| First-Attempt Delivery Success Rate | Packages successfully delivered on first attempt ÷ packages attempted | Measures failed-delivery problem |
| Failed Delivery Rate | Failed delivery attempts ÷ total delivery attempts | Identifies redelivery burden |
| Average Stop Service Time | `Depart Customer – Arrive Customer` | Measures customer-stop dwell time |
| Travel Time Between Stops | `Arrive next stop – Depart prior stop` | Measures route/travel efficiency |
| Planned vs Actual Travel Time Variance | `Actual travel time – planned travel time` | Measures planning accuracy and congestion impact |
| Route Completion Time | `Arrive Depot – Depart Depot` | Overall route duration |
| Delivery Productivity | Successful deliveries ÷ route hour; packages ÷ driver hour | Measures output |
| Stops per Hour | Total attempted stops ÷ active route hours | Assesses route productivity |
| Distance per Successful Delivery | Actual route km ÷ successful deliveries | Detects inefficient territory/sequence design |
| Fuel per km | Fuel consumed ÷ km traveled | Vehicle/driver/route fuel efficiency |
| Fuel per successful package | Fuel consumed ÷ successful deliveries | End-to-end cost efficiency |
| Idling Rate | Idle engine-on time ÷ operating time | Fuel, emissions, and wear indicator |
| Unplanned Stop Duration | Total duration of unplanned stationary events | Detects disruptions |
| Traffic Delay Time | Time in low-speed movement or traffic-defined events outside stops | Measures congestion impact |
| Vehicle Utilization Rate | Active delivery/travel time ÷ available shift time; or load volume/weight ÷ capacity | Measures asset use |
| Vehicle Availability | Available vehicle time ÷ scheduled vehicle time | Maintenance reliability |
| Breakdown Rate | Unscheduled maintenance events per 1,000 km or vehicle operating days | Reliability measure |
| Overtime Rate | Routes exceeding scheduled driver hours ÷ all routes | Workforce and planning impact |
| Route Adherence Rate | Planned stops performed in approved sequence/corridor within tolerance ÷ planned stops/routes | Conformance measure |
| Re-delivery Cost/Rate | Reattempts ÷ packages, plus added km/time/fuel | Quantifies failed delivery impact |

### 2.2 Calculating KPIs from the event log

Examples:

#### On-Time Delivery Rate

For each successful package delivery:

- Retrieve the customer-requested time window from dispatch.
- Compare `Delivery Success timestamp` with window start and end.
- Mark as on time if it falls within the agreed rules.

\[
\text{On-Time Delivery Rate} =
\frac{\text{Successful deliveries within time window}}{\text{Eligible successful deliveries}}
\times 100
\]

The analysis should also distinguish:

- Early deliveries
- Late deliveries
- Deliveries outside the window due to customer-approved changes
- Routes with no customer time window

#### Stop service time

\[
\text{Service Time} =
\text{Depart Customer Time} - \text{Arrive Customer Time}
\]

If the departure scan is unreliable, GPS geofence exit can supplement it. Service time can be segmented by:

- Delivery type: home, business, locker, apartment
- Parcel size and signature requirement
- Neighborhood density
- Driver
- Time of day
- Failed vs successful delivery
- Building access type

#### Travel versus service ratio

\[
\text{Travel-to-Service Ratio} =
\frac{\text{Total travel time}}{\text{Total customer service time}}
\]

A high ratio may signal poor territory design, excessive detours, or congestion. A low ratio combined with long route duration could signal slow customer-stop processes, parking problems, or difficult access.

#### Fuel per successful package

\[
\text{Fuel per Successful Delivery} =
\frac{\text{Fuel consumed during route}}{\text{Number of successful deliveries}}
\]

Fuel can be derived from fuel-card data, telematics, estimated consumption models, or vehicle CAN-bus data. It should be adjusted for vehicle type, load, terrain, and route characteristics where possible.

### 2.3 Bottleneck identification techniques

#### A. Performance-overlay process maps

Overlay average, median, and percentile durations onto transitions:

- Depot departure to first stop
- Travel between stops
- Arrival to delivery completion
- Delivery completion to departure
- Delay event to route resumption
- Maintenance interruption to resumption

Median values show typical performance; 90th or 95th percentiles expose unreliable tail behavior that drives late deliveries.

#### B. Waiting-time and dwell-time analysis

Analyze stationary intervals:

- At depot before departure
- At customer locations
- Near customers but outside the geofence, indicating parking/search behavior
- On road segments, indicating congestion
- At maintenance facilities
- At unplanned locations

A useful decomposition is:

\[
\text{Route Duration} =
\text{Travel Time} +
\text{Customer Service Time} +
\text{Traffic Delay} +
\text{Idle/Waiting Time} +
\text{Breakdown/Maintenance Time} +
\text{Other Non-productive Time}
\]

This makes the source of route inefficiency visible.

#### C. Spatial hotspot analysis

Map GPS-derived delays and dwell times to road segments, neighborhoods, or customer zones:

- Roads repeatedly associated with low speed
- Blocks with chronic parking difficulty
- Customer clusters with long service time
- Areas with high failed delivery rates
- Zones causing excessive route deviations

Heat maps can be segmented by hour of day, day of week, weather, vehicle type, and route.

#### D. Segmentation and cohort comparison

Compare KPIs across:

- Route and territory
- Driver
- Vehicle model/age
- Depot
- Day of week
- Shift start time
- Urban vs suburban zones
- Customer type
- Parcel size or signature requirement
- Planned load level
- Time-window tightness

This reveals whether the apparent “driver issue” is actually a territory, vehicle, planning, or customer-access issue.

#### E. Variant analysis

Compare process variants for:

- Top 20% on-time routes versus bottom 20%
- Low-fuel versus high-fuel routes
- Routes with and without maintenance disruption
- High vs low first-attempt success
- Routes with high versus low overtime

For example, late routes may share a variant:

`Late Depart Depot  Traffic Delay  Long Customer Dwell  Failed Delivery  Revisit Area  Overtime`

While high-performing routes may exhibit:

`On-Time Departure  Dense Stop Cluster  Short Service Time  Minimal Backtracking  On-Time Return`

#### F. Quantify impact

Each bottleneck should be expressed in business terms:

- Minutes of delay per route
- Percent contribution to late deliveries
- Additional kilometers
- Additional fuel consumption
- Additional overtime hours
- Number of failed deliveries and reattempts
- Maintenance downtime
- Estimated cost per bottleneck type

For example:

> Traffic on two road corridors accounts for 18% of all travel-delay minutes and contributes to 12% of late deliveries on weekday afternoon routes.

Or:

> Failed first attempts in three apartment-heavy zones generate 0.34 additional delivery attempts per package, adding 1,200 km and 180 driver-hours per month.

## 3. Root Cause Analysis for Inefficiencies

Finding a delay is not enough. The analysis must distinguish symptoms from underlying causes.

### 3.1 Suboptimal route planning

#### Potential signs

- Frequent route resequencing by drivers
- Repeated backtracking
- High actual-to-planned distance variance
- Stops in the same neighborhood visited at widely separated times
- Poor balance of route duration across drivers
- Overloaded routes consistently finishing late
- Underloaded routes returning early

#### Validation with process mining

- Compare planned stop order with actual stop order.
- Measure additional distance and time caused by sequence deviations.
- Perform variant analysis between routes that follow the planned sequence and routes that do not.
- Identify whether deviations improve or worsen KPIs. Not every deviation is poor; drivers may be compensating for inaccurate plans.
- Cluster routes by geography and compare actual route patterns with dispatch territory boundaries.

A finding that drivers often deviate from the plan but still outperform planned adherence may indicate that the route optimizer is using poor travel-time assumptions or ignoring local access constraints.

### 3.2 Inaccurate travel-time estimates

#### Potential signs

- Actual travel time consistently exceeds planned time on certain road segments or at specific hours.
- High route lateness despite good driver adherence to plan.
- Late first stops due to underestimated depot-to-territory travel.
- Plans that are feasible on paper but not in normal traffic.

#### Validation

- Calculate actual/planned travel-time ratios by road segment, time period, and day of week.
- Compare estimates against GPS-derived travel times.
- Use percentile-based travel time rather than only average travel time.
- Separate recurring congestion from exceptional incidents.

If a route requires 7.5 hours in the plan but historically takes 8.5 hours at the 75th percentile, the plan is structurally infeasible.

### 3.3 Traffic congestion patterns

#### Potential signs

- Persistent low-speed GPS events in the same locations and time periods.
- Afternoon routes showing materially worse on-time delivery than morning routes.
- High travel-time variability even when stop count is similar.
- Frequent route deviations around known congestion hotspots.

#### Validation

- Link low-speed events to geospatial road segments.
- Segment by time of day and weekday.
- Add external traffic, roadworks, weather, school-calendar, and event data where available.
- Test whether traffic events precede lateness or merely co-occur with it.
- Compare routes that use the same corridor at different departure times.

This may show that an apparently poor route sequence is actually reasonable, but its departure time causes it to enter congested zones at the wrong time.

### 3.4 High variability in customer service time

#### Potential signs

- Large difference between median and 90th-percentile stop duration.
- Specific buildings, customer segments, or parcel types requiring long dwell time.
- Long periods between “Arrive Customer” and “Delivery Success.”
- GPS dwell before scanner arrival events, suggesting parking/access problems.

#### Possible causes

- Parking scarcity
- Apartment access restrictions
- Building entry/security procedures
- Signature requirements
- Customer unavailability
- Large or bulky packages
- Poor address quality
- Long elevator/walking distances
- Commercial customers with receiving queues

#### Validation

- Analyze dwell-time distributions rather than averages alone.
- Compare building type, postcode, parcel type, and customer category.
- Use GPS proximity to determine whether the vehicle is delayed before reaching the exact customer geofence.
- Compare service times for successful and failed deliveries.
- Identify recurring address-level outliers.

A long service time should not automatically be attributed to a driver. It may reflect a dense building, no legal parking, access restrictions, or package handling requirements.

### 3.5 Vehicle breakdowns and maintenance issues

#### Potential signs

- Unscheduled stops associated with warning lights or repair records.
- Certain vehicles having disproportionate route interruption time.
- Repeated breakdown patterns after high utilization or high mileage.
- Increasing idle or fuel-consumption anomalies before repairs.
- More late deliveries on routes assigned to particular vehicle types.

#### Validation

- Link maintenance intervals to active route traces.
- Measure downtime, missed stops, route completion delays, and replacement-vehicle use.
- Compare vehicle reliability by age, model, mileage, and maintenance history.
- Analyze precursor signals such as engine warnings, abnormal idle, temperature, fuel use, or repeated fault codes.

This supports predictive maintenance rather than reacting after roadside failure.

### 3.6 Driver behavior and skill differences

#### Potential signs

- Differences in route adherence, idling, harsh driving, stop service time, fuel efficiency, or failed-delivery handling.
- Some drivers consistently managing difficult routes better than peers.
- Excessive speeding, acceleration, braking, idling, or unplanned detours.

#### Important caution

Driver comparisons must be adjusted for:

- Territory difficulty
- Parcel volume and size
- Customer type
- Vehicle type
- Traffic conditions
- Time window tightness
- Building density
- Planned route quality

#### Validation

Use matched comparisons: compare drivers performing similar routes under similar conditions. Examine process variants and practices of high-performing drivers rather than assuming low performance is individual behavior.

Useful patterns may include:

- High performers start routes promptly and avoid late first departures.
- They cluster stops effectively within allowable flexibility.
- They have lower engine idle time.
- They perform fewer unnecessary re-visits.
- They spend less time in known parking-problem areas because they use established stopping practices.

### 3.7 Failed deliveries and re-deliveries

#### Potential signs

- High “customer not home” failures at residential addresses.
- Specific customer time windows with poor success rates.
- Repeat failures for the same address.
- Failed attempts concentrated late in the day.
- Packages repeatedly carried across multiple route days.

#### Validation

- Build package-level process variants: `Out for Delivery  Failed Attempt  Reassigned  Successful Delivery`.
- Calculate time and distance added by each failed attempt.
- Segment failures by neighborhood, customer type, time window, communication status, and driver arrival timing.
- Compare the failure rate before and after customer notifications.
- Assess whether failed deliveries correlate with lateness; a delivery attempt outside the expected window may itself cause customer absence.

## 4. Data-Driven Optimization Strategies

Below are five complementary strategies. At least three should be prioritized based on the largest measured sources of delay and cost.

### Strategy 1: Dynamic routing and traffic-aware route execution

#### Bottleneck targeted

- Traffic delays
- High actual-versus-planned travel-time variance
- Frequent manual detours
- Late deliveries caused by predictable congestion

#### Root cause addressed

Static plans may use average travel times, outdated traffic assumptions, or routes that are optimal in distance but unreliable during actual delivery hours.

#### Process-mining evidence

Use:

- GPS-derived traffic-delay hotspots
- Planned versus actual travel-time variance
- Route-conformance analysis
- Recurring detours that drivers make around planned corridors
- Time-of-day performance comparisons

If drivers consistently avoid certain planned roads and arrive faster when deviating, their behavior is evidence that the planning model needs improvement.

#### Action

- Integrate real-time traffic and road closure feeds into dispatch.
- Re-optimize route sequence when congestion exceeds a threshold.
- Use historical time-dependent travel times by road segment and hour.
- Protect customer time windows during re-optimization.
- Give drivers controlled flexibility: recommended route changes, not constant disruptive rerouting.
- Establish rules for when rerouting is worthwhile, such as only if expected savings exceed five to ten minutes or prevent a time-window breach.

#### Expected KPI impact

- Higher On-Time Delivery Rate
- Lower actual/planned travel-time variance
- Lower traffic delay time
- Lower overtime
- Reduced fuel per package and distance per successful delivery
- Improved route adherence measured against an updated, realistic route plan

---

### Strategy 2: Rebalance delivery territories and optimize stop sequence using historical actual performance

#### Bottleneck targeted

- Excessive travel between stops
- Backtracking
- Uneven route workloads
- Persistent overtime on certain routes
- Underutilized capacity on other routes

#### Root cause addressed

Territories and route sequences may have been designed using geographic proximity alone, rather than actual travel time, service time, parking/access difficulty, time windows, parcel volume, and congestion patterns.

#### Process-mining evidence

Use:

- Actual stop-to-stop travel times
- Route duration distributions
- Spatial heat maps
- Stop dwell-time data
- Capacity utilization
- Route variants showing repeated backtracking
- Territory-level lateness and fuel consumption

#### Action

- Redesign territory boundaries based on actual operating time rather than straight-line distance.
- Cluster stops using historical travel-time matrices, not only geographic distance.
- Include customer service-time expectations in route planning.
- Assign difficult dense-city zones, bulky-parcel stops, and time-window-sensitive customers appropriately.
- Balance routes based on expected total workload:

\[
\text{Expected Route Workload} =
\text{Expected Travel Time} +
\text{Expected Service Time} +
\text{Expected Delay Allowance}
\]

- Use different route templates for weekday peak periods, weekends, and seasonal demand.

#### Expected KPI impact

- Lower route completion time
- Lower overtime rate
- Lower km per delivery
- Better vehicle capacity utilization
- Better on-time delivery rate
- Fewer route deviations and less backtracking
- Reduced fuel consumption per package

---

### Strategy 3: Improve failed-delivery prevention through customer communication and delivery-option design

#### Bottleneck targeted

- Customer-not-home failures
- Re-delivery trips
- Repeated failed attempts
- Undelivered packages returned to depot

#### Root cause addressed

Customers may not know the expected arrival period, time windows may be too broad or unreliable, delivery options may be inflexible, and drivers may reach customers outside the expected time due to route delays.

#### Process-mining evidence

Use package-level process mining to identify:

- Failure rates by time window, neighborhood, customer type, and delivery day
- Repeat failed-attempt patterns
- Relationship between late arrival and failed delivery
- Addresses with recurring access or availability issues
- Cost of reattempts in time, kilometers, fuel, and capacity

#### Action

- Send proactive ETA notifications with a narrower estimated delivery interval.
- Enable customer confirmation, rescheduling, safe-place authorization, locker pickup, neighbor delivery, or collection-point diversion where permitted.
- Trigger a pre-arrival message for high-risk stops.
- Use historical success probability to schedule residential stops at times with higher first-attempt success.
- Flag addresses with repeated failures for mandatory delivery preference capture.
- For dense urban zones, use parcel lockers, micro-hubs, or consolidated delivery points where economically justified.

#### Expected KPI impact

- Higher First-Attempt Delivery Success Rate
- Lower failed-delivery rate
- Lower re-delivery cost
- Lower distance and fuel per successful delivery
- Higher route productivity
- Reduced overtime and depot return load

---

### Strategy 4: Predictive maintenance and vehicle-assignment optimization

#### Bottleneck targeted

- Unscheduled maintenance stops
- Roadside breakdowns
- Route interruptions
- Vehicle downtime and late deliveries

#### Root cause addressed

Maintenance may be schedule-based but insufficiently informed by actual vehicle usage, operating conditions, warning signals, mileage, idling, and prior fault patterns.

#### Process-mining evidence

Use:

- Maintenance event history
- Vehicle-day process variants
- Unscheduled stop duration
- Warning-light events
- Mileage and utilization
- Fuel-consumption anomalies
- Breakdown rates by vehicle model, age, and route conditions

#### Action

- Build vehicle risk profiles using mileage, fault history, engine warnings, idling, route intensity, and prior repair events.
- Schedule preventive service before high-risk periods or high-demand days.
- Avoid assigning high-risk vehicles to routes with strict time windows.
- Maintain spare-vehicle or contingency capacity for critical delivery areas.
- Investigate vehicle models associated with disproportionate downtime or fuel consumption.
- Use maintenance windows that minimize impact on operational availability.

#### Expected KPI impact

- Higher vehicle availability
- Lower breakdown rate
- Lower unplanned maintenance duration
- Fewer disrupted routes
- Improved on-time delivery
- Lower maintenance cost volatility
- Potentially improved fuel efficiency

---

### Strategy 5: Targeted driver coaching and operational standardization

#### Bottleneck targeted

- Excess idling
- High fuel use
- Poor route adherence where it is harmful
- Long customer-stop time
- Unsafe or inefficient driving behavior
- Inconsistent handling of access, parking, and failed delivery situations

#### Root cause addressed

There may be differences in driving practices, local knowledge, scanning discipline, parking strategies, or customer interaction procedures. However, this must be separated from route difficulty.

#### Process-mining evidence

Use adjusted driver comparisons and variants:

- Fuel per km adjusted for vehicle and route type
- Idling time adjusted for traffic conditions
- Service-time distributions adjusted for customer segment
- On-time delivery adjusted for route workload
- Successful practices from top-performing peers
- Scan timing compliance and sequence consistency

#### Action

- Develop coaching based on observed process patterns, not only aggregate performance rankings.
- Share high-performing practices for parking, route preparation, package organization, customer communication, and scan discipline.
- Provide eco-driving training where idling, acceleration, or fuel patterns indicate opportunity.
- Improve pre-departure checks to reduce late starts and in-route problems.
- Use route-specific briefings for congestion zones and difficult-access customer clusters.
- Avoid punitive use of data; focus on fair, context-adjusted improvement.

#### Expected KPI impact

- Lower idle time and fuel per km
- Reduced customer-stop service time
- Improved scan-data quality
- Better route completion reliability
- Lower overtime
- Higher first-attempt success where delivery practice affects outcomes

## 5. Operational Constraints and Continuous Monitoring

### 5.1 Incorporating operational constraints

Recommendations must be implemented within real operational limits.

#### Driver working hours and regulations

Route optimization must account for:

- Maximum shift duration
- Mandatory breaks
- Overtime rules
- Driver start and end locations
- Driver qualifications and vehicle permissions
- Fair workload allocation

A route that appears efficient in distance but requires consistent overtime is not operationally feasible. The route planning objective should include legal and contractual working-time constraints.

#### Vehicle capacity and parcel characteristics

Plans should account for:

- Weight and volume capacity
- Vehicle type and access restrictions
- Refrigerated or special-handling parcels if applicable
- Parcel size and handling time
- Delivery density
- Vehicle availability due to maintenance

Capacity should be treated as both a physical constraint and a productivity factor. Overloaded vehicles can increase service time, fuel consumption, and risk of failed or deferred deliveries.

#### Customer time windows

Route optimization should prioritize:

- Mandatory customer windows
- Promised delivery windows
- Commercial receiving hours
- High-risk customer availability periods
- Dynamic customer-approved changes

The optimization objective should not minimize distance alone. It should balance on-time delivery, travel time, fuel use, workload fairness, vehicle capacity, and customer commitments.

#### Service-level and contingency rules

The operating model should define:

- When drivers may resequence stops
- When dispatch may reroute a vehicle
- Thresholds for escalation when a route is projected to miss time windows
- How failed deliveries are handled
- When a backup vehicle or driver should be deployed
- How maintenance alerts affect route assignment

### 5.2 Continuous process-mining monitoring plan

Process mining should become a recurring operational capability rather than a one-time diagnostic exercise.

#### A. Dashboard layers

A useful dashboard architecture has four levels.

##### 1. Executive dashboard

Track weekly and monthly trends:

- On-Time Delivery Rate
- First-Attempt Delivery Success Rate
- Fuel per km and fuel per package
- Cost per successful delivery
- Overtime rate
- Vehicle availability and breakdown rate
- Total route completion time
- Customer complaint or late-delivery volume

##### 2. Dispatch and route-operations dashboard

Track daily performance:

- Routes at risk of late completion
- Planned versus actual route progress
- Late departures from depot
- Live or near-real-time traffic delay hotspots
- Route adherence and deviations
- Unplanned stop duration
- Deliveries remaining versus available route time
- Capacity utilization and undelivered packages

##### 3. Fleet maintenance dashboard

Track:

- Unscheduled maintenance events
- Vehicle downtime
- Breakdown frequency per 1,000 km
- Vehicles with increasing warning/fault patterns
- Fuel-efficiency anomalies
- Upcoming preventive maintenance needs
- Vehicle availability by depot and day

##### 4. Process-improvement dashboard

Track process variants and root-cause indicators:

- Most common route variants
- Variants associated with late delivery
- Variants associated with high fuel cost
- Failed-delivery process paths
- Long dwell-time customer clusters
- Persistent traffic hotspots
- Performance by territory, route design, customer type, and vehicle type

### 5.3 Key process views to monitor

The organization should continuously monitor:

1. **Actual process map with frequency and time overlays**  
   Shows whether new exception paths are emerging.

2. **Planned-versus-actual conformance view**  
   Shows route sequence deviations, detours, skipped stops, time-window breaches, and late starts.

3. **Route performance distribution view**  
   Focus on median and 90th-percentile route duration, not only averages.

4. **Spatial delay heat map**  
   Identifies persistent congestion, parking, access, and dwell-time hotspots.

5. **Package re-delivery process map**  
   Tracks failed attempts, reassignments, repeat failures, and final outcomes.

6. **Vehicle reliability process map**  
   Tracks operational paths involving warning events, repair stops, and route disruption.

7. **High-performing versus low-performing variant comparison**  
   Identifies whether interventions are changing behavior and outcomes.

### 5.4 Closed-loop improvement cycle

A practical governance cycle is:

1. **Measure:** Capture and validate event data daily or weekly.
2. **Discover:** Refresh process maps and variants regularly.
3. **Diagnose:** Identify deviations, bottlenecks, and high-cost patterns.
4. **Intervene:** Pilot route, communication, maintenance, or training changes.
5. **Evaluate:** Compare intervention routes with matched control routes.
6. **Standardize:** Roll out successful changes.
7. **Monitor:** Watch for performance drift, unintended consequences, and newly emerging bottlenecks.

For example, a dynamic-routing pilot should be evaluated not only on travel time, but also on customer time-window compliance, driver workload, fuel use, route stability, and potential increases in customer-stop variability.

---

## Conclusion

For Speedy Parcels, process mining can connect dispatch plans, GPS traces, scanner events, and maintenance records into an evidence-based view of actual last-mile delivery operations. The most valuable outcome is not simply a process map; it is the ability to quantify which deviations cause late deliveries and excess cost, determine why they occur, and monitor whether interventions work.

The recommended priority sequence is:

1. Establish a reliable integrated event log and route/package case definitions.
2. Measure planned-versus-actual travel, stop service time, failed deliveries, unplanned stops, and maintenance disruption.
3. Identify the largest delay and cost contributors by territory, time, vehicle, and customer segment.
4. Pilot traffic-aware routing, territory rebalancing, failed-delivery prevention, and predictive maintenance.
5. Use continuous process-mining dashboards to sustain improvements and detect new operational issues early.
