As a Senior Operations Analyst, I approach the challenges at Precision Parts Inc. by recognizing that the current local, static dispatching rules are fundamentally inadequate for a high-mix, low-volume (HMLV) job shop with sequence-dependent setups and high variability. The MES event logs are a goldmine of untapped potential. By applying advanced process mining, we can transition from reactive firefighting to proactive, data-driven orchestration.

Here is the comprehensive, data-driven approach to transform Precision Parts Inc.'s scheduling performance.

---

### 1. Analyzing Historical Scheduling Performance and Dynamics

To understand the true state of the shop floor, we must reconstruct the actual flow of jobs and the execution sequence of tasks using process mining.

*   **Reconstructing Actual Flow:** Using process discovery algorithms (e.g., Heuristic Miner or Fuzzy Miner) on the event log, we will map the actual process variants. This reveals the real routing of jobs, bypassed steps, and rework loops. Dotted chart analysis will be crucial here, plotting Case ID against time, color-coded by Activity or Resource, to visually trace the exact sequence of tasks on each machine and the handoffs between them.
*   **Quantifying KPIs via Process Mining:**
    *   **Job Flow Times, Lead Times, and Makespan:** By calculating the time delta between "Job Released" and the final "Task End" for each Case ID, we can generate statistical distributions (mean, median, variance) rather than relying on averages. Dotted charts will highlight the exact moments jobs stall.
    *   **Task Waiting Times (Queue Times):** By calculating the delta between "Queue Entry" and "Setup Start" (or "Task Start" if no setup), we can quantify queue times per machine. Social Network Mining (organizational mining) will show which machines consistently pass work to others with long wait times.
    *   **Resource Utilization:** We will segment the timeline of each resource into states: *Productive* (Task Start to Task End), *Setup* (Setup Start to Setup End), *Idle* (no active task), and *Down* (Breakdown Start to End). Utilization is then calculated as (Productive + Setup) / Total Time.
    *   **Sequence-Dependent Setup Times:** This requires deep log enrichment. We will extract the "Previous job" from the Notes field during "Setup Start" events. By grouping setups by (Previous Job Type/Material, Current Job Type/Material), we can build a historical setup time matrix. Since HMLV shops have infinite job combinations, we will cluster jobs by attributes (e.g., raw material type, required tooling, tolerance) to create a generalized setup matrix.
    *   **Schedule Adherence and Tardiness:** We will compare the final "Task End" timestamp against the "Order Due Date." We will calculate the Tardiness distribution (percentage of jobs on-time, average tardiness of late jobs, maximum tardiness) and Earliness (to identify WIP holding costs).
    *   **Impact of Disruptions:** Using "Breakdown Start" and "Priority Change" events, we will perform trace analysis. We will isolate the Case IDs in queue or in-process during these events and measure the delta in their completion times compared to the baseline, quantifying the ripple effect of disruptions.

### 2. Diagnosing Scheduling Pathologies

Based on the performance analysis, we will identify specific pathologies using targeted process mining techniques:

*   **Bottleneck Identification:** Using bottleneck analysis (e.g., measuring queue length over time and resource utilization), we will pinpoint constrained resources. We will quantify their impact by measuring the correlation between their queue lengths and overall shop makespan. If MILL-02 is the bottleneck, we will see a direct correlation between its downtime and increased flow times across *all* jobs.
*   **Poor Task Prioritization:** Through variant analysis, we will compare the process variants of "On-Time Jobs" vs. "Late Jobs." We will likely find that late jobs spent disproportionate time in queues at bottleneck machines, waiting behind low-priority or early-released jobs (a classic First-Come-First-Served pathology).
*   **Suboptimal Sequencing:** By analyzing the setup time matrix against the actual sequence of jobs on bottleneck machines, we will identify "setup traps"—sequences where a machine undergoes long, unnecessary setups because dissimilar jobs are processed back-to-back.
*   **Starvation of Downstream Resources:** Using dotted charts, we will identify periods where downstream machines are idle (starved) while upstream machines are busy. This indicates a lack of synchronization; upstream machines are processing jobs that the downstream machine cannot yet accept, or the upstream machine is blocked and cannot release work.
*   **Bullwhip Effect in WIP:** By plotting cumulative WIP over time, we will observe the bullwhip effect. A minor disruption upstream (e.g., a slight delay in Cutting) will be amplified downstream, causing massive WIP accumulation before bottleneck machines and starvation after them, as the schedule fails to adapt dynamically.

### 3. Root Cause Analysis of Scheduling Ineffectiveness

Process mining allows us to differentiate between logic failures, capacity constraints, and inherent variability:

*   **Limitations of Static Rules:** FCFS and EDD are myopic. They optimize locally at one machine without considering downstream capacity or sequence-dependent setups. Process mining proves this by showing that jobs with later due dates but shorter setups or downstream urgency are processed first, causing systemic delays.
*   **Lack of Real-time Visibility:** The logs will show that operators and local supervisors make decisions based on local queue lengths. Process mining reveals the "hidden factory"—time spent searching for materials or waiting for upstream tasks that were not visible to the local work center.
*   **Inaccurate Duration Estimations:** By comparing "Task Duration (Planned)" vs. "Task Duration (Actual)" in the log, we can identify systematic underestimation. If actuals consistently exceed planned times, schedules are inherently unachievable from the moment they are published.
*   **Ineffective Handling of Setups:** The root cause is treating setup as a fixed, sequence-independent constant. The log will show that setups vary wildly based on sequence, yet the schedule ignores this, leading to chronic underestimation of machine occupancy.
*   **Differentiating Logic vs. Capacity vs. Variability:** 
    *   If a machine is 95% utilized and queues are growing, it is a *capacity* problem. 
    *   If a machine has 70% utilization but high idle time due to long setups, it is a *scheduling logic* problem. 
    *   If task durations have high variance (wide distribution) even under similar conditions, it is an *inherent variability* problem requiring buffering. Process mining isolates these by separating setup time, waiting time, and processing time in the flow analysis.

### 4. Developing Advanced Data-Driven Scheduling Strategies

Based on the root causes, I propose three sophisticated, data-driven strategies:

#### Strategy 1: Composite Dynamic Dispatching Rules (CDDR)
*   **Core Logic:** Replace static rules with a dynamic priority index calculated for every job in a queue. The index will be a weighted composite score: $Score = w_1(DD) + w_2(Priority) + w_3(Setup Saving) + w_4(Downstream Load)$. 
*   **PM Insight Integration:** The weights ($w_1, w_2$, etc.) will be calibrated using regression analysis on historical data to find the combination that maximizes on-time delivery. $w_3$ is informed by the historical setup matrix (favoring jobs similar to the current machine state). $w_4$ uses real-time downstream queue lengths to prioritize jobs heading to starved machines.
*   **Addresses:** Poor prioritization, suboptimal sequencing, and starvation.
*   **Expected Impact:** Reduces tardiness by ensuring high-priority/downstream-critical jobs bypass the queue; reduces setup times by intelligently sequencing similar jobs.

#### Strategy 2: Predictive Scheduling & Proactive Disruption Management
*   **Core Logic:** Instead of using average task durations, we use the full historical distributions (mined from the logs) to run Monte Carlo simulations at the time of scheduling. This generates probabilistic due dates (e.g., "Job X has an 85% chance of finishing by April 28"). Furthermore, we use predictive maintenance models (deriving MTBF/MTTR from breakdown events) to schedule proactive maintenance during natural lulls, and dynamically re-route jobs upon unplanned breakdowns.
*   **PM Insight Integration:** We mine the actual duration distributions by operator, shift, and job complexity. We also mine breakdown patterns to predict failure probabilities based on operational hours.
*   **Addresses:** Unpredictable lead times, impact of disruptions, and inaccurate duration estimations.
*   **Expected Impact:** Provides customers with highly reliable lead times; drastically reduces the ripple effect of breakdowns by pre-computing alternative routings.

#### Strategy 3: Setup Time Optimization via Virtual Batching (Setup TSP)
*   **Core Logic:** At bottleneck machines, instead of dispatching jobs one-by-one as they arrive, we implement a "look-ahead" window. We hold incoming jobs in a virtual queue for a short, calculated period and solve a Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) to find the sequence that minimizes total setup time while respecting due dates.
*   **PM Insight Integration:** The TSP relies entirely on the generalized Sequence-Dependent Setup Matrix mined from the event logs. Jobs are clustered by material and tooling requirements to make the matrix robust.
*   **Addresses:** Suboptimal sequencing and bottleneck throughput.
*   **Expected Impact:** Increases effective capacity at bottleneck machines by 10-20% purely by reducing setup time, directly lowering WIP and improving flow time.

### 5. Simulation, Evaluation, and Continuous Improvement

Before deploying these strategies on the live shop floor, rigorous testing is required.

*   **Discrete-Event Simulation (DES):** We will build a digital twin of Precision Parts Inc. using DES software. The model will be parameterized directly from process mining data: routing probabilities from process discovery, task duration distributions (not averages) from the logs, setup matrices from the setup analysis, and breakdown MTBF/MTTR from disruption events.
*   **Testing Scenarios:** We will run the baseline (current FCFS/EDD) against the three proposed strategies under various scenarios:
    *   *High Load:* 120% of current order volume to test bottleneck behavior.
    *   *Frequent Disruptions:* Injecting breakdowns at 2x the historical rate to test the predictive strategy.
    *   *Hot Job Injections:* Introducing urgent jobs mid-stream to test dynamic re-prioritization.
*   **Continuous Monitoring & Adaptation:** Once a strategy is deployed, process mining becomes a real-time monitoring tool. We will set up automated conformance checking and KPI dashboards tracking the exact metrics defined in Step 1. 
*   **Drift Detection:** We will implement concept drift detection on the event logs. If the process begins to deviate from the optimized model (e.g., a new type of job is introduced that breaks the setup matrix, or a machine begins degrading faster than predicted), the system will flag the drift. This triggers a re-calibration of the scheduling weights and simulation models, ensuring the scheduling logic evolves with the shop floor.
