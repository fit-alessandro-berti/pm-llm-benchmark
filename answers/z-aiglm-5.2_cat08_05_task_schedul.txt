Here is a comprehensive, data-driven approach to analyzing and optimizing the scheduling performance at Precision Parts Inc. using process mining and advanced scheduling techniques.

### 1. Analyzing Historical Scheduling Performance and Dynamics

To reconstruct the actual shop floor dynamics, I would extract the MES event logs and load them into a process mining tool. Using the Case ID (Job ID), Timestamps, and Activity/Resource attributes, we can create a dynamic process map (often resembling a "Spaghetti" diagram due to the high-mix nature) to visualize the actual flow of jobs across machines.

Specific process mining techniques and metrics to quantify performance include:

*   **Job Flow Times, Lead Times, and Makespan:** By comparing the "Job Released" timestamp to the final "Task End" timestamp, we calculate the actual lead time. We can use *Performance Analysis* on the process map (e.g., timing a timed Petri net) to highlight bottlenecks in the routing and view the distribution of lead times across all jobs to establish baseline unpredictability.
*   **Task Waiting Times (Queue Times):** We calculate the delta between "Queue Entry" and "Setup/Task Start" events for each Resource. Aggregating this by resource creates a "Queue Time Heatmap," instantly highlighting which machines are acting as choke points.
*   **Resource Utilization:** We transform the event log into a resource-state log. For each machine, we calculate the proportion of time in "Productive" (Task Start to End), "Setup" (Setup Start to End), "Idle" (gap between end of one job and start of next), and "Down" (Breakdown Start to End) states.
*   **Sequence-Dependent Setup Times:** We filter the log by a specific Resource, order by Timestamp, and extract consecutive tasks. By joining the "Setup Duration (Actual)" with the "Previous Job ID" and "Current Job ID," we build a historical Setup Time Matrix. We can use clustering or correlation analysis to categorize part families and quantify the specific setup penalty for transitioning from Part A to Part B.
*   **Schedule Adherence and Tardiness:** We compare the final "Task End" timestamp of a case against the "Order Due Date" attribute. Tardiness is calculated as $\max(0, CompletionTime - DueDate)$. We can overlay this on the process map to trace the exact paths that lead to the highest tardiness.
*   **Impact of Disruptions:** We isolate event log segments containing "Breakdown Start" or "Priority Change" and compare the KPIs (e.g., queue times, tardiness) of jobs active during these periods against a baseline of undisrupted periods.

### 2. Diagnosing Scheduling Pathologies

Based on the analysis above, we can identify critical pathologies:

*   **Bottleneck Identification:** Bottlenecks are easily identified by looking for resources with near 100% active time (Setup + Productive) coupled with the longest average queue times. The impact on overall throughput is quantified by the WIP accumulation directly upstream of this resource.
*   **Poor Task Prioritization:** Through *Variant Analysis*, we can split the log into "On-Time" and "Tardy" jobs. By analyzing the sequences at bottleneck machines, we often find that high-priority jobs (or those with imminent due dates) are waiting behind lower-priority jobs, proving the failure of the current localized FCFS/EDD mix.
*   **Suboptimal Sequencing:** By analyzing the Setup Time Matrix derived in step 1, we can identify "setup time inflation." If a machine is alternating between highly dissimilar part families (e.g., a Lathing job followed by a Grinding job, then back to Lathing), the setup times will spike. We can quantify the percentage of total machine time wasted on avoidable setups.
*   **Resource Starvation:** We look for downstream machines with high "Idle" time. If their idle time directly correlates with the queue build-up at an upstream bottleneck, this confirms starvation caused by poor flow synchronization.
*   **WIP Bullwhip:** By animating the process map over time, we can watch WIP levels pulse and build up at specific work centers, effectively visualizing the bullwhip effect caused by erratic upstream release schedules and localized dispatching rules.

### 3. Root Cause Analysis of Scheduling Ineffectiveness

Delving into the root causes requires differentiating between poor logic, capacity limits, and inherent variability:

*   **Limitations of Static Rules:** The current FCFS/EDD rules are myopic; they do not account for downstream machine loads or setup states. 
*   **Lack of Real-time Visibility:** Dispatching decisions are made locally without a holistic view, leading to starvation or WIP buildup.
*   **Inaccurate Estimations:** Comparing "Task Duration (Planned)" to "Task Duration (Actual)" in the log often reveals systemic underestimation, meaning schedules are fundamentally flawed from the start.
*   **Differentiating Root Causes via Process Mining:**
    *   *Poor Logic vs. Capacity:* If a machine exhibits 100% utilization and an infinite queue, the issue is *Capacity Limitation*. If a machine has 60% utilization but jobs are still late, the issue is *Poor Scheduling Logic* (e.g., jobs are waiting in the wrong queue, or the machine is starved).
    *   *Process Variability:* By calculating the standard deviation of "Setup Duration (Actual)" for the exact same job-to-job transition, we can identify if setups are highly variable (perhaps operator-dependent) or consistent. High variance in task execution time requires a fundamentally different scheduling buffer than low variance.

### 4. Developing Advanced Data-Driven Scheduling Strategies

Informed by the process mining insights, I propose three sophisticated strategies:

#### Strategy 1: Enhanced Multi-Factor Dynamic Dispatching
*   **Core Logic:** Replace static rules with a dynamic scoring algorithm calculated for every job in a machine's queue. A job's priority score is a weighted sum of: (1) Critical Ratio (Time to Due Date / Remaining Processing Time), (2) Job Priority level, and (3) Sequence-Dependent Setup penalty (if selected as the next job).
*   **PM Data Used:** We use historical data to determine the optimal weights for these factors. We use the mined Setup Time Matrix to inform factor (3).
*   **Addresses:** Poor prioritization and setup inflation. It forces the system to balance urgency against the operational efficiency of minimizing setups.
*   **Expected Impact:** Reduced tardiness for urgent jobs, lower average setup times, and improved machine utilization.

#### Strategy 2: Predictive-Adaptive Rolling Horizon Scheduling
*   **Core Logic:** Instead of relying on deterministic planned durations, schedules are generated on a rolling horizon (e.g., every shift) using probabilistic task durations. If predictive maintenance data is available, or derived from MTBF/MTTR calculated from the log, machine unavailability is anticipated and routed around proactively.
*   **PM Data Used:** We extract the empirical distribution of task durations per machine (and even per operator) to build stochastic schedules. We use historical breakdown frequencies to insert dynamic buffer times at highly unreliable machines.
*   **Addresses:** Unpredictable lead times, inaccurate estimations, and inadequate disruption response. By building variance into the schedule, due date promises become statistically reliable.
*   **Expected Impact:** Predictable lead times, lower WIP (due to controlled release rates), and resilient handling of hot jobs.

#### Strategy 3: Bottleneck-Centric Intelligent Batching (Setup Optimization)
*   **Core Logic:** At the identified bottleneck resources, we implement a bounded Traveling Salesperson Problem (TSP) approach. The algorithm looks at the current queue and groups similar part families together to minimize setup times. However, it includes a "slack threshold"—if a job's due date is too close, it breaks the batch to allow the urgent job through.
*   **PM Data Used:** Relies entirely on the historical sequence-dependent setup time matrix mined from the logs to define "distance" between jobs.
*   **Addresses:** Inefficient resource utilization on the most critical machines and high WIP at bottlenecks.
*   **Expected Impact:** Significantly increased throughput at bottlenecks, leading to higher overall shop output and reduced WIP buildup.

### 5. Simulation, Evaluation, and Continuous Improvement

**Simulation and Evaluation:**
Before live deployment, we must build a Discrete-Event Simulation (DES) model of Precision Parts Inc. Crucially, this DES will not use static inputs, but rather be parameterized entirely by the process mining outputs:
*   Routing probabilities derived from the process map.
*   Task duration distributions (not just averages, but full distributions) mined from the log.
*   The sequence-dependent setup matrix.
*   Breakdown frequencies and repair time distributions.

We would test the three proposed strategies against the current baseline under several scenarios: (1) Normal load, (2) Peak seasonal load (high WIP), (3) High-disruption environment (frequent breakdowns and hot jobs), and (4) Mixed product mix. KPIs (Tardiness, WIP, Lead Time, Utilization) would be tracked to select the winning strategy.

**Continuous Monitoring and Adaptation:**
Scheduling is never "set and forget." We would implement a continuous improvement framework:
1.  **Real-time Event Streaming:** Feed live MES data into a process mining dashboard.
2.  **KPI Control Charts:** Track key metrics (e.g., average setup time, bottleneck queue length) on statistical control charts to detect drift.
3.  **Automated Drift Detection:** If the average task duration for a specific machine drifts outside historical statistical bounds, the system triggers an alert. The Predictive-Adaptive Scheduling model is automatically re-trained on recent data to adjust its buffers.
4.  **Quarterly Strategy Reviews:** Re-run the variant and bottleneck analysis quarterly to ensure the chosen dispatching rules and setup matrices still reflect the reality of the shop floor, adjusting weights and part family definitions as product mixes evolve.
