Assumed "canonical" trace order (the order in which activities eventually follow each other in the log):

`<SS, OP, RC, QI, CA, PT, PK, WS, DT, AS>`

The averages below are built from plausible step-by-step lead times (supplier negotiation  3 days, procurement lead time  14 days, inspection  1 day, assembly  2 days, testing  1.5 days, packaging  12 h, hand-over to warehouse  6 h, storage before shipping  3 days, first support event  10 days after delivery). Standard deviations of multi-step pairs are accumulated (root-sum-of-squares), so the further apart two activities are in the flow, the higher the variance — exactly what you expect in a real supply chain.

### How to use it for conformance checking

With a chosen `ZETA` (e.g. `ZETA = 2`), for every pair `(a, b)` observed in a trace with elapsed time `t`:

```python
ZETA = 2

def is_deviation(pair, t, profile=temporal_profile, zeta=ZETA):
    if pair not in profile:
        return False                     # pair never observed in the log
    avg, std = profile[pair]
    return t < avg - zeta * std or t > avg + zeta * std
```

Examples with `ZETA = 2`:
* `('OP', 'RC')` accepted range  `[345600, 2073600]` s (4 – 24 days). A delivery after 40 days  **deviation** (supplier delay).
* `('PK', 'WS')` accepted range  `[7200, 36000]` s (2 – 10 h). A hand-over after 3 days  **deviation** (internal logistics bottleneck).
* `('CA', 'PT')` accepted range  `[43200, 216000]` s. Testing started 5 minutes after assembly  **deviation** (suspiciously fast, likely skipped test steps).
* Long-range pairs such as `('SS', 'DT')` ( 25 ± 5 days  14.5 – 36 days) catch end-to-end cycle-time anomalies even when each individual step looks normal.
