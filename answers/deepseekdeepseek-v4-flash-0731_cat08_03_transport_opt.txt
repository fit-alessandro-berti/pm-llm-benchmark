## 1. Process Discovery and Conformance Checking

### 1.1 Preprocessing and Data Integration

The raw data sources are heterogeneous, so the first task is to build a unified, timestamped event log. In this scenario, the most natural primary case ID is **vehicle-day**, e.g., `V12-20241205`, because the analysis goal is the end-to-end operational day of each vehicle: departing the depot, visiting stops, returning, and handling exceptions. A secondary package-level event log can be created for questions about individual delivery performance, time windows, and failed delivery redeliveries.

I would perform the following preprocessing steps:

- **Clean and harmonise timestamps** from GPS devices, handheld scanners, dispatch systems, and maintenance logs. Clock skew between devices must be corrected using known events, such as “Arrive Customer” scanner events aligning with GPS stationary points.
- **Transform raw GPS tracks into meaningful process activities.** GPS points are not process events by themselves. I would use a stop-detection algorithm: when the vehicle’s speed is zero or very low for more than a defined threshold near a known customer address or depot, generate events such as:
  - `Depart Depot`
  - `Arrive Stop`
  - `Depart Stop`
  - `Arrive Depot`
  - `Low Speed / Traffic Delay`
  - `Unscheduled Stop`
- **Merge scanner events with GPS-derived activities.** Scanner events give precise customer-level activities: `Arrive Customer`, `Delivery Success`, `Delivery Failed`, `Depart Customer`. These can serve as the authoritative stop activities, while GPS data supplies travel times, route distances, and idle periods between stops.
- **Join dispatch data to the actual operations.** The planned route gives each stop an expected sequence number, planned arrival time, customer time window, and package list. Each actual delivery stop can be matched to its planned stop via package ID, location, or route sequence.
- **Integrate maintenance events.** Maintenance records must be mapped to vehicle-days. Unscheduled maintenance that occurs during a shift should appear as an activity such as `Unscheduled Stop / Maintenance` between delivery activities.
- **Enrich the event log with attributes** that support root cause analysis: route ID, driver ID, vehicle age, vehicle type, package count, customer segment, geographic zone, weather conditions, external traffic data, and planned vs actual time window.

Key challenges in this phase:

- **Multiple case granularities:** one vehicle day contains many stops and many packages. A package may be scanned at the depot, loaded into the vehicle, delivered, attempted, or redelivered. Process mining tools such as object-centric process mining can help because they support multiple interacting object types: vehicle, driver, package, route, and stop.
- **Noise and missing data:** GPS drift, lost scanner scans, driver failure to scan at the exact stop, late scanning, and planned routes that are changed during the day all create ambiguity.
- **Distinguishing activities:** low speed in traffic, parking, walking to the door, waiting for a customer, and taking a break can look similar from GPS data alone. Scanner timestamps and contextual information help separate service time from travel and idle time.
- **Planned vs actual route matching:** a driver may visit stops in a different order than planned, making simple sequence matching impossible. Location and package association must be used instead.

### 1.2 Process Discovery

After building the event log, I would use process discovery algorithms such as the **inductive miner**, **heuristic miner**, or **fuzzy miner** to visualise the actual delivery process.

The end-to-end process will likely show a high-level structure like:

`Start Shift -> Route Assigned -> Depart Depot -> [Travel to Stop -> Arrive Customer -> Delivery Success/Failed -> Depart Customer]* -> Arrive Depot -> End Shift`

The discovered model will show:

- The **dominant activities** and their frequencies.
- The **average travel time** between stops.
- The **average service time** at each stop.
- The frequency of **exceptions**, such as failed deliveries, unscheduled stops, and returning to a customer later in the day.

Process variants are especially important in logistics. A variant is a distinct sequence of activities. For example, one variant may be:

`Depart Depot -> Stop 1 Success -> Stop 2 Success -> Stop 3 Failed -> Stop 4 Success -> Stop 3 Again Success -> Arrive Depot`

This variant tells us that a failed delivery caused a return visit, adding time and distance. Another variant may reveal that a driver systematically visits stops in a different order than planned, perhaps because the planned route is inefficient. The process discovery output therefore provides both the **actual route sequence** and the **frequency and cost of exceptional behaviours**.

I would also project GPS data onto maps to visualise spatial process variants: heatmaps of driving routes, traffic congestion points, and delivery stop density.

### 1.3 Conformance Checking

Conformance checking compares the actual event log with the planned route represented as a process model or planned trace. I would construct a **planned model** from the dispatch system: each vehicle-day has an expected sequence of stops, expected departure and arrival times, and expected delivery activities.

Using **alignment-based conformance checking**, I would compute fitness and precision metrics and identify the exact points where the actual process deviates from the planned process.

Deviations to look for include:

- **Sequence deviations:** Stop 5 visited before Stop 3.
- **Skipped stops:** A planned stop is missing because the package could not be delivered and was not revisited.
- **Inserted stops:** An extra stop appears, such as an unscheduled maintenance stop, a fuel stop, or a return delivery attempt.
- **Repeated stops:** A stop appears more than once, usually due to a failed delivery followed by a later successful attempt.
- **Timing deviations:** Arrival at a stop is significantly later than planned; travel time between two stops is much longer than expected; service time is far outside the expected range.
- **Resource deviations:** A different driver or vehicle than planned executes part of the route.
- **Route path deviations:** The GPS path taken between stops differs from the planned shortest or preferred path.

The purpose of conformance checking is not simply to punish drivers for not following the plan. It is to understand whether the plan itself is unrealistic, whether drivers are making local improvements, or whether operational failures are disrupting the plan. For example, if most drivers deviate from the planned stop order in the same way, the dispatch system’s route optimiser is probably producing poor sequences.

## 2. Performance Analysis and Bottleneck Identification

### 2.1 Key Performance Indicators

Relevant KPIs for Speedy Parcels’ goals of punctuality and cost reduction include:

| KPI | Definition | Data Source / Calculation |
|---|---|---|
| On-Time Delivery Rate | Share of delivered packages whose `Delivery Success` timestamp is within the customer-requested time window. | Scanner events joined with dispatch time windows. |
| Failed Delivery Rate | Failed delivery attempts divided by total delivery attempts. | Scanner events where `Delivery Failed` occurs. |
| First-Attempt Success Rate | Deliveries successful on the first attempt divided by total packages delivered. | Scanner history per Package ID. |
| Average Service Time per Stop | From `Arrive Customer` to `Depart Customer`, divided by the number of stops. | Scanner events. |
| Travel Time Ratio | Total travel time divided by total case time. High values mean too much driving and too little productive service. | GPS-derived travel activities. |
| Average Distance per Package | Total GPS distance driven per case divided by number of delivered packages. | GPS coordinates and scanner package counts. |
| Idle Time Ratio | Time with ignition on but no movement and no customer service, divided by total shift time. | GPS status and scanner data. |
| Fuel Consumption per 100 km | Fuel data from telematics or fuel cards divided by GPS distance. If fuel is unavailable, use idling time and average speed as proxies. | Telematics / fuel cards. |
| Traffic Delay Duration | Time spent in low-speed or congested movement, or excess travel time compared with free-flow baseline. | GPS low-speed events and segment travel-time comparisons. |
| Unscheduled Maintenance Frequency | Mid-shift breakdowns or maintenance stops per 1,000 km. | Maintenance logs and GPS `Unscheduled Stop` events. |
| Vehicle Utilization Rate | Active delivery time divided by total shift time; also volume/weight of packages loaded vs vehicle capacity. | Dispatch and GPS data. |
| Overtime | Difference between actual `End Shift` and planned shift end. | Driver scanner and dispatch system. |

These KPIs can be calculated by adding derived attributes to the event log and then aggregating across cases, drivers, routes, time periods, or vehicle types.

### 2.2 Bottleneck Identification Techniques

To identify bottlenecks, I would use several complementary process mining techniques:

- **Dotted Chart Analysis:** Plot every event over time. This immediately reveals periods of low activity, overnight gaps, and clusters of long stop durations.
- **Performance Spectrum / Bottleneck Heatmap:** Visualise where cases slow down over time. For example, a bottleneck may appear every morning on the same road segment between 08:00 and 09:30.
- **Activity Performance Analysis:** Calculate the average, median, and standard deviation of durations for each activity. Travel and service time will naturally be high, but the variance matters. A high standard deviation in service time suggests that some stops are causing unpredictable delays.
- **Variant Analysis:** Compare high-performing and low-performing cases. If the fastest variants use a different stop order, the planned order is likely suboptimal. If slow variants contain failed deliveries, then failed attempts are a major bottleneck.
- **Segment-Level Travel Time Analysis:** For each pair of consecutive stops, compute the actual travel time from GPS data. Then correlate travel time with time of day, day of week, and geographic zone. This quantifies how much traffic congestion affects the operation.
- **Resource Perspective Analysis:** Group cases by driver and vehicle. This reveals whether some drivers or vehicles are systematically slower, use more fuel, or cause more maintenance events, after controlling for route difficulty.
- **Queueing and Waiting Time Analysis:** Differentiate between driving, walking, waiting for the customer, searching for parking, and performing the scan. The scanner `Arrive Customer` likely happens after parking; the GPS stop may begin earlier. The gap between GPS stop start and scanner `Arrive Customer` is an important non-productive waiting or parking time.

The impact of a bottleneck can be quantified by calculating the **excess time cost**. For example, if a particular traffic hotspot causes an extra 20 minutes per vehicle per day across 25 vehicles, that is 500 minutes per day, or roughly 8.3 hours of lost productive time daily. Multiply by the loaded cost of a driver and vehicle per hour to express this in monetary terms.

## 5. Considering Operational Constraints and Monitoring

### 5.1 Operational Constraints

All proposed strategies must respect real-world operational limits.

- **Driver working hours:** Dynamic routing and territory redesign must ensure that planned and revised routes fit within legal and contractual driver hours, including breaks. If a route cannot be finished within the shift, the optimisation should either reduce the number of stops, reassign work, or add a second driver or vehicle. - **Customer time windows:** Re-sequencing deliveries must respect hard time windows. If a customer has requested delivery between 10:00 and 12:00, the route optimiser must ensure the predicted arrival time falls in that window. Where this is impossible, the system should identify the affected customer and attempt to notify or renegotiate in advance.
- **Maintenance schedules:** Predictive maintenance must be planned during off-peak periods, ideally overnight or on low-volume days, so vehicles do not go offline during peak delivery hours. The maintenance strategy must also account for spare-vehicle availability.
- **Failed deliveries:** A failed delivery cannot simply be “added to the next route” without considering whether the customer will actually be available at the new time. The failed-delivery strategy must include a customer confirmation or automatic routing to a pickup point before scheduling a redelivery.

- **KPI trend charts:** On-time delivery rate, first-attempt success rate, failed delivery rate, average service time, travel/time ratio, fuel per 100 km, unscheduled maintenance frequency, and overtime hours.
- **Control charts:** Statistical process control charts for critical KPIs can identify whether a change in performance is a real shift or random variation. For example, if the average delivery cycle time increases by two standard deviations in a week, an alert should be triggered.
- **Process variant distribution:** Track how often routes follow the planned sequence versus known alternative variants. A sudden rise in non-adherence may indicate a new problem, such as a missing customer service process or a failing route plan.
- **Bottleneck heatmaps:** Re-run the performance spectrum and GIS heatmap analyses to confirm that traffic congestion hotspots are shifting or being avoided. If a new bottleneck emerges, it can be investigated immediately.
- **Conformance reports:** Continue to compare actual routes with planned routes. After dynamic routing is introduced, the “planned route” becomes more flexible, so the dashboard should compare actual behaviour against the agreed operational policy rather than a rigid stop order.
- **Resource performance view:** Monitor driver and vehicle performance. Ensure that driver coaching is improving performance and that predictive maintenance is truly reducing unscheduled stops.
- **Outcome-based alerts:** For example:
  - On-time delivery below target in a specific postcode.
  - Failed delivery rate above 10% for a particular time window.
  - Fuel consumption per km increasing for a subset of vehicles.
  - Overtime exceeding legal limits on any route.

Finally, I would implement a structured improvement loop:

1. **Monitor** the current process from fresh event logs.
2. **Discover** whether the process is changing.
3. **Check conformance** to the intended operating model.
4. **Analyse** emerging bottlenecks and root causes.
5. **Improve** the operational strategy through targeted actions.
6. **Repeat** continuously.

This ensures that Speedy Parcels not only finds immediate data-driven improvements but also builds the capability to sustain and further improve delivery punctuality and cost efficiency over time.
