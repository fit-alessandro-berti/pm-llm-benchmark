Therefore, cases **2002, 2003, and 2005** should be considered performance outliers, with **2005** the largest problem.

## 2. Where the time is being spent

### Case 2002

The key delays are:

- Evaluate Claim to Request Additional Documents: **4 hours 15 minutes**
- Request Documents to Approve Claim: **20 hours**
- Approval to payment: only **45 minutes**

This case has only one document request, but there is a long wait between the request and approval. The delay may reflect waiting for the claimant's documents, an approval queue, or overnight processing.

### Case 2003

The main delays are:

- Two document requests, separated by **6 hours**
- Final document request to approval: **23 hours**
- Approval to payment: **17 hours**

This indicates both a documentation problem and possible internal approval/payment queues. The case is high complexity and requires repeated intervention by the adjuster.

### Case 2005

This is the clearest example of a documentation-driven delay:

- Three document requests
- First-to-second request interval: **29 hours 30 minutes**
- Second-to-third request interval: **22 hours**
- Final request to approval: **19 hours**
- Approval to payment: **4 hours**

The repeated requests create a loop in the process. Much of the total duration occurs before approval, suggesting incomplete information, slow claimant responses, repeated review cycles, or a lack of a consolidated documentation request.

## 3. Attribute analysis

### Complexity

Complexity is the strongest apparent contributor.

- Both **low-complexity** cases, 2001 and 2004, complete quickly and require no additional-document request.
- The **medium-complexity** case, 2002, requires one request and takes approximately one day.
- Both **high-complexity** cases, 2003 and 2005, require multiple requests and take two to three days.

This suggests that high-complexity claims are more likely to have:

- Incomplete or difficult-to-verify information
- More complicated policy or damage assessments
- More review iterations
- Greater need for manager approval
- Longer payment or quality-control checks

The data strongly supports complexity as a leading root cause, although the sample is small.

### Resource

The resource data suggests possible bottlenecks, but it does not prove that any individual resource is responsible.

#### Adjusters

- **Adjuster_Mike** handles a fast low-complexity case, 2001, and a slow high-complexity case, 2003.
- **Adjuster_Lisa** handles a fast low-complexity case, 2004, but also the delayed medium- and high-complexity cases, 2002 and 2005.

Since the same adjusters handle both fast and slow cases depending on complexity, the evidence points more toward **case complexity and documentation requirements** than adjuster performance alone. However, the repeated requests in cases 2003 and 2005 could indicate that the current review process or workload is causing inefficient document handling.

#### Managers

- **Manager_Ann** approves the two fast low-complexity cases, but also handles case 2002, which has a 20-hour wait before approval.
- **Manager_Bill** appears only in the two high-complexity cases, both of which have long approval waits.

This may indicate that high-complexity claims are routed to a smaller or more specialized approval pool. Manager_Bill may also have a larger queue, but this cannot be separated from the fact that both of Bill's cases are high complexity.

#### Finance resources

Payment is fast for most cases, but slower in the more complex cases:

- Case 2003: approval to payment takes **17 hours**
- Case 2005: approval to payment takes **4 hours**
- Low-complexity cases take only 15–30 minutes

This could reflect additional financial checks, payment batching, claim-value thresholds, or a finance queue for complex claims. It is probably a secondary bottleneck rather than the primary cause, since most of the delay occurs before payment.

### Region

There is no clear standalone regional effect.

- Region A contains one fast case, 2001, and one very slow case, 2003.
- Region B contains one fast case, 2004, and two slow cases, 2002 and 2005.

Region B may have additional delays related to staffing, local documentation requirements, or claimant response times, but the fast case 2004 shows that Region B is not inherently slow. The apparent regional effect is confounded with complexity and resource assignment.

A more cautious conclusion is:

> Region B may amplify delays for medium- and high-complexity claims, but the current data is insufficient to establish region as an independent root cause.

## 4. Likely root causes

The most plausible explanation is a combination of the following:

1. **High claim complexity**
   - Complex claims require more investigation and verification.
   - They are more likely to need additional documents and management review.

2. **Incomplete documentation at intake**
   - Cases 2003 and 2005 enter repeated document-request loops.
   - Requests appear to be made sequentially rather than consolidated.

3. **Waiting for claimant responses or document processing**
   - Large gaps occur between document requests.
   - The log does not distinguish between time waiting for the customer and time waiting for internal processing.

4. **Approval queues for complex claims**
   - Long gaps remain after the final document request and before approval.
   - High-complexity claims may be routed to a limited number of managers.

5. **Potential finance queue or additional payment checks**
   - Case 2003 has a 17-hour approval-to-payment delay.
   - This may reflect payment batching or additional financial controls.

6. **Possible regional or workload differences**
   - Region B's high-complexity case is the slowest, but the data does not isolate region from complexity and resource effects.

## 5. Recommended mitigations

### Improve document handling

- Perform an upfront document completeness check during submission.
- Provide the claimant with a single, comprehensive checklist rather than issuing multiple requests.
- Use online document upload, automated validation, and OCR where possible.
- Send automatic reminders and escalation notifications for outstanding documents.
- Record when documents are sent, received, and validated separately.

### Use complexity-based routing

- Route high-complexity claims directly to specialized adjusters.
- Apply a risk-based process so low-complexity claims can use straight-through or simplified approval.
- Set separate service-level targets for low-, medium-, and high-complexity claims.

### Reduce approval bottlenecks

- Establish approval SLAs for managers.
- Create a backup approver or shared approval pool.
- Automatically escalate claims that remain pending beyond the SLA.
- Consider delegated approval limits for routine or low-risk claims.

### Improve resource and regional capacity management

- Monitor case volumes and queues by adjuster, manager, finance resource, and region.
- Balance work across qualified resources rather than routing all complex claims to a small group.
- Provide cross-region support during periods of high workload.
- Compare resource performance only after controlling for complexity and case type.

### Improve performance monitoring

Track the following separately:

- Time spent waiting for customer documents
- Internal adjuster processing time
- Manager approval queue time
- Finance payment queue time
- Number and reason for each document request
- Case duration by complexity, region, and resource

The current log shows strong evidence that **complexity and repeated documentation requests** drive the longest cases. Resource and regional effects should be investigated further, but they should not be treated as confirmed root causes without a larger dataset and more detailed waiting-time events.
