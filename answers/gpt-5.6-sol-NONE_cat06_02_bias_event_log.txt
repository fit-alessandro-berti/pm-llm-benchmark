## Where bias appears

The clearest source of potential bias is the **explicit `+10 (Community)` score adjustment** given to members of the **Highland Civic Darts Club**:

- **C001:** 710  720, approved  
- **C004:** 690  700, approved  
- Applicants without this affiliation receive no adjustment.

This makes `CommunityGroup` a decision-relevant attribute even though club membership may have little or no legitimate relationship to creditworthiness.

## Attributes that appear to favor certain groups

### 1. Community affiliation

Membership in one named organization produces a direct scoring advantage. This creates unequal treatment between otherwise similar applicants:

- **C001** begins at 710 and reaches 720 through club membership.
- **C002**, with no affiliation, begins and remains at 720.
- Both are approved, but C001 needed the affiliation bonus to achieve the same final score.
- **C004** moves from 690 to 700 and is approved, suggesting the bonus may be decisive if 700 is an approval threshold.

Someone with the same financial profile as C004 but no club membership could remain at 690 and potentially be rejected.

The policy could also create **indirect or proxy discrimination** if access to the club correlates with characteristics such as neighborhood, socioeconomic status, ethnicity, age, social networks, or other protected traits.

### 2. Local-resident status

`LocalResident` also appears associated with outcomes:

- All three local residents—C001, C002, and C004—are approved.
- Of the two nonresidents, C005 is approved and C003 is rejected.

This small log does not prove that residency directly changes the score, because no explicit residency adjustment is shown and C003 has a lower score than C005. Nevertheless, residency is retained throughout the process and may interact with community membership: both club-affiliated applicants are local residents. It should therefore be tested as a possible direct factor or geographic proxy.

## Fairness implications

The process favors **social or geographic access**, not merely underlying creditworthiness. Applicants lacking the favored affiliation cannot earn the same bonus, even when their base scores are comparable:

- C001’s base score of 710 is lower than C003’s 715, yet C001 receives a bonus and is approved while C003 is rejected.
- Their residency statuses also differ, so the log cannot isolate whether community membership, residency, another rule, or their interaction caused the outcome.
- C004’s approval at an adjusted 700 suggests the club bonus may alter outcomes near the cutoff.

This raises concerns under several fairness concepts:

- **Individual fairness:** similarly creditworthy applicants may be treated differently due to club membership.
- **Group fairness:** groups less likely to belong to the club may have lower approval rates.
- **Equal opportunity:** access to approval may depend partly on access to a particular social institution.
- **Transparency:** the final decision is attributed to the Rules Engine, but the log does not disclose thresholds or explain why C003 was rejected at 715 while adjusted scores of 700 and 720 were approved.

Manual review does not appear to correct the issue. Every case is reviewed, but the community bonus persists unchanged, and the final decision is automated. Thus, review may merely ratify the biased adjustment rather than mitigate it.

## Important limitation

There are only five cases, so this is evidence of a **potentially discriminatory rule**, not sufficient statistical proof of disparate impact. The final outcome may depend on variables omitted from the log. Still, an explicit bonus for a named civic-club affiliation warrants immediate scrutiny.

## Recommended checks

1. Remove or justify the community bonus using evidence that it predicts repayment risk and is legally permissible.
2. Compare outcomes using **base scores only** versus adjusted scores, especially around the apparent threshold.
3. Audit approval rates and error rates by residency, community affiliation, and relevant protected groups.
4. Test whether club membership or residency acts as a proxy for race, ethnicity, income, age, disability, or neighborhood.
5. Document all decision rules and reason codes, particularly why C003 was rejected at 715 while C004 was approved at 700 adjusted.
6. Ensure manual reviewers can identify and reverse unjustified affinity-based adjustments.

Overall, the strongest bias signal is the **preferential community-membership adjustment**, with local residency presenting an additional potential geographic or proxy-bias concern.
