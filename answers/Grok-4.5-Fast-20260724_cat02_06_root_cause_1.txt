**Significantly longer cases**: **102, 104, and especially 105**.  
Average of the two fast cases (101 + 103) ≈ 1 h 45 min. The three long cases are 14–28× longer.

### 2. Potential Root Causes

**A. Presence of escalations (strongest correlate)**  
- Cases 102 and 105 both contain “Escalate to Level-2 Agent”.  
- 102: Escalation at 11:30 → Investigate only at 14:00 → Resolve next morning.  
- 105: Short Level-1 investigation (09:10–10:00), then escalation → next Investigate only on 2024-03-02 14:00 → Resolve on 2024-03-03.  
- Case 104 has **no** escalation yet is still long → escalations are a major but not the sole driver.

**B. Long waiting times / idle periods between activities**  
- **104** (no escalation):  
  - Assign (09:30) → Investigate (13:00) = **3.5 h idle**.  
  - Investigate (13:00) → Resolve (next day 08:00) = **~19 h**.  
- **102**: After escalation, ~2.5 h until Investigate, then overnight until Resolve.  
- **105**: After escalation, **~28 h** until the next Investigate step, then another overnight until Resolve.  
- Fast cases (101, 103) show almost continuous activity with gaps of only 10–45 minutes.

**C. Delays before or during investigation/resolution**  
- In long cases the bulk of time is spent **waiting** rather than actively working.  
- Level-1 agents sometimes appear to hold tickets without progress (104) or quickly escalate without resolution (105).  
- Overnight / multi-day gaps suggest limited Level-2 capacity, hand-off friction, or tickets sitting in queues outside business hours.

### 3. How These Factors Increase Cycle Time & Recommendations

**How the factors drive longer cycle times**  
- Escalation introduces an extra hand-off and queue. If Level-2 capacity is constrained, tickets wait many hours or overnight.  
- Large idle gaps (especially post-assignment or post-escalation) indicate either resource shortages, poor prioritization, or missing SLAs/alerts that would force progress.  
- The combination produces multiplicative delay: a ticket that could be resolved in <2 h stretches to 1–2 days once it enters a slow queue.

**Insights & recommendations**  
1. **Escalation process**  
   - Define clear, measurable criteria for when to escalate (and when Level-1 should keep working).  
   - Track Level-2 queue time and set an SLA (e.g., first response < 2–4 h during business hours).  
   - Consider a “warm hand-off” or shared workspace so Level-2 sees context immediately.

2. **Reduce idle time**  
   - Implement automatic aging/alerting on tickets that remain in “Assigned” or “Escalated” status beyond a threshold (e.g., 1–2 h).  
   - Ensure Level-1 agents have enough capacity or decision support so tickets do not sit un-investigated for hours (as in case 104).

3. **Visibility & prioritization**  
   - Real-time dashboard showing tickets approaching or exceeding target cycle time.  
   - Prioritize escalated or aging tickets higher in Level-2 queues.

4. **Further data collection**  
   - Capture agent IDs, ticket severity/priority, and reason codes for escalation.  
   - Measure working time vs. waiting time separately so capacity problems can be distinguished from process problems.

In short, the dominant performance issues are **escalations combined with long post-escalation (and sometimes pre-investigation) waiting times**. Addressing queue management and hand-off friction for escalated tickets, plus tighter control of idle periods after assignment, should materially reduce the long-tail cycle times observed in cases 102, 104, and 105.
