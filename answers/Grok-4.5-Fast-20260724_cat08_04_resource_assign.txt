**1. Analyzing Resource Behavior and Assignment Patterns**

Process mining starts from the event log (Case ID, Activity, Timestamp, Resource/Agent ID, Agent Tier, Agent Skills, Ticket Priority, Ticket Category, Required Skill, Timestamp Type, Notes). After standard preprocessing (filtering incomplete cases, consolidating START/COMPLETE pairs into durations, enriching with derived attributes such as “skill match flag”, “handover count”, “time-to-assignment”), the following analyses are performed.

**Agent- and tier-level performance metrics**  
- Workload distribution: number of active cases, total handling time, and concurrent open tickets per agent and per tier over daily/weekly windows.  
- Activity processing times: mean, median and 95th-percentile duration of “Work L1/L2/L3” activities, segmented by Priority, Category and Required Skill.  
- First-call resolution (FCR) rate for L1: percentage of tickets closed by the same L1 agent without escalation.  
- Skill-specific volume: frequency with which each agent (or tier) handles tickets whose Required Skill matches one of the agent’s documented skills.  
- Reassignment and escalation rates per agent and per originating tier.

These metrics are obtained via process mining resource dashboards and statistical aggregation on the event log.

**Revealing actual assignment patterns**  
- Resource interaction / social-network analysis: directed handover graph where nodes are agents (or agent–skill pairs) and edges are weighted by the number of “Escalate”, “Reassign” or “Assign” transitions. High-degree nodes and dense clusters identify informal “go-to” specialists and frequent ping-pong paths.  
- Role discovery (organizational mining): automatic clustering of agents based on the set of activities and ticket attributes they actually perform, revealing whether the declared L1/L2/L3 tiers match observed behaviour.  
- Directly-follows and variant graphs filtered by Resource and Required Skill expose the real routing logic (e.g., “P2 Network tickets almost always escalate from L1 to the same three L2 agents regardless of current load”).

Comparison with the intended logic (round-robin within tier + manual escalation) is performed by overlaying the discovered model on a normative model that encodes the stated rules; deviations appear as “unexpected” handovers or skill mismatches.

**Skill utilisation analysis**  
A skill-match matrix is built: for every “Work \ldots Start” event the Required Skill is compared with the agent’s skill list. Metrics include:  
- percentage of work performed by specialists on tickets below their skill level,  
- percentage of tickets whose Required Skill was never matched by any assigned agent,  
- average skill “over-qualification” score.  

Heat-maps and Pareto charts quickly show whether scarce skills (e.g., Networking-Firewall, Security-IAM) are diluted on routine P3/P4 work.

**2. Identifying Resource-Related Bottlenecks and Issues**

Bottlenecks are localised by combining performance metrics with process-mining filters:  

- Skill scarcity: queue-time distribution for tickets that require a rare skill, conditioned on the number of available agents possessing that skill. Long waiting times after “Assign L2/L3” that coincide with zero idle specialists constitute a clear bottleneck.  
- Reassignment/escalation delays: for every reassignment or escalation event the time from previous “Work End” to next “Work Start” is measured. Average extra delay per reassignment (commonly 30–90 min in such logs) and the cumulative contribution to overall cycle time are calculated.  
- Incorrect initial assignment: cases in which the first L1 or L2 agent’s skills do not intersect the Required Skill show systematically higher reassignment rates and longer total resolution times.  
- Over-/under-utilisation: agents whose average concurrent cases or total handling time deviate >2σ from the tier mean are flagged; correlation with SLA-breach flags quantifies impact.  
- SLA linkage: survival analysis or logistic regression on the event log attributes shows the odds ratio of SLA breach given “number of reassignments >1”, “skill mismatch on first assignment”, or “assignment to overloaded agent”.

Typical quantified findings (derived from analogous ITSM logs) are: 25–40 % of P2/P3 SLA breaches are preceded by ≥2 reassignments; average delay per unnecessary reassignment ≈45 min; 15–20 % of L3 specialist time is spent on tickets that historically were resolved by L2 agents with matching skills.

**3. Root Cause Analysis for Assignment Inefficiencies**

Root causes are diagnosed by combining process-mining techniques with domain knowledge:  

- Assignment-rule deficiencies: pure round-robin ignores both skill match and current workload; the discovered handover network shows systematic deviation from the normative model.  
- Incomplete skill profiles: agents frequently handle tickets outside their recorded skills, or specialists receive tickets that do not require their expertise—evidence of stale or coarse-grained skill data.  
- Poor ticket categorisation: high reassignment rates after the first “Work” activity, especially when Required Skill changes mid-case, point to inaccurate initial classification.  
- Lack of real-time visibility: long “Assign” waiting times even when suitable agents are idle (detectable by comparing agent availability derived from the log with assignment timestamps).  
- Insufficient L1 empowerment: low FCR rates and high escalation volumes for ticket types that a minority of L1 agents successfully close indicate training or knowledge-base gaps.

**Variant analysis** isolates the 10–15 % of cases with zero reassignments and short cycle times versus the multi-reassignment variants. Attribute comparison (priority, category, required skill, initial agent, day-of-week, etc.) surfaces the decision points that separate good from bad paths. **Decision mining** (or predictive process monitoring models trained on the log) further identifies the combinations of ticket attributes and agent features that most strongly predict subsequent reassignment or SLA breach, providing concrete evidence for rule redesign.

**4. Developing Data-Driven Resource Assignment Strategies**

Three concrete strategies grounded in the mined insights:

**Strategy A – Skill- and proficiency-weighted routing**  
- Addresses: skill mismatches and specialist under-/over-utilisation.  
- Leverages: the skill-match matrix and role-discovery results that quantify actual proficiency (frequency and success rate per skill).  
- Data required: up-to-date agent skill inventory enriched with proficiency scores derived from historical resolution success and handling-time distributions; real-time agent availability.  
- Expected benefits: 30–50 % reduction in skill-mismatch reassignments, higher specialist utilisation on high-value work, measurable improvement in FCR and mean resolution time.

**Strategy B – Workload- and queue-aware assignment engine**  
- Addresses: uneven load and delays caused by assigning to already overloaded agents.  
- Leverages: the workload-distribution and concurrent-case metrics that identified chronic overload patterns.  
- Data required: live event stream of open tickets per agent, predicted remaining handling time (from process-mining performance models), and SLA urgency.  
- Expected benefits: balanced utilisation (reduction of >2σ outliers), shorter queue times for P2 tickets, fewer SLA breaches linked to agent overload.

**Strategy C – Predictive initial routing + refined L1 escalation criteria**  
- Addresses: incorrect first assignments and excessive escalations.  
- Leverages: decision-mining models and variant analysis that identify ticket features predictive of required skill and of successful L1 closure.  
- Data required: ticket description embeddings or keyword/category features, historical success probabilities per (ticket type × agent skill set), continuously updated from the event log.  
- Expected benefits: higher first-assignment accuracy, 15–25 % reduction in escalations for trainable ticket types, improved L1 FCR and specialist capacity freed for true complex work.

(Optional complementary tactic: dynamic tier rebalancing—temporarily moving cross-trained agents between L1 and L2 queues when mined demand patterns show sustained skill shortages.)

**5. Simulation, Implementation, and Monitoring**

**Business-process simulation**  
A discrete-event simulation model is built from:  
- the mined process model (including probabilities of escalation/reassignment paths),  
- empirical activity-duration distributions per (tier × skill × priority),  
- resource calendars and skill matrices extracted from the log.  

Alternative assignment policies (Strategies A–C) are plugged in as routing logic. Monte-Carlo runs under current and projected ticket-arrival volumes quantify expected reductions in mean cycle time, reassignment count, SLA-breach rate and specialist idle/overload time. Sensitivity analysis on skill-profile accuracy and arrival-rate peaks guides implementation priorities and change-management effort.

**Post-implementation monitoring**  
A process-mining operational dashboard is configured with continuous replay of the live event stream. Key resource-related KPIs and views:  

- Skill-match rate on first assignment and overall.  
- Average and 95th-percentile queue time after each Assign/Escalate activity, segmented by Required Skill.  
- Reassignment and escalation counts per case and per originating agent.  
- Workload balance index (coefficient of variation of concurrent cases or handling time across agents within a tier).  
- L1 FCR rate and specialist time spent on “below-skill” tickets.  
- SLA compliance stratified by number of reassignments and by skill-match flag.  
- Social-network view of handovers (updated weekly) to detect re-emergence of informal bottlenecks.  

Threshold alerts trigger root-cause drills (variant comparison, decision-point analysis) so that the assignment logic can be iteratively refined. Quarterly full process-mining discovery cycles ensure the models stay aligned with evolving ticket mixes and agent skill sets.

This closed-loop approach—discover actual behaviour, quantify impact, redesign with data, simulate, deploy, and continuously monitor—turns the event log into a living control system for resource assignment and systematically reduces SLA breaches, reassignments and skill misallocation.
