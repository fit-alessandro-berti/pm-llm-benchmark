A stronger design would shift the process from static branching and late exception handling to **early predictive classification, straight-through processing, and adaptive case management**.

## 1. Proposed high-level redesign

```text
Start
  -> Automated Intake, Data Validation, and Enrichment
  -> Predictive Triage:
       classify request, estimate complexity/risk, predict customization,
       approval probability, expected duration, and required skills

  -> Gateway: Is information complete?
       No -> Automated clarification / customer self-service
             -> if unresolved, route to intake specialist
       Yes -> continue

  -> Gateway: Routing confidence and complexity
       High-confidence standard request
          -> Straight-through standard-processing subprocess
       Likely custom / complex request
          -> Adaptive custom-request subprocess
       Low-confidence or exceptional request
          -> Human triage / case-management subprocess

  -> Policy-based approval orchestration
  -> Invoice/quotation generation
  -> Customer confirmation
  -> End

Event-driven exception handling and continuous SLA monitoring apply throughout.
```

## 2. Changes by process area

### Task A: Receive Customer Request

Replace manual intake with an **omnichannel automated intake service** covering portals, email, APIs, chat, and assisted channels.

Possible automation:

- Extract structured fields from documents and messages using OCR/NLP.
- Validate customer, product, address, contract, and mandatory-field data.
- Enrich the request with CRM history, prior quotations, payment behavior, inventory, and account tier.
- Detect duplicates and known request patterns.
- Give customers immediate feedback when information is missing.
- Create a reusable case record rather than passing documents between tasks.

Add an XOR gateway: **“Is the request complete and valid?”**

- **Yes:** proceed to predictive triage.
- **No, automatically resolvable:** request missing information through a self-service form.
- **No, not automatically resolvable:** assign an intake specialist.
- Use a timer event to send reminders or close abandoned requests.

This reduces downstream rework and improves data quality, although document extraction and customer-data integration require governance and monitoring.

---

### Replace “Check Request Type” with predictive triage

A binary standard/custom gateway is too rigid. Introduce a decision service that produces:

- Probability that customization is required.
- Complexity and expected handling time.
- Feasibility risk.
- Approval and rejection probability.
- Revenue, margin, urgency, and customer-value indicators.
- Skills, systems, and teams likely to be needed.
- Confidence in the prediction.

Add a gateway: **“What is the routing outcome?”**

1. **High-confidence standard:** straight-through processing.
2. **Likely custom:** custom case subprocess.
3. **Low confidence:** human triage.
4. **Known exception or high risk:** specialist review.
5. **Fast-track eligible:** expedited lane, subject to capacity and policy.

Prediction should support—not silently replace—business rules. Hard constraints such as regulatory restrictions, credit policy, and product compatibility should remain deterministic. Low-confidence predictions should trigger human review, and model outcomes should be monitored for drift and bias.

## 3. Standard-request subprocess

### Task B1: Perform Standard Validation

Convert standard validation into a **business-rules and decision-management service**.

It can automatically check:

- Customer and contract eligibility.
- Product configuration.
- Pricing and discount rules.
- Regulatory constraints.
- Address and delivery restrictions.
- Required supporting documents.

Only failed or ambiguous rules should create human work items. The result should include explicit reason codes so employees and customers understand exceptions.

### Parallel checks: Credit and Inventory

Retain parallel execution, but use asynchronous service calls rather than waiting synchronously.

#### Task C1: Credit Check

Automate checks against internal exposure, payment history, credit limits, fraud signals, and external credit providers. Introduce risk bands:

- Low risk: automatic clearance.
- Medium risk: revised payment terms or targeted review.
- High risk: credit specialist review or rejection.

#### Task C2: Inventory Check

Extend this beyond current stock:

- Available-to-promise and capable-to-promise calculations.
- Incoming supply and supplier lead times.
- Substitution and alternate-location options.
- Reservation of scarce stock where appropriate.
- Demand and replenishment forecasts.

The parallel join should have a timeout and escalation policy. One unavailable system should not indefinitely block the entire process. A boundary timer can route the case to a fallback check or operations specialist.

### Task D: Calculate Delivery Date

Replace static delivery-date calculation with a predictive promise-date service that considers:

- Current and projected capacity.
- Inventory and replenishment dates.
- Supplier and carrier reliability.
- Historical processing times.
- Geography, seasonality, and disruption risk.
- Confidence intervals rather than an unrealistically precise date.

If the requested date is unlikely, the system can propose alternatives automatically: partial delivery, substitute products, premium shipping, or a revised date.

## 4. Custom-request subprocess

The custom path should become an **adaptive case-management subprocess**, since non-standard requests do not always follow the same sequence.

### Task B2: Perform Custom Feasibility Analysis

Decompose feasibility into parallel or conditionally activated assessments:

- Technical feasibility.
- Commercial and margin assessment.
- Supply-chain and capacity assessment.
- Legal, security, or compliance review.
- Delivery-risk assessment.

A request should invoke only the relevant assessments. For example, a pricing-only customization should not automatically wait for engineering.

Automated support could include:

- Similarity matching against previous custom requests.
- Retrieval of prior designs, estimates, and outcomes.
- Configurator or constraint-solving tools.
- Predicted engineering effort and delivery risk.
- Suggested specialists based on skill, workload, and past performance.

### Dynamic resource allocation

Introduce a resource-orchestration service that assigns custom work based on:

- Required skills and certifications.
- Current workload and queue age.
- SLA risk and customer priority.
- Location and working hours.
- Estimated effort.
- Conflict-of-interest and segregation-of-duties rules.

The orchestrator should periodically rebalance unstarted work. It can also split independent assessments across specialists. To avoid instability, work already in progress should be reassigned only under defined escalation conditions.

Add gateway: **“Can the request be handled from a known pattern?”**

- **Yes:** instantiate a reusable customization template.
- **Partly:** automate known components and send only novel issues to specialists.
- **No:** create an exploratory expert case.

### “Is Customization Feasible?” gateway

Replace the simple yes/no result with:

- Feasible as requested.
- Feasible with modifications.
- Feasible at added cost or longer lead time.
- More information required.
- Not feasible.
- Requires customer co-design.

This prevents potentially valuable requests from being rejected when alternatives exist.

### Task E1: Prepare Custom Quotation

Automate quotation assembly using:

- Configured bill of materials or service components.
- Estimated labor and engineering effort.
- Margin floors and risk contingencies.
- Contract-specific pricing.
- Predicted delivery date.
- Quote version control and validity periods.

Introduce an optional **customer collaboration subprocess** for interactive configuration, alternatives, and acceptance. This shortens repeated email cycles and preserves a clear audit trail.

### Task E2: Send Rejection Notice

Replace a generic rejection with an outcome-management task that:

- Explains the reason in customer-appropriate language.
- Offers feasible alternatives where possible.
- Routes high-value rejected requests to a commercial review.
- Captures structured rejection reasons for future analysis.

## 5. Approval redesign

### “Is Approval Needed?”

Turn this into a policy-based decision service evaluated earlier and continuously, not only after all processing.

Approval factors could include:

- Discount and margin.
- Credit exposure.
- Customization risk.
- Contract deviation.
- Legal or regulatory impact.
- Delivery-date confidence.
- Customer tier.
- Total value.

Use multiple outcomes:

- No approval required.
- Automatic approval.
- Single manager approval.
- Specialist approval.
- Parallel approvals.
- Sequential executive approval.

Low-risk requests should pass straight through. Approval requirements should be visible early so approvers can be pre-notified while other work continues.

### Task F: Obtain Manager Approval

Use a unified approval inbox with mobile support, summarized evidence, recommendation, risk indicators, and explicit reason codes. Add:

- SLA timers.
- Delegation and out-of-office handling.
- Load-balanced approver assignment.
- Automatic escalation.
- Batch handling for low-complexity requests.

Machine learning may recommend approval, but high-impact decisions should remain explainable and subject to policy controls.

### Approval rejection and re-evaluation

The current loop back to quotation or delivery calculation can create uncontrolled cycles. Replace it with a structured **remediation subprocess**:

1. Record the rejection reason.
2. Identify which elements must change.
3. Recalculate only affected outputs.
4. Ask the customer for acceptance if terms changed.
5. Resubmit only to necessary approvers.

Add:

- Maximum iteration count.
- Timer escalation.
- Material-change rules.
- Manual case review after repeated failure.

This avoids recomputing unrelated work and prevents endless loops.

## 6. Invoice and confirmation

### Task G: Generate Final Invoice

In many commercial processes, the pre-acceptance artifact should be a quotation, order confirmation, or pro forma invoice rather than a final invoice. The process should distinguish these explicitly.

Automate generation through ERP integration, but first run a consistency check across:

- Accepted configuration.
- Approved price.
- Taxes and currency.
- Payment terms.
- Delivery commitments.
- Customer and legal-entity data.

An XOR gateway can route mismatches to finance operations rather than issuing an incorrect document.

### Task I: Send Confirmation to Customer

Make confirmation event-driven and personalized:

- Send through the customer’s preferred channel.
- Include approved scope, price, expected date, and tracking information.
- Provide a self-service status page.
- Notify proactively when delay probability crosses a threshold.
- Capture acceptance, questions, and satisfaction feedback.

For custom work, confirmation may also trigger e-signature, deposit payment, or a customer approval event before fulfillment begins.

## 7. Cross-cutting subprocesses

### SLA and exception monitoring

Create an event subprocess active throughout the case:

- Predict SLA breaches from queue length and remaining effort.
- Escalate or reassign at-risk work.
- Notify customers before promised dates are missed.
- Handle system outages and failed integrations.
- Record manual overrides and root causes.

### Human-in-the-loop controls

Automation should use confidence thresholds:

- High confidence and low risk: automatic execution.
- Medium confidence: employee verification.
- Low confidence or high impact: specialist decision.

Employees should see the data and rationale used by automated services and be able to override recommendations with reason codes.

### Process and model feedback

Capture:

- Actual request type and complexity.
- Prediction accuracy.
- Rework and rejection reasons.
- Approval decisions.
- Actual effort and delivery time.
- Customer acceptance and satisfaction.
- Override outcomes.

Use this information for process mining, rule refinement, staffing forecasts, and model retraining.

## 8. Expected effects

### Turnaround time

Likely improvements come from:

- Straight-through processing for routine requests.
- Earlier detection of likely customization.
- Parallel and selective specialist assessments.
- Reduced approval waiting.
- Dynamic queue balancing.
- Recalculation of only affected items after rejection.
- Proactive resolution of missing information.

The largest gains will usually be in queue time rather than task execution time.

### Customer satisfaction

Customers receive:

- Immediate acknowledgment and validation.
- More reliable delivery estimates.
- Faster standard-request responses.
- Better alternatives for infeasible requests.
- Transparent status and proactive delay notifications.
- More collaborative handling of custom needs.

However, over-automation could feel impersonal or produce inappropriate outcomes. Clear escalation to a human remains important.

### Operational flexibility

Adaptive case handling, reusable templates, externalized business rules, and skill-based assignment make it easier to support new request types without redesigning the entire workflow.

### Operational complexity and risk

The redesign introduces additional complexity:

- More integrations and asynchronous failure modes.
- Model governance and drift monitoring.
- Data-quality, privacy, and security requirements.
- More sophisticated workforce orchestration.
- Version management for rules and models.
- Need for auditability and fallback procedures.

It should therefore be implemented incrementally. A practical sequence is:

1. Automate intake and rule-based standard validation.
2. Integrate credit, inventory, and approval systems.
3. Add SLA monitoring and skill-based routing.
4. Introduce predictive triage in recommendation mode.
5. Enable straight-through predictive routing after validation.
6. Add adaptive custom case management and predictive delivery forecasting.

Core metrics should include end-to-end cycle time, touch time, queue time, straight-through-processing rate, rework rate, approval latency, prediction precision/recall, delivery-date accuracy, SLA breaches, conversion rate, margin, override rate, and customer satisfaction. This ensures that faster processing does not come at the cost of quality, fairness, or profitability.
