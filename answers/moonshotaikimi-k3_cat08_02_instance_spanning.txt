A hazard order that is *ready* but not started during an at-cap period is constraint-blocked. | % of time at cap; blocking wait per hazard order; hazard throughput vs. hazard arrival rate (if arrivals exceed effective capped capacity, queue grows unboundedly in peaks); **compliance audit: verify zero violations** (hard guardrail) |

### 1.3 Differentiating Within-Instance vs. Between-Instance Waiting

This is the analytical core. I would decompose every order's cycle time into **service intervals** and **waiting intervals**, then attribute each waiting interval to a cause using the *state of the system at the moment the order became ready*:

**Decision logic for waiting-cause attribution:**
1. Required resource class fully occupied at ready-time  **resource contention** (between-instance)
2. Next activity is a batch activity and batch not yet triggered  **batch waiting** (between-instance)
3. Suspend/resume events or preemption signature present  **preemption delay** (between-instance)
4. Hazard flag = TRUE and concurrency = 10 at ready-time  **regulatory blocking** (between-instance)
5. Gap aligns with shift breaks/calendar  **schedule effect** (neither)
6. Residual  **handover latency / data lag** (flag for investigation)

**Validation:** train a gradient-boosted model predicting wait duration from state features (queue length at arrival, batch position, hazard count, utilization, time-of-day). Feature-importance analysis (e.g., SHAP values) confirms the taxonomy and quantifies each cause's share of total waiting. **Within-instance** issues are identified separately: activity durations that are outliers vs. their cohort (activity × order type × resource), rework loops detected in the discovered process model, or staff learning-curve effects.

**Expected headline output:** e.g., "42% of total waiting is between-instance (18% batch, 14% cold contention, 7% hazard blocking, 3% preemption)" — this becomes the business case and the baseline for improvement.

## 2. Analyzing Constraint Interactions

These constraints do not operate independently — they form a coupled system. Key interactions to quantify:

**Interaction 1 — Express × Cold-Packing:** An express cold order preempting one of only 5 cold stations hits the *scarcest* resource hardest. Preemption stretches the preempted order's occupancy (plus any resume/re-setup overhead, e.g., re-sanitizing a cold station), which raises effective utilization , which *nonlinearly* increases queue wait for everyone (Kingman dynamics). One preemption can cascade into minutes of added wait for every queued cold order.

**Interaction 2 — Batching × Hazard Cap:** If a region's batch requires, say, 8 hazardous orders, the batch can only form as fast as the 10-order concurrency cap lets hazard orders clear Packing/QC. The entire batch — including non-hazardous members — inherits the slowest member's delay (synchronization/convoy effect). A batching policy blind to hazard composition will systematically strand non-hazardous orders behind a regulatory bottleneck they don't even consume.

**Interaction 3 — Batching × Priority:** Expediting an express order through Packing is wasted if it then sits in a regional batch. Priority gains are *eroded downstream*. This is likely already visible in the data as express orders with fast activity times but mediocre end-to-end times.

**Interaction 4 — Cold × Batching:** Perishable orders waiting post-QC for batch formation may face spoilage/shelf-life limits, imposing a *hard upper bound* on batch wait for cold orders that standard orders don't have.

**Interaction 5 — Hazard Cap × Upstream Flow:** If hazard orders block at Packing but picking keeps releasing them, WIP piles up on the floor (congestion, safety risk) — a signal that *admission control* is missing.

**Why this matters:** Optimizing any single constraint in isolation risks **bottleneck shifting**. Example: adding cold stations flushes the cold queue but dumps volume into QC, where the hazard cap binds harder; aggressive batch triggers reduce wait but strand hazard-heavy batches and inflate shipping cost. Effective optimization must therefore be **multi-objective and system-level** — which is precisely why the strategies below are designed as a portfolio and validated holistically via simulation (Section 4) rather than piecemeal.

## 3. Constraint-Aware Optimization Strategies

### Strategy A — Constraint-Aware Dynamic Dispatching ("Orchestrator")

- **Constraints addressed:** Cold contention, priority handling, hazard cap.
- **Proposed changes:** Replace naive FIFO dispatch with a real-time rule: assign orders to stations by a priority score *f*(SLA deadline proximity, order type, downstream batch schedule) **subject to hard feasibility constraints** (cold capability; hazard token available — orders are never dispatched to Packing/QC if concurrency would exceed 10). During forecasted peak windows, dynamically **reserve 1 of 5 cold stations** for express/cold orders (auto-released to general pool if no qualifying express arrives within N minutes). Replace mid-activity preemption with **checkpoint-based preemption** (pause only at defined sub-step boundaries) to cut churn.
- **Data leverage:** Duration regression models (activity × type × station) and hourly arrival forecasts mined from the 3-month log; express arrival rates determine the reservation fraction; resume-overhead estimates justify the checkpoint policy.
- **Expected outcomes:** Lower p95 cold-queue wait; fewer and cheaper preemptions; express SLA compliance without starving standard orders; hazard compliance guaranteed *by construction* rather than by vigilance.

### Strategy B — Dynamic, SLA-Aware Batch Formation (+ Decoupling)

- **Constraints addressed:** Shipping batching; secondarily priority erosion and hazard–batch interaction.
- **Proposed changes:** Replace fixed size/time triggers with a composite release rule: release a batch when **(size  k)** OR **(oldest member's time-to-SLA  safety buffer)** OR **(an express member faces SLA risk)** OR **(cold/perishable members approach their shelf-life wait cap)**. Thresholds *k* and ** are tuned **per region and day-part** by optimizing the historical trade-off between wait time and routing cost. Where feasible, **decouple label generation from physical consolidation**: generate the label at QC-complete ("virtual batching") so the warehouse process is never blocked by batch formation; physical grouping becomes a downstream logistics decision. Batch composition becomes hazard-aware (a batch's hazard count is matched against expected token throughput).
- **Data leverage:** Historical batch wait vs. size curves; SLA distributions per region; hazard token throughput rates from Section 1 analysis.
- **Expected outcomes:** Eliminates the worst-case "first-in-batch" waits; preserves priority gains end-to-end; retains most routing-cost savings because thresholds are data-tuned rather than arbitrary.

### Strategy C — Hazard Pacing (Pull-Based Release) + Dedicated Hazard QC Cell

- **Constraints addressed:** Hazardous material cap and its interactions with batching and upstream flow.
- **Proposed changes:** Implement **admission control**: hold hazard orders at a controlled buffer *before* Packing and release them only when (a) a hazard token is available and (b) the release aligns with downstream batch timing. This converts chaotic blocking *at* the cap into a managed, visible queue *before* it — waiting happens where it is cheapest and safest. If operationally feasible, establish a **dedicated hazard QC cell** so hazard orders' QC doesn't compete with general QC capacity (the 10 cap still applies and is enforced by the dispatcher). Stagger hazard "waves" to synchronize with batch departures for hazard-heavy regions.
- **Data leverage:** Hazard arrival patterns by hour; token availability forecasts; batch schedules per region; release rules calibrated in simulation.
- **Expected outcomes:** Near-elimination of blocking wait at Packing; smoother token utilization (less time-at-cap followed by idle capacity); hazard-heavy batches form predictably; compliance hardened to 100%.

### Strategy D — Targeted Surge Capacity for Cold-Packing (Peak-Season Flex)

- **Constraints addressed:** Cold-packing contention (structural).
- **Proposed changes:** If analysis shows  > 0.85 during peaks (the regime where queueing delay explodes), deploy surge capacity: 1–2 **mobile/convertible cold-pack units** activated during forecasted peaks, plus cross-trained staff. Size the investment via simulation (Section 4): compute marginal wait reduction and SLA-penalty savings per added station.
- **Data leverage:** Seasonality and peak-window demand forecasts from the log; utilization heatmaps; simulation-based ROI.
- **Expected outcomes:**  returned to the 0.75–0.80 "sweet spot"  disproportionate (nonlinear) reduction in cold-queue wait; resilience against express surges without year-round fixed cost.

|---|---|---|
| A — Dynamic dispatching | Cold, Priority, Hazard | Scheduling rules |
| B — Dynamic batching | Batching (+Priority, +Hazard) | Policy redesign + decoupling |
| C — Hazard pacing + QC cell | Hazard (+Batching) | Process redesign |
| D — Surge cold capacity | Cold | Capacity adjustment |

## 4. Simulation and Validation

### 4.1 Approach

Use **discrete-event simulation (DES)** built directly from the mined model and parameters. DES is the right choice because shared resources, batching, preemption, and global semaphores are all native DES constructs — whereas standard BPMN simulators typically assume case independence and would *structurally fail* to reproduce these constraints. (Alternatives: colored Petri nets with multi-instance tokens, or object-centric simulation where orders, batches, and stations are first-class objects.)

**Parameterization (all mined, not assumed):**
- Non-stationary arrival process (by hour/day/season, with order-type mix)
- Duration distributions per activity × order type × resource (fitted, preserving variance)
- Resource calendars and counts (5 cold stations, standard stations, QC staff)
- Current batching trigger logic as discovered; candidate logics for what-if
- Preemption policy with resume-overhead distribution
- Hazard cap as a **global semaphore (=10)** spanning Packing  QC, with explicit queue discipline

### 4.2 Validation Before Experimentation

Replay the historical 3-month period (trace-driven, with warm-up) and compare simulated vs. actual: cycle-time distributions (KS test), queue lengths, utilizations, batch waits, time-at-cap. Calibrate until key KPIs are within an agreed tolerance (e.g., MAPE < 10%). A model that cannot reproduce the *observed symptom* (missed deadlines at peaks) cannot be trusted to evaluate the cure.

### 4.3 Experiments

- **Factorial design:** baseline  each strategy alone  combinations (especially A+B, A+B+C), to quantify interaction effects predicted in Section 2.
- **Stress tests:** +30% peak volume; one cold station down; express share doubling; hazard-order surge to one region.
- **KPIs:** mean/p90/p95 cycle time, OTIF, throughput, waiting time *decomposed by cause*, preemption counts, batch wait, hazard blocking time, and — as a hard guardrail — **hazard concurrency must never exceed 10 in any replication**.

### 4.4 Fidelity Aspects That Must Be Explicitly Modeled

1. **Resource contention:** stations as finite-capacity resources with realistic queue disciplines — not infinite-capacity approximations.
2. **Batching delays:** batch as an entity with trigger logic, member synchronization, and partial-release rules (the convoy effect only emerges if members genuinely wait for each other).
3. **Priority interruptions:** true preempt/resume semantics including resume overhead and checkpoint constraints — not just "express goes first in queue."
4. **Regulatory limit:** a global token pool consumed by *either* Packing or QC, never both double-counted, with hard enforcement.
5. **Non-stationarity:** time-varying arrivals and shift calendars (otherwise peak-season behavior — the actual problem — won't appear).
6. **Statistical rigor:** multiple replications with confidence intervals; strategies accepted only if improvement is significant at both mean *and* p90/p95 (tail behavior is where SLA breaches live).

### 5.2 Periodic Process Mining (Weekly/Monthly Re-Mining)

- **Conformance & compliance:** continuous checking of the hard rules — hazard concurrency  10 (zero-tolerance query), dispatch-rule adherence, batch trigger adherence — plus soft rules (e.g., "express Packing starts within 10 minutes of readiness").
- **Waiting-time cause attribution trend:** the Section 1.3 decomposition run continuously. **Target signature of success:** batch-wait % and contention-wait % declining; hazard blocking  0 (converted to planned upstream pacing); residual wait understood.
- **KPI suite:** cycle time mean/p90/p95 by cohort (order type, region, cold, hazard); OTIF; throughput per shift; utilization within the 70–85% sweet spot; batch wait avg/p95; express end-to-end time; preemption count and added minutes.
- **Leading indicators & drift detection:** monitor cold queue length and time-at-cap as *early warnings* (they rise before SLA breaches occur); concept-drift monitors on arrival mix and durations to catch demand shifts (e.g., express share creeping up, requiring re-tuning of the reservation fraction).

### 5.3 Governance Loop

- **Rollout measurement:** stepped-wedge or pilot-cell deployment, comparing treated vs. untreated lines with the same mined KPIs — avoiding attribution of seasonal effects to the change.
- **Guardrail metrics:** hazard compliance and safety KPIs are non-negotiable; any flow improvement that pressures the cap is rejected.
- **Living parameters:** batch thresholds (*k*, **), reservation fractions, and pacing rules are treated as **configuration, re-optimized quarterly** from fresh mining results — the process is managed as a continuously learning system, optionally evolving toward a simulation-fed **digital twin** for ongoing what-if support.

## Summary

The root insight is that this warehouse's deadline problems are substantially **system-effects**, not activity-speed problems: orders wait for *each other*. The strategy therefore (1) measures waiting by *cause* using cross-case state reconstruction, (2) maps constraint interactions so fixes don't merely move the bottleneck, (3) applies a portfolio of scheduling, batching, pacing, and capacity interventions that encode the constraints as first-class rules, (4) validates everything in a constraint-faithful simulation before touching the floor, and (5) institutionalizes monitoring so the constraints remain visibly managed — with regulatory compliance as an unbreakable guardrail throughout.
