## 1. Queue Identification and Characterization

### 1.1 Define the patient journey and prepare the event log

Before calculating queues, the clinic should create a clean, analysis-ready event log. Each visit should have:

- A unique visit/case identifier.
- Activity name, ideally standardized (for example, “Doctor Consultation—Cardiology” rather than multiple spelling variants).
- Lifecycle timestamps: `START` and `COMPLETE`.
- Resource identifiers, such as clinician, nurse, room, machine, or desk.
- Relevant patient attributes: new/follow-up, specialty, urgency, appointment type, time/day, and possibly insurance or language-support needs where appropriate and ethically permitted.

For every activity instance, pair the start and completion events to derive:

\[
\text{Service Time}_{a} = \text{Completion Time}_{a} - \text{Start Time}_{a}
\]

For example:

\[
\text{Registration Service Time} =
08{:}45 - 08{:}02 = 6.5 \text{ minutes}
\]

Data quality checks are essential before queue analysis:

- Missing starts or completions.
- Completion before start.
- Duplicate events.
- Activities with implausibly long durations caused by staff failing to close encounters.
- Resource identifiers that combine multiple resources inconsistently.
- Overnight visits or events crossing operational-day boundaries.
- Patients who leave, reschedule, or do not complete the intended route.

The analysis should preserve valid exceptions rather than treating every unusual trace as a data error. For example, an urgent patient bypassing registration may be clinically appropriate.

---

### 1.2 Calculate waiting or queue time

For a patient moving from activity \(i\) to activity \(j\), define elapsed inter-activity time as:

\[
\text{Inter-activity Time}_{i \rightarrow j}
=
\text{Start Time}_{j}
-
\text{Completion Time}_{i}
\]

When the patient is clinically ready for the next activity immediately after completion of the prior activity, this is a useful approximation of queue time:

\[
\text{Queue Time}_{i \rightarrow j}
=
\max(0,\text{Start}_{j}-\text{Complete}_{i})
\]

Example:

- Nurse assessment completes: 09:25:10
- Doctor consultation starts: 09:45:55

\[
\text{Wait for Doctor} =
20 \text{ minutes } 45 \text{ seconds}
\]

In practice, the clinic should distinguish several types of time that may appear in this gap:

1. **True queue time**  
   The patient is ready and waiting for a constrained resource, such as a physician, nurse, ECG technician, room, or check-out clerk.

2. **Handover or coordination delay**  
   The patient is waiting because information, an order, room assignment, or patient transfer has not occurred.

3. **Intentional clinical delay**  
   For example, a physician may intentionally wait for lab results before reviewing the patient. This should not automatically be treated as poor operational performance.

4. **Patient-caused or patient-choice delay**  
   The patient may be late returning from imaging, may leave the unit temporarily, or may defer a test.

5. **Scheduled delay**  
   Some services may intentionally occur later in the visit or on another day. These should be analyzed separately from walk-in queueing.

Therefore, the raw timestamp gap is the starting point, not the final interpretation. The clinic should label transitions as “queue-eligible” only when the patient is expected to proceed promptly and is waiting for the next care resource.

For the first activity, such as registration, the event log may not contain the patient’s physical arrival time. If arrival or kiosk check-in time exists, calculate:

\[
\text{Arrival-to-Registration Wait}
=
\text{Registration Start}
-
\text{Patient Arrival/Check-in Time}
\]

If only registration start time is recorded, the clinic cannot reliably measure waiting before registration; it can only measure registration service time. Capturing arrival/check-in timestamps should be a high-priority data enhancement.

---

### 1.3 Define queues at multiple levels

Queues should be analyzed at more than one level.

#### A. Transition queues

Examples:

- Registration \(\rightarrow\) Nurse Assessment
- Nurse Assessment \(\rightarrow\) Doctor Consultation
- Doctor Consultation \(\rightarrow\) ECG
- ECG \(\rightarrow\) Specialist Review
- Final clinical activity \(\rightarrow\) Check-out

This identifies where patients wait in the process.

#### B. Activity/resource queues

Examples:

- Queue for Cardiology physicians.
- Queue for ECG room 3.
- Queue for phlebotomy.
- Queue for check-out clerks.

This identifies which constrained resources drive the wait.

#### C. Patient-segment queues

Analyze separately by:

- New versus follow-up patients.
- Urgent versus normal priority.
- Specialty.
- Appointment type.
- Day of week.
- Hour of day.
- Time since scheduled appointment.
- Diagnostic pathway versus consultation-only pathway.

A queue that looks acceptable on average may be severe for a particular group, such as new cardiology patients requiring ECG, or patients arriving in the late morning.

---

### 1.4 Key queue metrics

For every transition, activity, resource, specialty, and patient segment, calculate the following.

| Metric | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Mean waiting time | Average wait across eligible visits | Useful for aggregate planning, but sensitive to extreme values |
| Median waiting time | Typical patient experience | More robust than the mean |
| 75th, 90th, and 95th percentile wait | Long waits experienced by the worst-served patients | Essential for patient experience and equity |
| Maximum wait | Longest observed wait | Useful for investigating extreme failures, not for routine performance targets |
| Queue frequency | Percentage of eligible visits that encounter the queue | Distinguishes a widespread issue from a rare one |
| Excessive-wait rate | Percentage exceeding a service threshold, such as 20 or 30 minutes | Directly supports operational targets |
| Total waiting burden | Sum of all patient waiting minutes for a queue | Measures total organizational impact |
| Average queue length | Average number of patients ready and waiting | Helps quantify staffing/capacity need |
| Peak queue length | Largest queue observed | Reveals periods of operational overload |
| Service time | Completion minus start for an activity | Identifies duration variability and resource use |
| Resource utilization | Fraction of operating time resource is actively occupied | Helps distinguish under-capacity from poor synchronization |
| Visit cycle time | Final completion minus arrival/check-in | Measures overall visit duration |
| First-contact delay | Arrival to first clinical interaction | Important for satisfaction and safety |
| Rework/repeat rate | Repeated registration, test, or consultation events | May indicate quality problems that create queues |

The clinic should set service standards by queue type. For example:

- Registration wait: target median under 10 minutes.
- Nurse-to-doctor wait: target 90th percentile under 30 minutes.
- Urgent patient wait for clinician: more stringent target, such as under 10–15 minutes depending on clinical policy.
- Check-out wait: target median under 5–10 minutes.

The exact thresholds should be defined with clinical leadership and patient-experience teams rather than selected arbitrarily.

---

### 1.5 Identify the most critical queues

The most critical queue should not simply be the queue with the highest average wait. A queue with a 40-minute average affecting only ten patients a month may be less important than a 20-minute queue affecting 5,000 patients monthly.

A practical prioritization score can combine:

\[
\text{Priority Score}
=
\text{Volume}
\times
\text{Mean Wait}
\times
\text{Excessive-Wait Rate}
\times
\text{Clinical/Patient Impact Weight}
\]

Queues should be prioritized using several criteria:

1. **High total waiting burden**  
   High-volume queues with substantial waits may deliver the largest overall reduction in patient waiting minutes.

2. **Poor tail performance**  
   A queue with an acceptable median but a 90th percentile of 75 minutes indicates unreliable service and likely dissatisfaction.

3. **Impact on vulnerable or urgent groups**  
   Long waits for urgent patients, elderly patients, new patients, or patients requiring multiple diagnostics warrant higher priority.

4. **Contribution to total visit duration**  
   Analyze what percentage of the full visit is spent waiting. A 30-minute wait before the doctor may account for most of a two-hour visit.

5. **Downstream propagation**  
   A delay early in the visit may cause missed diagnostic slots, delayed checkout, overtime, or missed appointments later in the day.

6. **Resource bottleneck signals**  
   A queue linked to a highly utilized physician, diagnostic room, or check-out station may be a structural bottleneck.

For example, the clinic may find that:

- Registration has a moderate average wait but affects nearly every patient.
- The nurse-to-doctor queue has the highest total patient waiting burden.
- ECG wait is severe only for cardiology patients but causes missed specialist-review windows.
- Check-out delays are short but frequent and generate a poor “last impression.”

All may deserve attention, but the order of intervention should follow measurable impact, clinical importance, and feasibility.

## 2. Root Cause Analysis

Queue mining identifies where patients wait. Root cause analysis explains why those waits occur.

### 2.1 Resource bottlenecks and capacity mismatch

A critical queue may arise because resource demand exceeds resource capacity during certain periods.

Examples include:

- Too few registration clerks from 8:00–10:00 AM.
- Nurse staffing not aligned to arrival peaks.
- Physicians beginning clinic later than appointment schedules assume.
- Limited ECG rooms or technicians.
- A single check-out clerk serving multiple specialties at the end of the day.
- A consultation room unavailable even when the doctor is available.

Relevant analyses include:

- Resource utilization by hour/day.
- Concurrent activity counts per resource.
- Idle time between assignments.
- Resource workload distribution.
- Number of patients ready to start an activity compared with the number of available resources.
- Queue length over time.

A resource operating near 100% utilization for prolonged periods is likely unstable: even small increases in arrival volume or service-time variation can cause queue growth. However, low recorded utilization does not automatically mean excess capacity; it may reflect missing room data, inaccurate timestamps, or synchronization delays.

---

### 2.2 Activity dependencies, handovers, and coordination failures

Patients may wait not because staff are unavailable, but because the next step is not triggered efficiently.

Examples:

- A nurse completes assessment but does not promptly notify the physician.
- A physician orders an ECG but the order is not visible to diagnostic staff immediately.
- The patient waits for room assignment despite an available room.
- Test results are complete but not routed to the physician for review.
- The patient waits for transport, consent, billing validation, or documentation completion.

Process mining can identify whether wait begins before or after a necessary handover event. If additional data are available, include events such as:

- Order placed.
- Test scheduled.
- Patient called from waiting room.
- Room assigned.
- Specimen collected.
- Result available.
- Physician notified.
- Discharge order signed.

This allows decomposition of a broad “doctor-to-ECG wait” into:

\[
\text{Order Delay}
+
\text{Scheduling Delay}
+
\text{Patient Calling Delay}
+
\text{Actual Resource Queue}
\]

That decomposition prevents the clinic from solving the wrong problem. Adding another ECG technician will not help if the real delay is late order entry or slow patient notification.

---

### 2.3 High variability in service times

Queueing problems often result from variability, not merely high average service time.

For example, most physician consultations may last 15 minutes, but a subset of complex new-patient visits may last 45 minutes. If appointment slots are uniformly 15 minutes, delays accumulate throughout the day.

Service-time analysis should examine:

- Mean, median, standard deviation, and percentile service times.
- Service time by specialty, clinician, visit type, urgency, and diagnosis category where appropriate.
- Whether long consultations are clinically justified or caused by documentation burden, late patient arrivals, or room/equipment delays.
- Service-time differences by time of day, such as consultations becoming longer late in the day because schedules are already delayed.
- Resource-specific variation, for example whether some ECG rooms or technicians have systematically longer turnaround times.

A useful pattern is:

- Long and variable service time for new patients.
- Standard appointments booked at the same duration for new and follow-up patients.
- Result: new visits create cascading delays for later appointments.

---

### 2.4 Appointment scheduling policies and arrival patterns

A clinic may have sufficient daily capacity but still experience large queues because patient arrivals and staffing are poorly synchronized.

Common causes include:

- Too many appointments scheduled at the top of the hour.
- All new patients scheduled in the morning.
- Multiple specialties sending patients to the same diagnostic service at the same time.
- Walk-ins and urgent patients not accounted for in capacity planning.
- Patients arriving much earlier than scheduled.
- High no-show rates leading to overbooking patterns that create volatile queues.
- Appointment durations not matched to patient complexity.

The clinic should analyze:

- Appointment time versus actual arrival time.
- Arrival distribution by 15-, 30-, or 60-minute interval.
- No-show and late-arrival rates.
- Scheduled versus realized consultation duration.
- Demand for each resource over time.
- Spillover effects from one session into another.
- Whether one specialty’s schedule creates diagnostic demand peaks for another department.

For example, if most cardiology consultations end between 10:30 and 11:30 and immediately trigger ECG orders, the ECG queue may be a downstream scheduling artifact rather than an ECG staffing problem.

---

### 2.5 Differences by patient type and urgency

Queue performance should be evaluated for patient segments, not just the aggregate population.

Possible findings include:

- New patients wait longer after nursing assessment because they require longer physician consultations.
- Follow-up patients experience unnecessary waits because they follow the same pathway as more complex new patients.
- Urgent patients appropriately bypass some queues but create delays for routine patients.
- Patients needing multiple diagnostics have long waits due to poor sequencing of tests.
- Certain specialties have a more complex process variant with repeated handovers.

The clinic should assess both operational efficiency and fairness. For example, an improvement that reduces average wait but disproportionately increases waiting for routine patients, language-support patients, or mobility-limited patients may be unacceptable.

---

### 2.6 Process mining methods for root cause analysis

Beyond simple queue-time calculations, the clinic can use the following techniques.

#### Process discovery

Use tools such as directly-follows graphs, process maps, or Petri-net/BPMN discovery to visualize actual patient pathways.

This can reveal:

- Unexpected loops.
- Repeated registration or assessment.
- Test-before-consultation versus consult-before-test variants.
- Bypassed or delayed steps.
- Unplanned referrals and rework.

#### Variant analysis

Compare common process variants, such as:

- New cardiology visit with ECG.
- Follow-up cardiology visit without diagnostics.
- Urgent walk-in.
- Patients requiring blood test and X-ray.
- Patients discharged directly after consultation.

For each variant, compare waiting time, total visit duration, resource use, and completion outcomes.

#### Bottleneck analysis

Identify activities or resources with:

- High utilization.
- Long queue time before start.
- High workload concentration.
- Long service duration.
- Strong downstream delay effects.

A true bottleneck typically has both high demand and evidence that patients are ready but cannot start the activity promptly.

#### Resource and organizational mining

Analyze resource handovers and staffing patterns:

- Which clinicians, nurses, rooms, or diagnostic devices are linked to long waits?
- Does performance change when specific staff combinations are working?
- Are handovers between departments slower than within departments?
- Are there gaps when staff are on breaks, in meetings, or changing shifts?

This must be used carefully and constructively. The goal is system improvement, not simplistic ranking or blaming individual staff. Differences may reflect patient complexity, specialty mix, training roles, or data-capture behavior.

#### Conformance checking

Compare actual processes with intended care pathways and scheduling rules.

Examples:

- Are urgent patients seen within the intended escalation window?
- Are tests performed before the required physician order?
- Are patients waiting after all prerequisites are complete?
- Are routine visits using resource-intensive pathways unnecessarily?

#### Statistical and predictive analysis

Use regression, survival analysis, or machine-learning models to estimate which factors predict excessive waits. Candidate predictors include:

- Specialty.
- Time of day.
- Day of week.
- Arrival delay.
- Patient type.
- Urgency.
- Required diagnostics.
- Provider schedule.
- Queue size at arrival.
- Resource utilization.
- Prior activity duration.

The model should be interpretable and validated before operational use. It should support decisions, not replace clinical judgment.

## 3. Data-Driven Optimization Strategies

The exact interventions should follow measured bottlenecks, but the following are concrete strategies suitable for a multi-specialty outpatient clinic.

### Strategy 1: Align staffing and room capacity with demand peaks

**Target queues**

- Arrival-to-registration.
- Registration-to-nurse assessment.
- Nurse assessment-to-doctor consultation.
- Check-out queues.
- Diagnostic queues during predictable demand peaks.

**Likely root cause addressed**

Demand is concentrated during certain time intervals while staffing, room availability, or diagnostic capacity is distributed evenly or misaligned with demand.

**Data support**

Queue mining can show:

- Queue lengths by 15- or 30-minute interval.
- Resource utilization by hour.
- Patient arrival rates.
- The number of patients ready for each activity.
- Peak periods where waits rise sharply.
- Staff or rooms that are highly utilized while others are underused.

For instance, analysis may show that registration waits exceed 20 minutes from 8:00–9:30 AM while clerks are underutilized after 2:00 PM. Alternatively, it may show that ECG demand peaks shortly after cardiology consultations.

**Intervention**

- Move existing staff shifts toward observed peaks rather than increasing total staffing.
- Stagger breaks and meetings outside peak periods.
- Assign a flex nurse, clerk, or technician to the most constrained area during predictable surges.
- Cross-train selected staff for limited support functions, such as check-in assistance, room turnover, or patient flow coordination.
- Reserve specific rooms for high-throughput activities during peak hours.
- Use dynamic staffing triggers, for example activating a flex resource when queue length exceeds a threshold.

**Expected impact**

The clinic should estimate impact using queueing simulation or historical “what-if” analysis. For example, if the model shows that one additional registration-capable staff member for two morning hours reduces the 90th percentile registration wait from 28 to 12 minutes, this may be more cost-effective than adding a full-time clerk.

Expected results should be expressed as modeled ranges and validated in a pilot, rather than assumed in advance.

**Key caution**

Adding capacity at one stage can shift the bottleneck downstream. Faster registration may increase the nurse or physician queue unless those stages are also considered.

---

### Strategy 2: Redesign appointment templates and smooth arrivals

**Target queues**

- Registration.
- Nurse assessment.
- Doctor consultation.
- Shared diagnostics such as ECG, blood testing, and imaging.

**Likely root cause addressed**

The clinic’s schedule creates avoidable demand spikes: appointments clustered at the same time, inappropriate slot lengths, insufficient allowance for new or complex patients, or multiple specialties competing for diagnostics simultaneously.

**Data support**

Analyze:

- Scheduled appointment times compared with actual arrival times.
- Actual service duration by visit type and specialty.
- Waiting time by appointment slot.
- Queue build-up over the day.
- No-show and late-arrival behavior.
- Demand for diagnostic services generated by each specialty.

A likely finding may be that new patients require a median physician consultation of 30 minutes while follow-up patients require 15 minutes, yet both are booked into 20-minute slots. Another finding may be that many patients are scheduled at 8:00 AM, producing an avoidable registration peak.

**Intervention**

- Stagger appointment starts rather than booking all patients at the top of the hour.
- Create differentiated appointment lengths for new, follow-up, urgent, and complex patients.
- Reserve protected slots for urgent add-ons rather than inserting them unpredictably into fully booked schedules.
- Schedule diagnostic-dependent patients in coordinated blocks.
- Limit simultaneous bookings that create a shared diagnostic peak.
- Use controlled overbooking only where no-show patterns justify it and where care quality and staff workload remain protected.
- Introduce pre-visit readiness checks to resolve insurance, referrals, forms, or prior authorizations before arrival.

**Expected impact**

A scheduling simulation can test alternatives such as:

- Staggering arrivals every 10–15 minutes.
- Reserving ECG capacity after cardiology blocks.
- Increasing new-patient slot duration.
- Redistributing follow-up appointments to less congested periods.

Possible expected outcomes include reduced queue peaks, lower physician schedule overruns, and fewer patients exceeding the clinic’s excessive-wait threshold. The clinic should define success in terms of percentile waits and total visit duration, not just the daily average.

**Key caution**

Longer slots for complex patients may reduce the number of daily appointments. This should be evaluated against reduced overtime, fewer delays, improved patient satisfaction, and lower missed-appointment risk.

---

### Strategy 3: Create a coordinated diagnostic pathway and parallelize work where clinically appropriate

**Target queues**

- Doctor consultation-to-ECG.
- Doctor consultation-to-blood test.
- Doctor consultation-to-X-ray.
- Diagnostic test-to-specialist review.
- Delays caused by repeated waiting between multiple diagnostics.

**Likely root cause addressed**

Patients wait because diagnostic orders are placed late, activities are sequential when they could be parallel, resources are not coordinated, or test results are not routed promptly for review.

**Data support**

Process variants can identify whether patients with similar clinical needs follow different sequences, such as:

- Consultation \(\rightarrow\) ECG \(\rightarrow\) review.
- ECG \(\rightarrow\) consultation \(\rightarrow\) review.
- Consultation \(\rightarrow\) long wait \(\rightarrow\) ECG.
- Consultation \(\rightarrow\) check-out \(\rightarrow\) return for diagnostics.

The analysis may show that patients requiring ECG and blood tests have long cumulative waits because they queue independently for each service rather than being actively coordinated.

**Intervention**

- Introduce a diagnostic navigator, electronic worklist, or patient-flow coordinator for high-volume pathways.
- Use standardized order sets for common specialty pathways where clinically appropriate.
- Pre-order routine diagnostics before the visit when clinically justified and approved by physicians.
- Schedule diagnostic slots at the time of appointment booking for predictable pathways.
- Run eligible diagnostics in parallel rather than sequentially.
- Automatically notify the next department when prerequisites are complete.
- Implement real-time status boards showing patients waiting, rooms available, orders pending, and result status.
- Create “fast-track” pathways for routine follow-up visits that require standardized testing.

**Expected impact**

For a pathway involving consultation, ECG, and review, eliminating a duplicated handover or unnecessary sequential wait may reduce total visit time materially even if the service times themselves do not change.

The clinic should model and pilot whether, for example, scheduling ECG before consultation for selected patients reduces the median total visit duration without delaying clinically necessary decision-making.

**Key caution**

Parallelization and pre-ordering must be clinically governed. Tests should not be ordered solely for operational convenience if they are not medically necessary. Care teams must retain the ability to override standardized pathways.

---

### Strategy 4: Implement real-time queue visibility and escalation rules

**Target queues**

- Any queue with unpredictable spikes, particularly nurse-to-doctor, diagnostic, or check-out queues.
- Urgent-patient queues where rapid response is clinically important.

**Likely root cause addressed**

The clinic may have adequate capacity overall but lacks visibility into queue build-up, patient readiness, delayed handovers, or unassigned work.

**Data support**

Historical queue mining can define operational thresholds, such as:

- More than five patients waiting for a nurse assessment.
- More than three patients waiting for a specific diagnostic room.
- Any urgent patient waiting beyond a clinically defined threshold.
- A patient waiting more than 30 minutes after a completed nurse assessment.

**Intervention**

Develop a live operational dashboard drawing from the same event structure, displaying:

- Number of patients waiting by next activity.
- Waiting-time aging buckets: 0–10, 10–20, 20–30, and more than 30 minutes.
- Resource availability and current workload.
- Patients with urgent status.
- Delayed handovers or unacknowledged orders.
- Expected versus actual physician schedule progression.

Create escalation rules, for example:

- Notify a charge nurse if more than four patients wait over 20 minutes for assessment.
- Reassign a flex resource when a queue exceeds a threshold.
- Alert the responsible team if an urgent patient has not entered a clinical activity within the target time.
- Trigger room-turnover support when room availability becomes limiting.

**Expected impact**

Real-time visibility primarily improves responsiveness and reliability. It may reduce long-tail waits even when average demand remains unchanged.

**Key caution**

Dashboards can create alert fatigue if thresholds are poorly designed. They should support staff decision-making, not become a surveillance or punitive tool.

---

### Strategy 5: Reduce avoidable administrative work through pre-registration and digital check-out

**Target queues**

- Arrival-to-registration.
- Registration service time.
- Final clinical activity-to-check-out.
- Check-out service time.

**Likely root cause addressed**

Registration and check-out queues may be driven by repetitive forms, insurance verification, copay processing, appointment scheduling, printing, and manual handoffs.

**Data support**

Service-time distributions may show that a subset of registrations or check-outs take much longer because of insurance, referral, payment, or documentation issues. Process variants may reveal repeated registration attempts or patients returning to the desk multiple times.

**Intervention**

- Offer pre-registration through a patient portal, kiosk, or assisted telephone process.
- Verify insurance and referral requirements before the appointment date.
- Use electronic consent forms and document upload.
- Enable digital check-out for eligible patients.
- Pre-schedule follow-up appointments before the patient arrives, where appropriate.
- Separate complex financial/insurance exceptions from routine check-in so they do not block the standard queue.
- Use a greeter or digital support role during peak periods to help patients complete self-service steps.

**Expected impact**

This may reduce both service time and variability at front-desk processes. It can also free clerical staff to focus on complex patients rather than routine transactions.

**Key caution**

Digital workflows must not disadvantage patients with limited digital access, language barriers, disabilities, or low technology literacy. An assisted, equitable alternative should always remain available.

## 4. Trade-offs and Constraints

### 4.1 Shifting bottlenecks rather than removing them

A common failure is optimizing one stage in isolation.

For example:

- Faster registration can increase the nurse queue.
- Faster nurse assessment can increase the doctor queue.
- More ECG capacity can create a physician-review backlog.
- Faster check-out may expose delays in discharge-order completion.

For this reason, interventions should be evaluated across the full patient journey, from arrival through check-out, rather than by a single local metric.

---

### 4.2 Cost versus capacity

Adding permanent staff, rooms, or equipment can reduce waits but may be expensive and underutilized outside peak times.

The clinic should first evaluate lower-cost alternatives:

- Shift redesign.
- Break staggering.
- Flex staffing.
- Cross-training.
- Appointment smoothing.
- Better handovers.
- Improved scheduling.
- Digital pre-registration.
- Better use of existing rooms and equipment.

If added capacity is needed, it should be justified through demand and queue analysis. For example, a part-time ECG technician during a two-hour peak may be more cost-effective than a full-time addition.

---

### 4.3 Staff workload, burnout, and quality of care

Reducing waits by simply increasing staff speed or compressing appointments can reduce care quality, documentation quality, patient communication, and staff wellbeing.

Potential risks include:

- Shortened consultations that reduce patient understanding.
- Reduced time for clinical reasoning.
- More documentation performed after hours.
- Increased clinician burnout.
- Safety issues from rushed handovers.
- Inappropriate diagnostic pre-ordering.

The clinic should treat service-time reductions as beneficial only when they remove non-value-added work, avoidable rework, or unnecessary delay—not when they compromise clinical care.

---

### 4.4 Priority policies and fairness

Urgent patients may need to bypass queues. This is clinically appropriate but can increase waits for routine patients.

The clinic should explicitly define priority rules:

- Which patient categories qualify for expedited handling?
- What are the maximum acceptable waits by urgency level?
- How will the impact on routine patients be monitored?
- Are priority decisions clinically governed and documented?

Queue performance should be reported by urgency, patient type, specialty, and other relevant fairness dimensions. A lower overall average must not conceal worsening service for particular populations.

---

### 4.5 Data limitations and privacy

The event log may not capture key operational states, such as:

- Actual patient arrival.
- Patient ready-for-service time.
- Room availability.
- Order entry time.
- Test result availability.
- Staff breaks and scheduled working hours.
- Patient departure before completion.
- Reasons for delays.

The clinic may need to add targeted timestamp fields or integrate scheduling, electronic health record, room-management, and diagnostic systems.

All analysis should comply with healthcare privacy and security requirements. Patient data should be minimized, access-controlled, de-identified for broad reporting where possible, and used for operational improvement rather than inappropriate staff surveillance.

---

### 4.6 Balancing objectives

A balanced optimization objective should include:

\[
\text{Minimize}
\quad
\text{Patient Wait}
+
\text{Visit Duration}
+
\text{Excessive-Wait Risk}
+
\text{Cost}
+
\text{Staff Overload}
\]

subject to:

- Clinical quality requirements.
- Patient safety requirements.
- Appropriate urgency prioritization.
- Staffing constraints.
- Room/equipment constraints.
- Financial limits.
- Regulatory and documentation requirements.

The clinic should use pilot testing, simulation, and phased rollout rather than applying major changes across all specialties at once.

---

## 5. Measuring Success and Ongoing Monitoring

### 5.1 Core post-implementation KPIs

The clinic should establish a baseline for at least several months before intervention and then compare performance after rollout, adjusting for seasonality, specialty mix, and patient complexity.

#### Patient flow and queue KPIs

- Mean and median wait by transition.
- 75th, 90th, and 95th percentile wait by transition.
- Percentage of patients exceeding defined wait thresholds.
- Total patient waiting minutes per day/week/month.
- Average and peak queue length by activity/resource.
- Arrival-to-first-clinical-contact time.
- Nurse-to-doctor wait.
- Doctor-to-diagnostic wait.
- Final activity-to-check-out wait.
- Total visit duration from arrival/check-in to departure/check-out.

#### Reliability KPIs

- Percentage of visits completed within a target total duration.
- Percentage of patients seen by a clinician within the scheduled time window.
- Percentage of urgent patients seen within the clinical urgency target.
- Percentage of diagnostic tests completed within the same visit when intended.
- Frequency of process deviations, rework, or repeat activities.

#### Resource KPIs

- Resource utilization by role, room, and equipment.
- Workload balance across comparable clinicians or staff, adjusted for patient complexity.
- Overtime and late clinic completion.
- Room turnover time.
- Idle time caused by missing handovers, orders, or patient readiness.
- Flex-resource activation frequency and effectiveness.

#### Patient and staff experience KPIs

- Patient satisfaction, especially questions related to waiting, communication, and visit duration.
- Patient complaints related to delays.
- Appointment abandonment, no-show, or early departure rates.
- Staff workload and burnout indicators.
- Staff feedback on workflow usability and safety.

#### Clinical quality and safety guardrails

- Adverse events or delays in urgent care.
- Diagnostic completion appropriateness.
- Follow-up completion rates.
- Documentation quality.
- Readmission or repeat-visit indicators where relevant.
- Care guideline compliance.

An intervention should not be considered successful if it reduces wait times but worsens clinical quality, safety, patient understanding, or staff wellbeing.

---

### 5.2 Monitoring methodology

The clinic should use the same event-log structure to build a continuous process-monitoring capability.

A practical monitoring cycle is:

1. **Daily or intraday operational monitoring**
   - Live queue lengths.
   - Aging waits.
   - Urgent-patient alerts.
   - Resource availability.
   - Exception escalation.

2. **Weekly operational review**
   - Wait times by specialty, day, hour, and resource.
   - Changes in bottleneck location.
   - Staffing and scheduling alignment.
   - Review of long-wait outliers.

3. **Monthly or quarterly management review**
   - Trends in total visit duration.
   - Percentile waits.
   - Patient satisfaction.
   - Cost and overtime.
   - Equity by patient segment.
   - Clinical-quality guardrails.

4. **Periodic process re-discovery**
   - Re-run process discovery and variant analysis after major changes.
   - Confirm whether intended new workflows are actually being followed.
   - Detect emerging workarounds, new loops, or shifted bottlenecks.

---

### 5.3 Use statistical process control and alerts

Control charts can distinguish normal operational variation from meaningful deterioration or improvement.

For example:

- Monitor weekly median nurse-to-doctor wait.
- Monitor the 90th percentile ECG wait.
- Monitor the proportion of urgent patients waiting beyond target.
- Trigger investigation when performance exceeds control limits rather than reacting to every single fluctuation.

This prevents management from making frequent changes based on random variation.

---

### 5.4 Evaluate interventions through pilots and comparison groups

Where possible, use structured evaluation rather than simple before-and-after comparison.

Examples:

- Pilot a new scheduling template in cardiology before deploying it clinic-wide.
- Compare one morning session using a flex registration clerk with comparable sessions without one.
- Test digital check-out in one specialty.
- Phase in coordinated diagnostic pathways across selected clinics.

Evaluation should compare:

- Target queue times.
- Overall visit duration.
- Downstream queues.
- Resource utilization.
- Patient satisfaction.
- Staff workload.
- Clinical outcomes.

This helps establish whether observed improvement came from the intervention rather than seasonal demand changes, staffing changes, or case-mix variation.

---

### 5.5 Definition of sustained success

Sustained success would be demonstrated by:

- Lower median and 90th percentile waits in critical transitions.
- Reduced total visit duration.
- Fewer patients experiencing extreme waits.
- Improved patient satisfaction with waiting and communication.
- No deterioration in clinical quality or safety.
- No unacceptable increase in overtime, staffing costs, or burnout.
- Stable or improved performance over multiple months, not only immediately after implementation.
- Evidence that bottlenecks have not simply moved to another part of the clinic.

The ultimate objective is not merely to make individual activities faster. It is to create a reliable, coordinated patient flow in which patients spend less time waiting, staff can work at a sustainable pace, resources are used effectively, and clinical care remains safe and patient-centered.
