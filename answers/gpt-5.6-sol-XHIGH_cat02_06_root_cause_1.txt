## 1. Cases with longer resolution times

Total cycle time is measured from **Receive Ticket** to **Close Ticket**.

| Case | Total cycle time | Assessment | Main delays |
|---|---:|---|---|
| 101 | 2h 15m | Fast | No material delay |
| 102 | 25h 10m | Delayed | 5h from assignment through escalation to investigation; 19h from investigation to resolution |
| 103 | 1h 20m | Fastest | No material delay |
| 104 | 24h 10m | Delayed | 3h 30m before investigation; 19h from investigation to resolution |
| 105 | 49h 05m | Severe outlier | 28h after escalation before Level-2 investigation; another 19h before resolution |

The overall average is **20h 24m**, although it is heavily inflated by the three slow cases. Therefore:

- **Case 105** is the clearest performance outlier.
- **Cases 102 and 104** are also substantially slower than the efficient cases, 101 and 103.
- The cases form two broad groups: fast cases resolved in roughly **1–2 hours**, and delayed cases taking **24–49 hours**.

## 2. Potential root causes

### A. Escalation and Level-2 handoff delays

Both escalated cases have long cycle times:

- **Case 102:** 25h 10m
- **Case 105:** 49h 05m

Case 105 has a particularly serious escalation bottleneck:

- Escalated: March 1 at 10:00
- Level-2 investigation: March 2 at 14:00
- Delay: **28 hours**

This may indicate:

- Limited Level-2 capacity or working-hour coverage
- A backlog in the specialist queue
- Poor ownership during handoff
- Incomplete diagnostic information requiring reassessment
- Lack of escalation priority or response-time targets

Case 105 also contains a second investigation after escalation, suggesting additional handoff or rework.

### B. Repeated 19-hour investigation-to-resolution delay

Cases 102, 104, and 105 each have exactly **19 hours** between the final investigation event and resolution:

- Case 102: March 1 14:00 to March 2 09:00
- Case 104: March 1 13:00 to March 2 08:00
- Case 105: March 2 14:00 to March 3 09:00

This repeated overnight pattern is a strong indication of a systemic bottleneck, such as:

- Resolution work being deferred until the next business day
- No evening or overnight resolution coverage
- Waiting for approval, customer feedback, or an external dependency
- Resolutions being processed or recorded in a morning batch
- Investigations finishing too late to complete resolution the same day

This is important because it affects both escalated and non-escalated tickets. Therefore, escalation is **not the only cause**.

### C. Delays before investigation

Some cases wait too long after assignment:

- **Case 104:** 3h 30m from assignment to investigation
- **Case 102:** 5 hours from Level-1 assignment through escalation to investigation
- **Case 105:** 28 hours from escalation to Level-2 investigation

Possible causes include agent workload, queue congestion, ineffective routing, or tickets sitting without clear ownership.

Case 104 is particularly informative because it has no escalation but still takes more than 24 hours. Its delays point to a Level-1 capacity or workflow issue rather than a specialist-team problem alone.

### D. Triage and closure are secondary issues

Triage is somewhat slower in cases 102 and 104, but the delays are measured in tens of minutes rather than many hours. Similarly, closing takes only 15–30 minutes in all cases. These are not the main drivers of the extended cycle times.

## 3. Recommendations

1. **Investigate the recurring 19-hour delay.**  
   Record explicit waiting reasons after investigation, such as “awaiting customer,” “awaiting approval,” “vendor dependency,” or “outside support hours.” Determine whether work is actually waiting or whether the resolution event is simply recorded the next morning.

2. **Set transition-level service targets.**  
   Monitor more than total ticket age. Useful targets include:
   - Assignment to first investigation
   - Escalation to Level-2 acceptance
   - Final investigation to resolution
   - Total time spent in each queue

3. **Improve escalation handling.**
   - Introduce an escalation response SLA.
   - Assign clear ownership during the handoff.
   - Require diagnostic notes and evidence before escalation.
   - Use warm handoffs for urgent or aging tickets.
   - Alert managers when an escalated ticket remains untouched beyond a threshold.

4. **Route complex tickets earlier.**  
   Better triage and skill-based routing could send specialist issues directly to Level 2, avoiding time in the Level-1 queue and reducing duplicate investigation.

5. **Review staffing and coverage.**  
   The overnight pattern may justify extended coverage, an on-call specialist, or late-day prioritization of investigated tickets that can be resolved before the end of the shift.

6. **Use aging alerts and queue balancing.**  
   Cases approaching SLA limits should be automatically prioritized or reassigned to available agents.

7. **Validate the findings on a larger log.**  
   This sample is small, and timestamps alone cannot distinguish active work from waiting. Additional data on agent, team, priority, issue type, business hours, and waiting reason would help confirm whether the cause is capacity, complexity, external dependency, or process design.

Overall, the strongest bottlenecks are the **Level-2 escalation queue**, especially in case 105, and the consistent **overnight investigation-to-resolution delay** across cases 102, 104, and 105.
