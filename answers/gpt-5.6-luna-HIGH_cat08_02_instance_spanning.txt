## 1. Identifying Instance-Spanning Constraints and Their Impact

### 1.1 Prepare the event log for multi-instance analysis

A conventional case-centric event log is useful for reconstructing each order's path, but it is not sufficient by itself to represent all dependencies. The analysis should extend the log into an object-centric or multi-perspective event log containing:

- **Order object:** order ID, order type, destination, cold-packing flag, hazardous-material flag, promised delivery time.
- **Activity instance:** start and completion timestamps, activity, resource, station, status.
- **Resource object:** station ID, resource pool, capacity, availability calendar.
- **Batch object:** batch ID, region, batch creation time, release time, members, route cutoff.
- **Interruption object:** suspend, resume, reason, and order that caused the interruption.
- **Regulatory admission events:** hazardous order admitted to or released from the packing/QC capacity pool.

The current log contains some of this information, but important fields such as queue entry, batch release, preemption, and resume events may need to be added. For example, ORD-5001 has a Quality Check completion at 10:35:15 and a Shipping Label completion at 10:45:50. The approximately 10-minute gap may include batching delay, label-generation processing, or both. Without a label start and batch-release event, those causes cannot be separated reliably.

Useful process mining techniques include:

- Performance-enhanced process maps and directly-follows graphs.
- Case and resource variant analysis.
- Queue and bottleneck analysis.
- Conformance checking against capacity and priority rules.
- Object-centric process mining for orders, stations, batches, and staff.
- Temporal rule checking, such as “no more than 10 hazardous orders are active in Packing or Quality Check.”
- Process simulation and counterfactual replay to estimate what would have happened without a constraint.

### 1.2 Shared cold-packing stations

For each order requiring cold packing, calculate:

\[
W_{\text{cold}} = \text{Cold Packing Start} - \text{Packing Ready Time}
\]

where Packing Ready Time is normally the completion of Item Picking, plus any necessary handoff or preparation time.

The analysis should reconstruct the occupancy of the five cold stations by creating intervals between cold-packing start and completion. At any time \(t\):

\[
\text{Cold Occupancy}(t) =
\text{number of active cold-packing orders at }t
\]

Important measures include:

- Average, median, and 95th-percentile cold-packing queue wait.
- Percentage of cold orders that started while all five stations were occupied.
- Average and maximum queue length.
- Percentage of time all five stations were busy.
- Cold-station utilization by hour, day, region, and peak period.
- Number of orders delayed by each station pool.
- Cold-packing wait as a percentage of total order cycle time.
- Cold orders missing their promised dispatch time due to cold-station contention.
- Station imbalance: whether some stations are underused while others are overloaded.
- Number and duration of cold-packing interruptions caused by express orders.

A performance map could show that cold orders have a substantially longer wait between Item Picking and Packing than non-cold orders, particularly during peak hours. To demonstrate that the delay is caused by contention rather than inherently longer processing, compare:

- Cold-packing service time versus cold-packing queue time.
- Cold orders arriving when all five stations are busy versus when one or more are idle.
- Similar orders across different shifts and demand levels.

### 1.3 Shipping batches

Batching should be modeled as a separate object rather than simply as a property of the Shipping Label Generation activity.

For each order, define:

\[
W_{\text{batch}} =
\text{Batch Release Time} - \text{Order Ready-for-Label Time}
\]

The Order Ready-for-Label Time is generally Quality Check completion. This wait should be separated from the actual label-generation service time.

Measures include:

- Average and 95th-percentile waiting time for a batch to form.
- Time from the first order becoming ready to batch release.
- Batch size and fill rate.
- Number of orders in each batch.
- Percentage of batches released below the target size.
- Percentage of orders waiting beyond a maximum allowed batch age.
- Batch wait by region, service level, time of day, and destination cutoff.
- Number of orders missing dispatch or delivery cutoffs because of batching.
- Route efficiency or transportation cost achieved by batching.
- Number of express orders held in standard batches.
- Number of partial or exception batches created.

The analysis should identify whether a batch waits for:

1. A minimum number of orders.
2. A specific region member.
3. A route cutoff.
4. A hazardous order or other special order.
5. A system or label-generation resource.

These are different operational problems and require different remedies.

### 1.4 Priority handling and express-order interruptions

For express orders, reconstruct the priority sequence at each shared step. If the log contains suspend and resume events, calculate:

\[
W_{\text{preemption}} =
\text{Resume Time} - \text{Suspend Time}
\]

for an interrupted standard order, separating the actual interruption from any subsequent queue wait.

If interruption events are not recorded, infer likely preemptions from:

- A standard order's activity being split into multiple intervals.
- A change in resource assignment.
- An express order starting immediately after a standard order is suspended.
- A standard order's unusually long activity duration compared with the same activity and resource.

Metrics should include:

- Number of standard orders interrupted by express orders.
- Total and average interruption duration.
- Additional standard-order delay attributable to express priority.
- Express-order waiting time before and after the policy.
- Express SLA attainment.
- Standard SLA attainment and weighted tardiness.
- Number of standard orders experiencing starvation or repeated interruptions.
- Priority benefit: the reduction in express waiting time resulting from preemption.
- Priority cost: additional delay imposed on standard orders.

The strongest estimate of priority impact comes from counterfactual replay. For example, compare the actual schedule with a schedule using the same arrivals and service times but without preemption. The difference in standard-order completion time is an estimate of the priority cost. A second scenario can compare different policies, such as strict priority, earliest-due-date priority, or priority with aging.

### 1.5 Hazardous-material concurrency limit

For every hazardous order, construct an activity interval covering the period during which the order is undergoing Packing or Quality Check. If the order moves directly from Packing to Quality Check, the two intervals should be combined so that it is counted once, not twice.

At time \(t\):

\[
H(t) =
\left|\{\text{hazardous orders active in Packing or Quality Check at }t\}\right|
\]

The hard compliance rule is:

\[
H(t) \leq 10 \quad \text{for all }t
\]

Measures include:

- Maximum hazardous-material concurrency.
- Number of periods at or near the limit, for example \(H(t) \geq 9\).
- Total hours during which all 10 hazardous capacity slots were occupied.
- Number of hazardous orders waiting for admission.
- Average and 95th-percentile hazardous admission wait.
- Number of starts blocked because the limit was already reached.
- Hazardous orders' throughput and cycle time.
- Packing and QC utilization for hazardous orders.
- Number of regulatory violations; this should remain zero.
- Estimated throughput reduction due to the cap.

The last measure should not be estimated by hypothetically violating the regulation in production. It should be estimated through simulation or counterfactual scheduling with the cap relaxed only in the model. This gives an estimate of the capacity sacrificed by the constraint while preserving compliance in reality.

### 1.6 Separating within-instance and between-instance waiting

For each activity, reconstruct the following times:

1. **Service time**

\[
S = \text{Completion} - \text{Start}
\]

2. **Internal precedence wait**

\[
W_{\text{internal}} =
\text{Next Activity Ready Time} -
\text{Previous Activity Completion}
\]

This may reflect handoffs, missing information, rework, or a genuine within-order dependency.

3. **Resource queue wait**

\[
W_{\text{resource}} =
\text{Activity Start} - \text{Activity Ready Time}
\]

when the activity was ready but the required resource was unavailable.

4. **Batch wait**

The time from being ready for label generation until the batch is released.

5. **Interruption wait**

The time an order is suspended because another order receives priority.

6. **Regulatory wait**

The time a hazardous order is ready but cannot begin Packing or Quality Check because the global limit is reached.

The distinction can be made most accurately if the log records queue-entry, queue-exit, admission, suspension, and batch-release events. If those events are absent, resource calendars and overlapping activity intervals can be used for inference, but the result should be labeled as estimated.

A useful order-level decomposition is:

\[
\text{Cycle Time} =
\sum S_i +
\sum W_{\text{internal},i} +
\sum W_{\text{resource},i} +
W_{\text{batch}} +
W_{\text{priority}} +
W_{\text{regulatory}}
\]

The components may overlap in complex situations, so an attribution hierarchy or counterfactual analysis should be used to avoid double counting. For example, a standard cold order may be delayed by an express order occupying a cold station. Its primary cause is cold-station unavailability, with express priority as the contributing cause.

## 2. Analyzing Constraint Interactions

The constraints should not be optimized independently because they influence one another.

### Express priority and cold-packing capacity

An express order requiring cold packing can displace a standard order at one of only five cold stations. This may improve express SLA performance but increase:

- Standard cold-order queue length.
- Repeated interruptions.
- Completion time for a standard order that was nearly finished.
- Cold-station setup or restart time.
- The probability that several standard orders miss a shipping batch cutoff.

If several express cold orders arrive together, strict priority can create a second-order effect: express demand consumes the complete cold-packing capacity, causing a large standard backlog.

### Batching and priority

If label generation waits for a full regional batch, an express order may be delayed unnecessarily. Conversely, allowing express orders to bypass normal batching may produce small, inefficient shipments or leave a standard batch underfilled.

A batch policy should therefore distinguish between:

- Express orders with very little delivery slack.
- Standard orders with enough time to wait.
- Orders that can be shipped in partial batches.
- Orders tied to a hard route departure or carrier cutoff.

### Batching and hazardous-material limits

If several hazardous orders destined for the same region are processed together, the region may create a large burst of hazardous demand at Packing and Quality Check. The batch itself occurs later, but its demand pattern can create upstream synchronization and a queue at the regulatory capacity limit.

If a shipping batch cannot be released until all members are ready, a single hazardous order delayed by the 10-order limit may hold an entire regional batch. This creates a delay that appears to be a batching problem but is actually caused by regulatory admission.

A better policy should distinguish between:

- A batch waiting because the required number of orders has not arrived.
- A batch waiting for a hazardous order.
- A batch waiting for transport or route cutoff.
- Orders that can safely be released as a partial shipment.

### Hazardous orders and express priority

An express hazardous order can consume two scarce dimensions at once:

- It may require a cold station.
- It requires one of the ten permitted hazardous slots.
- It may receive priority over non-express hazardous orders.

This creates the risk that a locally optimal decision—starting an express hazardous order immediately—creates a wider regulatory or cold-packing queue. The scheduler must evaluate the combined resource requirement, not just the order's priority.

### Resource utilization and batching waves

A long batch wait may cause several orders to be released together. If these orders then arrive at Packing, Quality Check, or cold-packing stations in a wave, utilization may become highly variable:

- Idle periods while a batch forms.
- Overloaded periods when the batch is released.
- Increased probability of priority interruptions.
- More frequent activation of the hazardous-material limit.

This is why average utilization alone is insufficient. Variability, queue age, and synchronized arrivals must also be measured.

## 3. Constraint-Aware Optimization Strategies

### Strategy 1: Implement an integrated finite-capacity dispatching policy

**Primary constraints addressed:** Cold-packing capacity, express priority, hazardous-material limit.

Instead of dispatching each activity independently, use a scheduler that considers:

- Order priority and promised delivery time.
- Whether the order needs cold packing.
- Whether it is hazardous.
- Remaining batch or carrier cutoff time.
- Estimated activity duration.
- Current occupancy of cold stations and hazardous capacity.
- The effect of starting the order on downstream queues.

A practical policy could include:

1. **Priority tiers:** Express orders receive priority, but priority is combined with due-date slack rather than being unlimited.
2. **Aging:** Standard orders gain priority as their wait increases, preventing starvation.
3. **Safe-point preemption:** A standard order may be interrupted only at a defined safe point, with its progress recorded and preserved.
4. **Dynamic cold-station reservation:** Reserve some cold capacity for imminent express orders only when forecast demand and delivery slack justify it. Release unused reserved capacity to standard orders.
5. **Global hazardous admission control:** Treat the ten hazardous slots as a shared semaphore. An order cannot enter Packing or Quality Check unless a slot is available.
6. **Combined-resource feasibility:** An express hazardous cold order is admitted only when both a cold station and a hazardous slot are available.

The scheduling score might combine:

- Delivery slack.
- Express status.
- Queue age.
- Estimated processing time.
- Batch value.
- Resource scarcity.
- Hazardous-slot availability.

Historical event data can estimate arrival patterns by hour and forecast when cold or hazardous capacity will become scarce.

**Expected outcomes:**

- Lower cold-packing queue time.
- Better express SLA attainment.
- Fewer repeated interruptions.
- Lower standard-order starvation.
- Zero hazardous-material capacity violations.
- More balanced use of cold stations.

The policy should be tested carefully because maximizing cold-station utilization without considering due dates could worsen customer-facing performance.

### Strategy 2: Replace fixed batching with dynamic, deadline-aware batching

**Primary constraints addressed:** Shipping batch delays, interaction between batching and priority, regional demand variability.

Use a hybrid batch-release rule:

Release a batch when any of the following occurs:

- The target batch size is reached.
- The oldest order reaches a maximum batch wait.
- A route or carrier cutoff is approaching.
- The order's delivery slack falls below a threshold.
- An express order requires immediate release.
- A partial shipment is economically preferable to additional waiting.

Batch thresholds should vary by:

- Destination region.
- Time of day.
- Carrier departure schedule.
- Historical order arrival rate.
- Service level.
- Cost of a partial shipment.
- Expected risk of missing a delivery promise.

Express orders should not automatically wait for a standard batch. Options include:

- An express-only batch.
- A smaller express batch.
- Immediate release when the order's slack is low.
- A pre-booked carrier capacity window.

For standard regional batches, a “ready subset” can be released when appropriate instead of waiting indefinitely for one late member. The system should record the reason for every batch delay, such as “waiting for fill,” “waiting for route cutoff,” or “waiting for hazardous order.”

Historical process mining results can estimate the cost-benefit curve between batch size and waiting time. For example, the company may discover that increasing a batch from six to eight orders produces only a small transport saving but adds 20 minutes of average waiting time.

**Expected outcomes:**

- Lower batch formation time.
- Fewer orders waiting beyond the permitted age.
- Fewer missed carrier cutoffs.
- Better express performance.
- More predictable release waves into downstream processes.
- Preservation of transport savings where batching genuinely adds value.

### Strategy 3: Introduce a hazardous-material capacity controller and smooth demand

**Primary constraints addressed:** Hazardous-material concurrency limit, interactions with priority and batching.

The regulatory rule should be implemented as an explicit real-time control mechanism rather than inferred informally by supervisors.

The controller should:

- Maintain a live count of hazardous orders active in Packing or Quality Check.
- Admit an order only if the count remains below 10.
- Reserve or release capacity based on actual activity state.
- Count an order once across the combined Packing/QC interval.
- Use conservative treatment for paused orders until compliance confirms that a paused order no longer counts as “undergoing” the activity.
- Provide warning states at nine active orders.
- Prevent a dispatching policy from starting a new hazardous order when the limit is reached.

Demand can be smoothed by:

- Releasing hazardous orders in a controlled sequence rather than in large waves.
- Prioritizing the most urgent orders with the shortest delivery slack.
- Improving preparation outside the regulated activities.
- Cross-training additional packing and QC staff to reduce the duration of each hazardous slot.
- Creating a dedicated compliant hazardous lane if this reduces activity duration, without exceeding the ten-order global limit.
- Coordinating hazardous order timing with regional batch cutoffs.

Additional stations do not automatically permit more than ten hazardous orders to be active. They can, however, reduce the duration of each hazardous order and improve the turnover of the ten permitted slots.

**Expected outcomes:**

- Continued zero regulatory violations.
- Lower hazardous admission wait.
- Higher throughput per hazardous capacity hour.
- Fewer hazardous orders delaying regional batches.
- Better treatment of express hazardous orders without uncontrolled priority effects.

### Strategy 4: Reduce the time that scarce resources are occupied

**Primary constraints addressed:** Cold-packing contention, hazardous capacity, interruption effects.

Process redesign can reduce the duration of constrained activities rather than only trying to schedule them better.

Potential changes include:

- Pre-stage packaging materials before a cold order reaches the station.
- Perform non-temperature-sensitive preparation at a standard station and reserve the cold station for the temperature-controlled portion.
- Standardize cold-packing work instructions to reduce variation.
- Use specialized staff or tools for fast turnover during forecast peaks.
- Pre-stage hazardous documentation and safety materials before the order enters Packing.
- Prepare QC information or checklists before the hazardous order arrives at Quality Check.
- Move shipping data preparation earlier, while keeping final label release after QC approval.
- Decouple shipping-label staging from final batch release so that the label-generation system does not become part of the critical path unnecessarily.

The event log can identify high-variance or low-value work within cold Packing and hazardous Packing/QC. If, for example, 30% of cold-station occupancy is caused by waiting for materials or documentation, eliminating that internal delay creates capacity without adding a station.

**Expected outcomes:**

- Shorter cold-station service times.
- Faster turnover of hazardous slots.
- Less sensitivity to demand peaks.
- Lower impact of express preemption.
- More predictable batch readiness.

### Strategy 5: Add flexible capacity based on forecasted demand

**Primary constraints addressed:** Cold-packing peaks and labor-related bottlenecks.

Using historical order arrivals, estimate cold and hazardous demand by hour, weekday, season, and promotion. Then:

- Schedule additional trained staff during forecast cold-demand peaks.
- Cross-train standard packing staff to operate approved cold stations.
- Convert an eligible standard station into a cold station when needed, subject to food-safety and equipment requirements.
- Consider additional cold capacity if the simulation shows that the investment materially improves SLA performance.
- Use temporary or mobile capacity during known seasonal peaks.

This strategy should be evaluated against demand smoothing and scheduling changes. If the cold queue is caused primarily by poor dispatching, adding stations may not be the best first intervention.

## 4. Simulation and Validation

A discrete-event simulation or process-mining-based digital twin should be built before major operational changes are implemented.

### 4.1 Calibrate the model from the event log

The model should use historical data to estimate:

- Order arrival distributions by time and season.
- Activity durations by order type, station, staff member, and complexity.
- Probability of cold-packing and hazardous-material requirements.
- Express-order arrival patterns.
- Resource availability, breaks, and downtime.
- Batch arrivals by region.
- Carrier and route cutoff times.
- Probability and duration of interruptions.
- Rework and quality-failure behavior.

A useful approach is to replay historical order arrivals through the proposed model and compare simulated results with a holdout period from the event log.

Validation should compare:

- End-to-end cycle-time distributions.
- Stage-level waiting times.
- Cold-station utilization and queue length.
- Batch sizes and batch formation times.
- Express and standard SLA attainment.
- Hazardous concurrency patterns.
- Peak-period throughput.

### 4.2 Model the constraints explicitly

The simulation must not treat each order as independent. It should include:

#### Cold-packing resources

- Five cold stations as a finite-capacity resource pool.
- Station-specific speed or eligibility differences.
- Queue discipline and dispatch rules.
- Preemption and resume behavior.
- Setup or restart time after interruption.
- Station calendars and breakdowns.

#### Batching

- Batch objects with region compatibility.
- Minimum and maximum batch sizes.
- Batch creation and release events.
- Maximum waiting age.
- Carrier cutoff times.
- Partial-batch rules.
- Express bypass or express-only batches.

#### Priority

- Express and standard priority classes.
- Due-date slack.
- Safe-point preemption.
- Resource release during interruptions.
- Starvation prevention through aging.
- Additional setup time caused by interruption, if observed.

#### Hazardous-material regulation

- A global capacity semaphore of ten.
- A hazardous order counted once while active in Packing or Quality Check.
- Admission control when all ten slots are occupied.
- Correct treatment of paused hazardous orders.
- Zero-tolerance compliance monitoring.

### 4.3 Compare alternative scenarios

At minimum, simulate:

1. Current operating policy.
2. Integrated cold/priority/hazardous scheduling.
3. Dynamic batching only.
4. Hazardous admission control only.
5. Reduced activity durations through process redesign.
6. Additional cold capacity.
7. Combined strategies.

The simulation should be run across normal and peak scenarios. Results should include confidence intervals rather than relying on one random run. Common random-number techniques can be used so that alternative policies are compared under the same arrival and service-time conditions.

Key outputs include:

- Throughput per shift.
- End-to-end median and 95th-percentile cycle time.
- On-time shipment and delivery percentage.
- Express SLA attainment.
- Standard-order delay and fairness.
- Cold-packing queue wait and utilization.
- Batch wait and batch fill rate.
- Hazardous admission wait.
- Maximum hazardous concurrency and number of violations.
- Labor and equipment utilization.
- Partial shipment and transport-cost effects.

The best policy is not necessarily the one with the highest utilization. It should optimize customer-facing performance while maintaining compliance and avoiding unacceptable delays to standard orders.

## 5. Monitoring After Implementation

A set of process mining dashboards should monitor both normal performance and the health of the shared constraints.

### 5.1 End-to-end fulfillment dashboard

Track:

- Orders received, shipped, and completed per hour.
- Median, 90th, and 95th-percentile fulfillment time.
- On-time shipment and delivery rate.
- WIP by activity.
- Aging orders and orders at risk of missing delivery promises.
- Performance by express versus standard orders.
- Performance by destination region and peak period.

A critical-path view should show whether delays are primarily due to Picking, cold Packing, Quality Check, batching, or Shipping Label Generation.

### 5.2 Cold-packing dashboard

Track in real time and historically:

- Current cold queue length.
- Number of busy cold stations out of five.
- Cold-station utilization.
- Percentage of time all five stations are occupied.
- Average and 95th-percentile cold wait.
- Oldest cold order waiting.
- Express versus standard cold queue composition.
- Preemption count and duration.
- Station-level utilization and idle time.
- Cold orders at risk of missing a cutoff.

Alerts could be generated when:

- All five stations are occupied for a sustained period.
- The oldest cold order exceeds its maximum wait.
- A standard order has been interrupted repeatedly.
- A station is idle while eligible cold orders are waiting.

### 5.3 Batch dashboard

Monitor:

- Open batches by region.
- Age of the oldest order in each batch.
- Current batch size versus target size.
- Average and 95th-percentile batch formation time.
- Orders waiting for batch completion.
- Partial-batch release rate.
- Batch delays by reason.
- Orders missing carrier cutoffs because of batching.
- Express orders held in standard batches.
- Transport savings versus additional customer waiting time.

This dashboard should distinguish beneficial batching from excessive batching.

### 5.4 Priority dashboard

Track:

- Express cycle time and SLA attainment.
- Standard cycle time and SLA attainment.
- Number of express-induced interruptions.
- Average delay imposed on standard orders.
- Orders experiencing repeated preemption.
- Aging of the oldest standard order.
- Priority decisions by resource and time period.
- Express benefit compared with a non-priority counterfactual.

This helps ensure that priority handling improves express performance without creating systematic starvation of standard orders.

### 5.5 Hazardous-material compliance dashboard

This should be treated as a safety and compliance dashboard, not only a performance dashboard.

Monitor:

- Current hazardous concurrency.
- Maximum concurrency by shift and day.
- Time spent at 9 or 10 active hazardous orders.
- Hazardous admission queue.
- Average and 95th-percentile regulatory wait.
- Number of blocked hazardous starts.
- Hazardous throughput per hour.
- Packing and QC duration for hazardous orders.
- Compliance violations, which must remain zero.

The system should issue an immediate alert if the calculated concurrency reaches 10 and should prevent an eleventh admission through a hard control, not merely a visual warning.

### 5.6 Interaction and conformance monitoring

Additional dashboards should show:

- Cold wait by express status and hazardous flag.
- Hazardous wait by destination region.
- Batch delay caused by one late hazardous order.
- Standard delay following express cold-order arrivals.
- Correlation between batch releases and cold-station queue spikes.
- Resource contention heatmaps by hour.
- Policy conformance, such as whether orders were admitted without a cold station or hazardous slot.
- Drift in activity durations or arrival patterns compared with the simulation assumptions.

Control charts and weekly process-mining refreshes can identify whether improvements are sustained or whether peak-season behavior is beginning to differ from the original model.

The overall objective is to manage the constraints as measurable shared-capacity mechanisms rather than treating each order as an isolated case. This allows the company to reduce waiting time, protect express commitments, improve batching efficiency, and increase throughput while preserving the hazardous-material safety limit.
