**This visualization illustrates the Proclets framework**, a process-modeling approach (developed by van der Aalst and others) for handling complex, intertwined processes with one-to-many or many-to-many relationships between entities. Proclets are lightweight, interacting process fragments (object/life-cycle-centric) that communicate via channels using structured messages called *performatives*, rather than forcing everything into one monolithic workflow.

The example models a simple healthcare scenario: a patient lab visit that may require multiple laboratory tests (e.g., blood tests), with possible re-testing and result reconciliation.

### (a) Two proclet classes connected through two channels
This is the main process diagram showing the life-cycles of the two interacting proclets:

- **Lab visit** (left side) – Instantiated once per patient visit. Roles involved: doctor and lab assistant.  
  Typical flow:  
  - Doctor performs *Order entry*.  
  - Lab assistant *Take[s] blood sample*.  
  - Results are received and judged (*Receive result* → *Judge results*).  
  - Decision point (“Everything fine”): if OK → *Send report* and end; if not → *Do tests again*, receive updated results, and possibly loop.  
  - Ends at a black square (termination).

- **Lab test** (right side) – Instantiated once per individual test (e.g., HGB/hemoglobin). Role: chemical analyst.  
  Typical flow:  
  - *Perform test* → *Make report*.  
  - Can loop via *Perform test again* / *Provide updated report* if needed.  
  - Ends via *Finish lab test*.

**Interaction** occurs through two channels (the dashed boxes in the middle):
- **Order system** – Used to create/initiate lab-test instances (outgoing from Lab visit after sampling/ordering).
- **HIS** (Hospital Information System) – Used to send results and updated results back from Lab test to Lab visit.

Ports on the tasks have annotations such as `1:1`, `1:*`, `*:1`, etc. These indicate:
- **Cardinality** (left of the colon): how many recipients a message goes to (1 = exactly one, * = zero or more, etc.).
- **Multiplicity** (right of the colon): how many times a performative is exchanged over that port during the lifetime of one proclet instance.

Arrows show the direction of performatives. This design cleanly separates the life-cycle of a visit from the life-cycles of its individual tests while still allowing them to interact.

### (b) Class diagram containing the two proclet classes
A simple UML-style class diagram showing the structural relationship:

- A **Lab visit** (multiplicity 1..1) **requires** **Lab test**(s) (multiplicity 1..*).

This captures that one lab visit can spawn multiple independent lab-test instances (one-to-many).

### (c) Example of a performative
A concrete message exchanged over a channel:

| Time  | Channel      | Sender          | Receivers         | Action | Content                          | Scope  | Direction |
|-------|--------------|-----------------|-------------------|--------|----------------------------------|--------|-----------|
| 11:00 | Order system | Lab visit – John| Lab test – HGB John | Create | Can you perform a HGB test for John? | Private | OUT      |

This is the creation message that instantiates a specific Lab-test proclet (hemoglobin test for patient John) from the Lab-visit proclet. Performatives are stored in a knowledge base and can be queried by tasks.

### Why this approach?
Traditional workflow nets struggle with dynamic one-to-many relationships and loosely coupled, concurrent life-cycles. Proclets “divide, interact, and conquer”: each entity type has its own simple process model, and interactions are made explicit via channels and performatives. This is especially useful in healthcare (and similar domains) where cases involve multiple related objects (patients, tests, samples, reports, etc.) whose life-cycles interleave in flexible ways.
