## 1. Process Discovery and Conformance Checking

### 1.1 Data preprocessing and event-log integration

A suitable process-mining log should contain, at minimum:

- **Case ID:** normally a vehicle-operational-day, such as `V12-20241205`
- **Activity:** for example, `Start Shift`, `Depart Depot`, `Travel`, `Arrive Customer`, `Delivery Success`, `Delivery Failed`, `Unscheduled Stop`, `Maintenance`, `Arrive Depot`, and `End Shift`
- **Timestamp:** preferably both start and completion timestamps where available
- **Resource:** vehicle, driver, depot, and possibly route planner
- **Package ID:** where the event concerns a particular parcel
- **Location:** GPS coordinates or geocoded stop/site
- **Planned attributes:** planned sequence, planned arrival, planned departure, customer time window, planned route
- **Operational attributes:** speed, distance, fuel, vehicle status, failure reason, traffic condition, maintenance reason

A practical integration process would be:

1. **Standardize timestamps**
   - Convert all systems to one time zone.
   - Correct clock drift between GPS, handheld devices, and dispatch systems.
   - Retain both raw and corrected timestamps for auditability.

2. **Clean and validate source data**
   - Remove duplicate scanner events.
   - Detect impossible sequences, such as `Depart Customer` before `Arrive Customer`.
   - Identify missing GPS points, implausible coordinates, and impossible speeds.
   - Reconcile inconsistent vehicle or driver identifiers.

3. **Map GPS events into business activities**
   Raw GPS points should not all become process events. They can be transformed into meaningful states using geofences and rules:
   - Depot geofence entry/exit  `Arrive Depot`, `Depart Depot`
   - Customer geofence entry  `Arrive Customer`
   - Vehicle stopped for a threshold period  `Stop` or `Idle`
   - Movement between stops  `Travel`
   - Unexpected stationary period  `Unscheduled Stop`
   - Maintenance location or warning code  `Maintenance Event`

4. **Match events across systems**
   - Link scanner events to GPS-derived arrivals and departures.
   - Match packages to the assigned route and actual vehicle.
   - Link maintenance records to the vehicle-day case and relevant downtime period.
   - Resolve events where a driver changes vehicle during a shift.

5. **Create event intervals**
   Many activities have duration rather than only a timestamp:
   - Travel: departure from one location to arrival at another
   - Service: customer arrival to customer departure
   - Maintenance: repair start to repair completion
   - Idle or traffic delay: start and end of low-speed or stationary condition

6. **Construct multiple analytical views**
   A single case model may not answer every question. Useful views include:
   - **Vehicle-day case:** operational route and resource utilization
   - **Package case:** depot handling through successful or failed delivery
   - **Stop case:** arrival, service, departure, and outcome
   - **Maintenance case:** warning, breakdown, repair, return to service

The principal vehicle-day event log could resemble:

`Start Shift  Route Assigned  Load/Scan Depot  Depart Depot  Travel  Arrive Customer  Delivery Success  Depart Customer  Travel  ...  Unscheduled Stop  Travel  Arrive Depot  End Shift`

Package identifiers and planned sequence numbers should be retained as attributes rather than necessarily defining the primary case.

### 1.2 Main data challenges

Important challenges include:

- **Different levels of granularity:** GPS records points every few seconds, whereas scanner systems record milestone events.
- **Clock differences:** source systems may not have synchronized clocks.
- **Missing or late scans:** drivers may scan after leaving a customer or omit a departure scan.
- **Ambiguous stops:** a vehicle may stop for parking, congestion, loading, personal reasons, or mechanical problems.
- **GPS accuracy:** urban canyons and weak signals can distort the vehicle location.
- **Data association:** several packages may be delivered at one stop, and one customer may have multiple parcels.
- **Planned-versus-actual mismatch:** planned routes may change during the day without the dispatch system retaining every revision.
- **Maintenance overlap:** scheduled maintenance may appear to overlap with a route because of inaccurate start or end times.
- **Privacy and governance:** driver performance analysis should use appropriate access controls and avoid unfair conclusions based on poor-quality data.

Data-quality dashboards should therefore be built before interpreting operational performance.

### 1.3 Process discovery

After integration, discovery algorithms can be applied to identify the actual process.

Useful approaches include:

- **Directly-Follows Graphs:** simple and highly interpretable; show common transitions and their frequencies.
- **Heuristics Miner:** filters infrequent GPS-derived noise and is useful when the process is variable.
- **Inductive Miner:** produces a structured model and is often suitable for conformance checking.
- **Process maps with performance overlays:** show average, median, and percentile durations on each transition.
- **Trace clustering:** groups similar vehicle-day routes into operational variants.

The discovered model should show:

- Depot preparation and departure delays
- Travel between stops
- Stop sequence and skipped or reordered stops
- Successful and failed delivery outcomes
- Re-delivery or return-to-depot patterns
- Unscheduled stops
- Traffic-related low-speed periods
- Maintenance interruptions
- Return to depot and overtime

For example, one discovered variant might be:

`Route Assigned  Late Departure  Travel/Traffic Delay  Multiple Successful Deliveries  Failed Delivery  Unscheduled Mechanical Stop  Reduced Route Completion  Late Depot Return  Overtime`

This is more informative than merely calculating average route duration because it reveals the operational sequence that produces poor outcomes.

### 1.4 Conformance checking against planned routes

The dispatch system provides the reference model or planned trace:

- Planned stop sequence
- Planned arrival and departure times
- Assigned vehicle and driver
- Planned package load
- Customer time windows
- Planned route geometry

Conformance checking can use:

- **Alignment-based conformance:** calculates the minimum-cost edits needed to align actual and planned traces.
- **Token-based replay:** identifies missing, unexpected, or incorrectly ordered events.
- **Deviation tables:** summarize deviations by vehicle, route, driver, area, and date.
- **Route and timestamp comparison:** compare actual GPS paths and stop times with planned routes.

Deviations to examine include:

#### Sequence deviations
- Stops visited out of order
- Stops skipped
- Unplanned stops inserted
- Packages delivered on a different vehicle
- Repeated visits to the same customer

#### Timing deviations
- Late departure from depot
- Arrival before or after the customer time window
- Excessive service duration
- Long gaps between planned stops
- Late return and overtime

#### Geographic deviations
- Excess distance
- Route detours
- Travel outside the assigned territory
- Repeated backtracking

#### Resource deviations
- Vehicle substitution
- Driver substitution
- Capacity overload or underuse
- Maintenance occurring during an assigned route

#### Outcome deviations
- Failed delivery where success was expected
- Packages not attempted
- Re-delivery caused by a previous failed attempt

The cost of each deviation should be estimated in terms of minutes, kilometres, fuel, missed deliveries, and overtime.

## 2. Performance Analysis and Bottleneck Identification

### 2.1 Key performance indicators

#### On-Time Delivery Rate

For delivery \(i\):

\[
OTD_i =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if actual delivery time is within the promised window} \\
0, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\]

\[
OTD = \frac{\text{on-time successful deliveries}}{\text{all eligible deliveries}}
\]

This should be reported separately for early, late, failed, and rescheduled deliveries.

#### Average time per delivery stop

\[
\text{Stop Time} =
\text{Depart Customer} - \text{Arrive Customer}
\]

Report:

- Mean
- Median
- 90th and 95th percentiles
- Results by driver, route, customer type, area, and delivery outcome

#### Travel time versus service time

\[
\text{Travel-Service Ratio} =
\frac{\text{total travel duration}}{\text{total customer service duration}}
\]

A high travel ratio may indicate poor route density or excessive geographic spread. A high service ratio may indicate parking, access, customer interaction, or scanning problems.

#### Fuel consumption

If fuel data is available:

\[
\text{Fuel per km} =
\frac{\text{fuel consumed}}{\text{actual kilometres}}
\]

\[
\text{Fuel per package} =
\frac{\text{fuel consumed}}{\text{packages delivered or attempted}}
\]

If direct fuel data is unavailable, an estimate can be derived from distance, vehicle type, speed, idle time, and stop frequency, but it should be clearly labelled as an estimate.

#### Vehicle utilization

Possible definitions include:

\[
\text{Utilization} =
\frac{\text{productive operating time}}{\text{available shift time}}
\]

or:

\[
\text{Capacity Utilization} =
\frac{\text{packages loaded}}{\text{vehicle capacity}}
\]

Both are useful. A vehicle may have high time utilization but poor capacity utilization.

#### Traffic delay

Traffic delay can be estimated by comparing:

- Actual travel time
- Planned or free-flow travel time
- Historical travel time for the same road segment and time period

\[
\text{Traffic Delay} =
\text{Actual Travel Time} - \text{Expected Travel Time}
\]

Track both frequency and duration of traffic events.

#### Failed delivery rate

\[
\text{Failure Rate} =
\frac{\text{failed delivery attempts}}{\text{all delivery attempts}}
\]

Break it down by reason, customer, time window, route, and driver.

#### Other useful KPIs

- Depot departure punctuality
- Route completion rate
- Packages delivered per driver-hour
- Overtime hours per route
- Unscheduled stop frequency
- Breakdown minutes per 100 route-hours
- Maintenance cost per vehicle-kilometre
- Excess kilometres over plan
- Idle time and low-speed time
- Re-delivery rate
- First-attempt success rate

### 2.2 Bottleneck analysis techniques

Process mining can identify bottlenecks using several complementary views.

#### Performance-enhanced process maps

Overlay average and percentile durations on transitions such as:

- `Route Assigned  Depart Depot`
- `Arrive Customer  Delivery Success`
- `Delivery Success  Depart Customer`
- `Depart Customer  Next Arrive Customer`
- `Unscheduled Stop  Resume Travel`

This distinguishes occasional extreme delays from consistently slow activities.

#### Waiting-time and throughput analysis

Measure:

- Time between route assignment and departure
- Time between customer arrival and service completion
- Time between consecutive stops
- Time spent idle or in low-speed movement
- Time lost to maintenance

#### Variant analysis

Compare high-performing and low-performing traces based on:

- On-time rate
- Cost per package
- Route completion
- Overtime

For example, low-performing variants may contain:

- Late departure
- More out-of-sequence stops
- More failed deliveries
- Higher traffic exposure
- Mechanical stops
- Longer customer-service durations

#### Cohort and segmentation analysis

Analyze performance by:

- Route and territory
- Day of week and time of day
- Vehicle type and age
- Driver or driver tenure
- Customer segment
- Building type or delivery location
- Weather or traffic condition
- Number of packages and route density

#### Bottleneck impact quantification

For each bottleneck, calculate:

- Total lost minutes
- Lost deliveries
- Additional kilometres
- Additional fuel
- Overtime cost
- Missed time windows
- Re-delivery workload

For example, if traffic delays account for 4,000 minutes per month and service-time outliers account for 2,500 minutes, the company can prioritize interventions based on measurable opportunity rather than intuition.

## 3. Root Cause Analysis for Inefficiencies

### 3.1 Suboptimal route planning

A route may be inefficient when it contains:

- Excessive backtracking
- Low stop density
- Crossings between territories
- Long distance between consecutive stops
- Frequent sequence changes
- High excess distance relative to the planned route

Use conformance analysis to compare actual and planned sequences and trace clustering to identify route variants. Compare high-performing and low-performing routes after controlling for package count, area, and time windows.

A useful measure is:

\[
\text{Route Excess Distance} =
\text{Actual Distance} - \text{Planned Distance}
\]

High excess distance may indicate poor planning, dispatch changes, driver unfamiliarity, or unavoidable customer changes.

### 3.2 Inaccurate travel-time estimates

Compare planned travel times with actual travel times by:

- Road segment
- Time of day
- Day of week
- Weather
- Area
- Vehicle type

If planned times are systematically optimistic during morning and afternoon peaks, the route model is miscalibrated. Process mining can identify where the resulting lateness first appears and whether it propagates through the rest of the route.

### 3.3 Traffic congestion

Correlate GPS-derived travel delays with:

- Time of day
- Traffic feeds
- Road segments
- Low-speed events
- Stop density
- Customer time-window pressure

A process map can show whether congestion causes a single delay or creates a cascading effect, leading to missed time windows and overtime.

### 3.4 Variable service time

Separate service time into meaningful components where possible:

- Parking and walking
- Customer interaction
- Parcel retrieval
- Signature or identity verification
- Scanning
- Failed-delivery procedure

Analyze dwell-time distributions by customer, building type, parcel characteristics, driver, and time of day. A high median may suggest a systemic problem; a high 95th percentile may indicate specific difficult locations or exceptional cases.

Do not attribute all variation to drivers without controlling for location and delivery complexity.

### 3.5 Breakdowns and maintenance

Link unscheduled stops and maintenance records to:

- Vehicle age
- Mileage
- Engine warnings
- Prior repair history
- Route length
- Idle and high-load patterns
- Days since preventive maintenance

Calculate the operational consequences:

\[
\text{Breakdown Impact} =
\text{repair duration}
+ \text{lost travel time}
+ \text{missed deliveries}
+ \text{overtime}
+ \text{recovery or replacement cost}
\]

A vehicle that has only a few incidents but causes major route disruption may deserve priority over one with many minor issues.

### 3.6 Driver behavior and skill differences

Compare drivers using risk-adjusted measures such as:

- Stop service time
- Excess distance
- Idle time
- Harsh acceleration or braking, if available
- Failed-delivery rate
- On-time rate
- Route completion
- Fuel per kilometre

The analysis should control for route difficulty, customer mix, traffic, vehicle, and package volume. Variant analysis can identify effective practices used by consistently strong drivers, such as better stop sequencing or reduced depot preparation time.

### 3.7 Failed delivery attempts

Analyze failure patterns by:

- Customer and address
- Time window
- Arrival time
- Day of week
- Failure reason
- Driver
- Route
- Advance customer notification

A useful question is whether failures occur because the driver arrives outside the promised window. If so, the root cause may be planning or traffic rather than customer behaviour.

Estimate the downstream impact:

- Additional re-delivery kilometres
- Additional driver time
- Repeated handling
- Customer-service contacts
- Lost capacity on future routes

## 4. Data-Driven Optimization Strategies

### Strategy 1: Dynamic, traffic-aware route execution

**Targeted inefficiency:** Traffic-related delays, route backtracking, and cascading lateness.

**Root cause:** Static plans and inaccurate travel-time assumptions that do not reflect real-time congestion or actual service-time distributions.

**Process-mining support:**

- Identify road segments and time periods with recurring delays.
- Compare actual travel time with planned travel time.
- Find route variants where an early delay causes multiple subsequent missed windows.
- Determine thresholds at which route resequencing would have prevented lateness.

**Implementation:**

- Use historical process-mining data to recalibrate travel-time matrices by time of day.
- Feed real-time congestion into a routing engine.
- Permit controlled resequencing of future stops while respecting customer windows, vehicle capacity, and driver hours.
- Establish rules for when dispatch intervention is required.

**Expected KPI impact:**

- Higher on-time delivery rate
- Lower excess kilometres
- Lower traffic-delay minutes
- Reduced overtime
- Improved route completion
- Lower fuel per package

Dynamic routing should not simply minimize distance; it should minimize total operational cost while protecting time-window commitments.

### Strategy 2: Territory and stop-sequence redesign

**Targeted inefficiency:** Excess travel, low stop density, cross-territory travel, and inefficient route variants.

**Root cause:** Territory boundaries or route sequences that are based on geography alone rather than actual delivery times, traffic, parking constraints, and customer availability.

**Process-mining support:**

- Identify clusters of stops that are repeatedly served out of sequence.
- Compare planned and actual route distances.
- Find zones with high service-time or parking delays.
- Analyze package density and time-window compatibility.

**Implementation:**

- Redesign delivery territories using historical demand and service-time data.
- Group stops by realistic travel-time proximity rather than straight-line distance.
- Assign difficult buildings or high-service-time customers earlier or in dedicated route segments.
- Use route templates for recurring high-volume areas, with daily adjustment for demand and traffic.

**Expected KPI impact:**

- Lower travel time and fuel per package
- Higher packages per driver-hour
- Reduced route variability
- Fewer missed windows
- Better vehicle and capacity utilization

### Strategy 3: Reduce failed deliveries through time-window and customer communication improvements

**Targeted inefficiency:** Failed attempts, re-deliveries, and wasted kilometres.

**Root cause:** Customer absence, inaccurate availability windows, poor notification, or arrival outside the promised window.

**Process-mining support:**

- Identify customers, areas, and windows with high failure rates.
- Determine whether failure is preceded by late arrival.
- Measure the cost and delay associated with each failure reason.
- Compare results before and after notifications or alternative delivery options.

**Implementation:**

- Send estimated-arrival notifications with live updates.
- Allow customers to select lockers, safe places, neighbours, or narrower availability windows.
- Predict high-risk deliveries and schedule them when the customer is most likely to be present.
- Offer proactive rescheduling before a likely failed attempt.
- Separate operationally caused failures from customer-caused failures.

**Expected KPI impact:**

- Higher first-attempt success rate
- Lower failed-delivery and re-delivery rates
- Lower fuel per successful package
- Reduced route duration and customer complaints
- Better on-time performance

### Strategy 4: Predictive maintenance and vehicle allocation

**Targeted inefficiency:** Unscheduled stops, breakdowns, replacement vehicles, and maintenance-related route disruption.

**Root cause:** Maintenance scheduled only by fixed intervals or after faults occur, without using actual vehicle condition and operating patterns.

**Process-mining support:**

- Link warnings, GPS behaviour, repair events, mileage, and route load.
- Identify leading indicators of breakdowns.
- Measure which vehicles produce disproportionate delay and overtime.
- Compare preventive-maintenance downtime with unscheduled-failure downtime.

**Implementation:**

- Build a vehicle-risk score from mileage, fault history, warning events, idle time, and recent repairs.
- Schedule inspections before high-risk vehicles are assigned to long or dense routes.
- Allocate reliable vehicles to routes with strict time windows.
- Reserve backup capacity for high-risk periods.
- Incorporate maintenance availability into dispatch planning.

**Expected KPI impact:**

- Fewer unscheduled stops
- Lower breakdown minutes
- Improved route completion
- Lower maintenance and recovery costs
- Improved punctuality and vehicle availability

### Strategy 5: Targeted driver and operational training

**Targeted inefficiency:** Excessive service time, idle time, poor scanning discipline, fuel waste, or inconsistent route execution.

**Root cause:** Variation in procedures, unfamiliarity with locations, inefficient parking or parcel retrieval practices, and inconsistent use of handheld devices.

**Process-mining support:**

- Identify process variants associated with strong performance.
- Compare drivers on risk-adjusted service time, excess distance, failed deliveries, and fuel use.
- Detect repeated missing scans or late scan patterns.
- Identify locations where experienced drivers consistently perform better.

**Implementation:**

- Provide targeted coaching rather than generic training.
- Standardize depot loading, customer approach, scanning, and failed-delivery procedures.
- Share effective practices from high-performing drivers.
- Use driver feedback to distinguish system constraints from individual behaviour.

**Expected KPI impact:**

- Lower average and 95th-percentile service time
- Lower idle and fuel consumption
- Better scan completeness
- Fewer failed deliveries
- Reduced performance variability

## 5. Operational Constraints and Continuous Monitoring

### 5.1 Incorporating operational constraints

Optimization must remain feasible, not merely mathematically efficient.

#### Driver working hours

Route planning should enforce:

- Maximum shift duration
- Legally required breaks
- Overtime policies
- Return-to-depot requirements
- Driver qualification and vehicle assignment rules

A route that improves distance but regularly creates overtime is not a successful solution.

#### Vehicle capacity

The routing model should account for:

- Parcel count
- Parcel volume and weight
- Loading order
- Vehicle type
- Return parcels and failed-delivery items
- Capacity reserved for re-deliveries or pickups

Capacity utilization should be measured alongside service performance. Overloading may create safety and compliance problems, while very low utilization may indicate poor territory design.

#### Customer time windows

Every route change should preserve:

- Promised delivery windows
- Priority deliveries
- Restricted-access locations
- Business opening hours
- Special handling requirements

Time-window feasibility should be tested using realistic, percentile-based travel and service times rather than optimistic averages.

#### Maintenance and vehicle availability

The optimizer should exclude vehicles unavailable for maintenance and consider vehicle reliability when assigning routes.

#### Data-quality and human constraints

Changes should account for:

- Driver familiarity with areas
- Dispatch workload
- Customer communication lead time
- GPS and scanner reliability
- Explainability of route changes

### 5.2 Continuous process-mining monitoring

After implementation, Speedy Parcels should operate a process-mining control system with daily and weekly dashboards.

#### Executive dashboard

Track:

- On-time delivery rate
- First-attempt success rate
- Route completion rate
- Cost per package
- Fuel per kilometre and package
- Overtime
- Excess kilometres
- Breakdown minutes
- Customer complaints

Show trends against baseline and target values, segmented by depot and service type.

#### Operational control dashboard

Monitor current or previous-day exceptions:

- Routes with late departure
- Routes projected to miss time windows
- Vehicles with repeated warnings
- Excessive idle or low-speed time
- Unscheduled stops
- Unvisited or skipped stops
- High-risk failed deliveries
- Routes approaching driver-hour limits

#### Conformance dashboard

Track:

- Planned-versus-actual sequence compliance
- Unplanned stops
- Reordered and skipped stops
- Actual-versus-planned arrival times
- Actual-versus-planned distance
- Vehicle and driver substitutions
- Percentage of routes requiring manual intervention

#### Bottleneck dashboard

Display:

- Travel-time distributions
- Service-time percentiles
- Parking or access delays
- Depot loading and departure delays
- Traffic hotspots
- Maintenance downtime
- Failed-delivery concentrations

#### Vehicle and maintenance dashboard

Monitor:

- Faults by vehicle
- Failure frequency
- Preventive versus corrective maintenance
- Maintenance cost per kilometre
- Vehicle availability
- Breakdown risk scores
- Post-maintenance performance

### 5.3 Measurement and continuous improvement cycle

A robust improvement cycle should be:

1. Establish a six-month baseline.
2. Pilot changes on selected depots, routes, or vehicle groups.
3. Compare pilot and control groups where possible.
4. Measure both intended and unintended effects.
5. Recalibrate models as traffic, demand, and customer behaviour change.
6. Investigate new process variants and deviations.
7. Standardize successful practices and retire ineffective ones.

Monitoring should include statistical alerts, not only averages. For example:

- On-time rate falls below a threshold
- 95th-percentile service time increases
- Excess distance rises for a route family
- Breakdown risk increases for a vehicle
- Failed deliveries concentrate in a new time window

The objective is to create a closed loop in which actual operational events continuously improve route planning, maintenance, driver support, and customer communication. This allows Speedy Parcels to improve punctuality and reduce fuel, maintenance, and overtime costs while preserving capacity, legal, and customer-service constraints.
