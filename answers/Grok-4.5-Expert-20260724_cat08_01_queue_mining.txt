**1. Queue Identification and Characterization**

In process mining applied to healthcare event logs with explicit START and COMPLETE timestamps, waiting (queue) time between two consecutive activities is defined as the elapsed time from the COMPLETE timestamp of activity \(A_i\) to the START timestamp of the immediately succeeding activity \(A_{i+1}\) for the same Case ID (patient visit). This interval captures pure waiting/queueing time and excludes the service/processing time of either activity. Idle time within an activity itself is not counted as queue time; only the handover gap is.

From the six-month event log one would:

- Sort events by Case ID and timestamp.
- Reconstruct the process path for each visit (handling multi-resource activities and possible concurrent paths if the log supports them).
- Compute the inter-activity waiting time for every consecutive pair that actually occurs in the data.
- Filter out negative or zero waits (data-quality artifacts) and extreme outliers only after investigation (e.g., overnight stays or cancelled activities).

Key descriptive metrics calculated for each distinct queue (i.e., each unique activity pair, optionally stratified by resource, patient type, urgency, specialty, day-of-week, or time-of-day):

- Average (mean) waiting time
- Median waiting time
- 90th- and 95th-percentile waiting times
- Maximum waiting time
- Standard deviation / coefficient of variation (to quantify variability)
- Queue frequency: number and percentage of cases that experience that particular transition
- Number and percentage of cases whose wait exceeds a clinically or operationally defined threshold (e.g., >15 min, >30 min)
- Total patient-hours spent waiting in that queue across the entire log

The most critical queues are ranked by a composite impact score that multiplies (or otherwise combines) average wait, frequency, and downstream effect. Priority is given to queues that simultaneously exhibit:

- High average or high-percentile wait (patient experience impact)
- High frequency (affects many patients)
- Occurrence early in the pathway or before high-value activities (propagation effect)
- Disproportionate impact on specific cohorts (new patients, urgent cases, or particular specialties)

This multi-criteria ranking prevents over-focusing on rare extreme outliers while still highlighting systemic bottlenecks.

**2. Root Cause Analysis**

Once critical queues are identified, process-mining techniques beyond simple waiting-time calculation are applied to the same event log:

- **Resource analysis / utilization heat-maps**: Calculate busy time versus available time for each staff member, room, and equipment item. High utilization (>85–90 %) of a scarce resource immediately preceding a long queue is strong evidence of a capacity bottleneck.
- **Bottleneck analysis (performance spectrum or dotted-chart views)**: Visualize the distribution of service times and waiting times by resource and by time-of-day; clusters of long waits concentrated on particular resources or time windows reveal capacity or scheduling problems.
- **Variant analysis**: Discover the most frequent process variants and compare waiting-time distributions across them. Variants that insert extra diagnostic steps or require specialist hand-overs often generate longer queues.
- **Handover and social-network analysis**: Measure the frequency and duration of transitions between specific pairs of resources; frequent hand-overs that consistently produce long waits point to coordination or physical-layout issues.
- **Service-time variability analysis**: Fit distributions to activity durations stratified by patient type and urgency. High variance in doctor consultation or diagnostic test times can create unpredictable queues even when average capacity appears adequate.
- **Arrival-pattern and scheduling analysis**: Reconstruct inter-arrival times and compare them with scheduled appointment slots. Batch arrivals, overbooking, or systematic early/late arrivals relative to scheduled times surface as root causes.
- **Context filters**: Slice the log by Patient Type (New vs Follow-up), Urgency, specialty, day of week, and hour of day. Systematic differences (e.g., new patients wait significantly longer for nurse assessment) isolate cohort-specific causes.

Taken together, these analyses typically surface a small set of dominant root causes such as under-staffed registration desks at peak morning hours, high variability in cardiology consultation length, sequential rather than parallel ordering of certain diagnostics, or appointment templates that do not match actual service-time distributions.

**3. Data-Driven Optimization Strategies**

Three concrete strategies, each tied to evidence that would be extracted from the event log:

**Strategy A – Dynamic, demand-driven staffing of registration and nurse-assessment stations**  
- Targets the Registration → Nurse Assessment and Nurse Assessment → Doctor Consultation queues (commonly the highest-frequency, high-percentile waits).  
- Root cause addressed: capacity mismatch at peak arrival periods.  
- Data support: utilization heat-maps and hourly queue-length profiles show registration and nurse resources operating near or above 90 % utilization between 08:30–11:00 while later hours are under-utilized.  
- Expected impact: reduction of average Registration → Nurse wait by 40–60 % and of overall visit duration by 8–12 minutes for morning patients, achieved by shifting one or two staff members from low-utilization afternoon slots without net head-count increase.

**Strategy B – Specialty-specific appointment templates that incorporate observed service-time distributions and controlled overbooking**  
- Targets the Doctor Consultation queues and subsequent diagnostic queues.  
- Root cause addressed: mismatch between scheduled slot length and actual consultation duration plus uncontrolled batching of arrivals.  
- Data support: service-time histograms and variant analysis reveal that cardiology consultations have a long right tail; current fixed 15-minute slots systematically under-estimate duration for new patients.  
- Expected impact: 25–35 % reduction in average post-consultation diagnostic wait and a measurable drop in the 90th-percentile total visit duration, while keeping physician utilization stable.

**Strategy C – Parallelization of selected diagnostic activities and introduction of a “results-ready” notification mechanism**  
- Targets the queues that form after ECG, blood tests, or X-ray while patients wait for results before the next clinical step.  
- Root cause addressed: strictly sequential process design and lack of real-time coordination.  
- Data support: process-variant and concurrency analysis show that a substantial fraction of visits contain independent diagnostic activities that are currently executed serially; dotted charts reveal long idle periods while results are awaited.  
- Expected impact: 15–25 % reduction in total visit duration for the multi-diagnostic cohort and elimination of one intermediate waiting stage for approximately 30 % of patients, with negligible additional resource cost if existing equipment capacity is rebalanced.

**4. Consideration of Trade-offs and Constraints**

Each strategy carries potential side-effects. Dynamic staffing may increase perceived unfairness among staff if the reallocation is not transparent, and may temporarily raise overtime costs during the transition period. Tighter appointment templates can improve flow but risk higher no-show or late-arrival penalties if patients feel rushed; they may also compress the thoroughness of history-taking if physicians feel time pressure. Parallel diagnostics improve throughput but can increase short-term equipment contention and require reliable real-time messaging; any failure in the notification system re-introduces waiting.  

Balancing objectives is achieved by defining an explicit multi-objective utility function before implementation: e.g., maximize the reduction in patient-hours waited subject to (a) no increase in total staff FTE, (b) no statistically significant rise in clinical adverse-event or rework rates, and (c) physician utilization remaining inside a pre-agreed band. Pilot deployments on a single specialty or half-day block, followed by rapid A/B comparison against the historical baseline, allow early detection of unintended bottleneck shifts and permit rollback if quality or cost constraints are breached.

**5. Measuring Success**

Primary KPIs tracked continuously from the live event log (identical schema) are:

- Average and 90th-percentile total visit duration
- Average and 90th-percentile waiting time for each previously critical queue
- Percentage of visits whose total wait exceeds defined thresholds
- Resource utilization rates (to confirm capacity is not merely shifted)
- Patient-reported experience scores linked to visit IDs (where available)
- Secondary safety/quality proxies: rate of incomplete assessments, unplanned return visits within 7 days, and diagnostic rework rates

A rolling four-week control chart (or process-mining dashboard with statistical process-control limits) is maintained for each KPI. Sustained improvement is declared only when both the mean and the upper control limit of the critical waiting-time metrics remain below the pre-intervention baseline for at least eight consecutive weeks, while the secondary quality metrics show no deterioration. Monthly re-mining of the full log also detects the emergence of new queues or process variants, enabling continuous, data-driven refinement rather than one-time optimization.
