**1. Analyzing Historical Scheduling Performance and Dynamics**

Process mining begins by treating the MES event log as an event log in the classical sense: each row is an event with a case identifier (Job ID / Case ID), activity label (e.g., “Setup Start (Cutting)”, “Task End (Milling)”, “Queue Entry”, “Breakdown Start”), timestamp, and rich attributes (resource/machine, operator, priority, due date, planned vs. actual duration, previous job for setups, notes).

**Process discovery and reconstruction.**  
Apply inductive miner, heuristic miner, or fuzzy miner (filtered by frequency) to discover the multi-instance process map of actual job routings. Because routings are highly variable, the discovery yields a family of process variants rather than a single rigid model. Resource-centric discovery (or directly-follows graphs projected onto machines) reconstructs the exact sequence of jobs processed on each machine, including idle periods and setups. Token-based replay or alignment-based conformance checking validates that the discovered model faithfully reproduces observed behavior.

**Key performance metrics extracted via process mining:**

- **Job flow times, lead times, and makespan distributions.**  
  For each case, compute case duration (release to final completion), flow time (first activity start to last activity end), and makespan relative to the shop calendar. Produce histograms, box plots, and cumulative distribution functions; stratify by priority class, product family, or month. Cycle-time analysis and dotted charts reveal seasonal or load-dependent shifts.

- **Task waiting (queue) times.**  
  Identify “Queue Entry → Setup Start / Task Start” pairs per machine. Compute waiting-time distributions per work center, average queue length over time, and the fraction of total lead time spent waiting. Performance overlays on the process map color-code activities by mean/median waiting time.

- **Resource utilization.**  
  For each machine (and optionally operator), partition calendar time into: productive processing, sequence-dependent setup, idle (starvation or no queued work), planned downtime, and unplanned breakdown. Utilization = (processing + setup) / available time. Heat maps or resource timelines visualize utilization profiles and contention windows.

- **Sequence-dependent setup times.**  
  From consecutive “Task End” / “Setup Start–End” events on the same machine, extract the ordered pair (previous job attributes → current job attributes). Attributes can be product family, material, tool requirements, or geometric features (if available in the log or master data). Cluster or regress setup duration against the distance between successive jobs (e.g., changeover matrix). The resulting empirical changeover matrix or predictive model quantifies how much extra time each sequence incurs.

- **Schedule adherence and tardiness.**  
  Compare actual completion timestamp of the final activity of each case against its Order Due Date. Compute tardiness (max(0, completion − due)), earliness, percentage of tardy jobs, mean tardiness, and weighted tardiness (weight by priority or penalty). Service-level curves and lateness distributions are stratified by priority and by whether a disruption occurred during the case.

- **Impact of disruptions.**  
  Isolate breakdown intervals and priority-change events. Measure the immediate queue growth on the affected machine, the propagation of delays to downstream jobs (via case-level delay attribution), and the recovery time until the machine returns to a stable utilization regime. Comparative process mining (before/after disruption windows) quantifies the temporary inflation of flow times and setup frequency caused by re-sequencing.

All metrics are obtained from standard process-mining platforms (e.g., via PM4Py, Celonis, or Disco) augmented with custom Python/R scripts for sequence-dependent and multi-attribute analyses.

**2. Diagnosing Scheduling Pathologies**

Process-mining diagnostics surface several recurring pathologies:

- **Bottleneck resources.**  
  Machines with the highest average queue time, longest busy periods, and highest fraction of cases that wait appear as dark nodes on the performance-annotated process map. Bottleneck analysis (e.g., using the “bottleneck score” that multiplies utilization by average queue length) quantifies their contribution to total flow time. Resource contention graphs show simultaneous high queues on the bottleneck and starvation on downstream flexible machines.

- **Poor prioritization.**  
  Variant analysis comparing on-time versus tardy cases reveals that high-priority or near-due-date jobs frequently experience longer waiting times at critical machines than low-priority jobs—evidence that local FCFS/EDD rules are overridden by opportunistic sequencing or by the arrival of even hotter jobs. Dotted charts colored by priority show high-priority cases interspersed among long-running low-priority batches.

- **Suboptimal sequencing and excess setup.**  
  The extracted changeover matrix, when compared with a lower-bound matrix (minimum observed setup for each pair), shows that realized sequences incur 20–40 % more setup time than necessary. Process variants that group similar jobs exhibit markedly lower cumulative setup time and higher throughput.

- **Starvation of downstream resources.**  
  Timeline analysis reveals intervals in which a flexible machine sits idle while upstream bottleneck queues grow—classic “push” behavior induced by local dispatching that ignores downstream load.

- **WIP bullwhip / variability amplification.**  
  Time-series of aggregate WIP (number of active cases) and machine-level queue lengths display large oscillations even under relatively stable order arrival rates. Correlation analysis links these oscillations to periods of high setup intensity or recovery after breakdowns.

Evidence is obtained by (a) performance overlay on the discovered model, (b) comparative process mining of on-time vs. late cohorts, (c) resource-centric performance analysis, and (d) statistical tests on waiting-time and setup-time distributions conditioned on priority and preceding job type.

**3. Root Cause Analysis of Scheduling Ineffectiveness**

The observed pathologies stem from a combination of algorithmic, informational, and organizational limitations:

- Static local dispatching rules (FCFS mixed with simple EDD) cannot simultaneously optimize sequence-dependent setups, remaining processing time, due-date urgency, and downstream congestion. They are myopic by design.
- Absence of a real-time global view of queue lengths, machine states, and predicted remaining times prevents any work center from making load-aware decisions.
- Planned durations stored in the MES are systematically biased (actuals exceed plans, especially for complex jobs or certain operators). Setup times are treated as constant or ignored, so sequencing decisions never trade off setup cost against due-date risk.
- No formal mechanism exists for coordinating release or sequencing across work centers; each machine optimizes locally.
- Disruptions are handled reactively: a breakdown simply freezes the queue, and a hot job is inserted by manual override, destroying any residual sequence quality.

Process mining differentiates causes by residual analysis. After fitting a pure capacity model (queueing network parameterized by mined service-time distributions and routing probabilities), residual tardiness and excess setup time that cannot be explained by capacity constraints are attributed to sequencing logic. Conversely, when even an idealized optimal sequencer (computed offline on historical traces) still produces high tardiness, the root cause is structural capacity shortage or excessive process variability. Conformance checking against an idealized “good-scheduling” model further isolates logic failures from resource failures.

**4. Developing Advanced Data-Driven Scheduling Strategies**

**Strategy 1 – Multi-attribute dynamic dispatching with learned setup cost.**  
At each machine, when a resource becomes free, rank all waiting jobs by a composite priority index:

\[
I_j = w_1\cdot\frac{1}{\text{slack}_j} + w_2\cdot\text{priority}_j + w_3\cdot\frac{1}{\text{remaining work}_j} + w_4\cdot\frac{1}{\widehat{\text{setup}}(prev,j)} - w_5\cdot\text{downstream load after }j
\]

where \(\widehat{\text{setup}}\) is the predicted sequence-dependent setup obtained from the mined changeover model (regression or nearest-neighbor on historical pairs), and downstream load is estimated from current queue lengths plus predicted processing times on successor machines. Weights \(w_i\) are tuned offline by simulation on mined distributions or by online reinforcement learning.  

This directly attacks excess setup, poor prioritization, and starvation. Expected impact: 25–40 % reduction in mean tardiness, 15–25 % reduction in total setup time, moderate WIP reduction through better flow.

**Strategy 2 – Predictive, rolling-horizon scheduling.**  
Fit empirical distributions (or conditional models) of processing times, setup times, and inter-breakdown times from the event log, stratified by machine, operator, job family, and recent load. Use these distributions inside a discrete-event simulator or a stochastic scheduling heuristic (e.g., sample-average approximation of a chance-constrained model) that re-optimizes the sequence of the next \(k\) jobs on each bottleneck every few hours or after every major disruption. Predictive maintenance signals (derived from frequency and duration of past breakdowns correlated with operating hours) further inflate capacity buffers.  

Addresses inaccurate duration estimates and disruption recovery. Expected impact: more reliable lead-time quotations, lower variance of flow times, reduced frequency of extreme tardiness events.

**Strategy 3 – Setup-minimizing sequencing and selective batching at bottlenecks.**  
On identified bottleneck machines, solve a dynamic traveling-salesman-like sequencing problem whose distance matrix is the mined setup-time matrix, subject to due-date windows and priority constraints. Non-bottleneck machines continue with the multi-attribute rule of Strategy 1. When several similar jobs are present, a limited batching window (informed by the frequency of similar successive jobs in the historical log) is allowed, provided the batch does not violate any high-priority due date.  

Targets the excess-setup pathology and bottleneck congestion. Expected impact: substantial reduction in cumulative setup time on critical resources, higher bottleneck throughput, secondary reduction in WIP and lead-time variability.

**5. Simulation, Evaluation, and Continuous Improvement**

Construct a discrete-event simulation model whose stochastic elements are parameterized directly from the process-mining results: routing probabilities and variant frequencies, processing-time and setup-time distributions (including covariates), breakdown inter-arrival and repair distributions, order-arrival processes, and priority-change intensities. The baseline policy is the current mix of FCFS/EDD; each of the three proposed strategies is implemented as an alternative decision logic inside the same model.

Test scenarios include:
- nominal load,
- high-load (arrival rate increased 20–30 %),
- high-disruption (breakdown frequency doubled),
- surge of hot jobs,
- and combinations thereof.

Primary KPIs: mean and 95th-percentile tardiness, percentage of tardy jobs, average and maximum WIP, mean flow time, total setup time, and machine utilization (especially bottlenecks). Statistical comparison (common random numbers, paired t-tests) ranks the strategies. Sensitivity analysis on weight vectors and prediction accuracy quantifies robustness.

For continuous improvement, stream the live MES log into a process-mining cockpit that recomputes the same KPI dashboards and changeover matrices on a rolling window (daily/weekly). Automated drift detection (e.g., Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests on waiting-time distributions, or concept-drift detectors on the process map) triggers re-tuning of dispatching weights or re-estimation of the setup model. Periodic offline re-simulation with the latest distributions keeps the chosen strategy aligned with evolving shop-floor reality. A closed-loop architecture—mine → diagnose → re-optimize parameters → simulate → deploy—ensures sustained performance gains.
