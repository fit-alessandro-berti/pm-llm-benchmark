# 1. Analyzing Historical Scheduling Performance and Dynamics

## 1.1 Build an analysis-ready, object-centric event log

The raw MES log should first be transformed into a consistent event model. A conventional case-centric log using only `Job ID` is insufficient because several objects interact:

- Jobs and individual operation instances
- Machines and machine groups
- Operators
- Setups and tooling configurations
- Breakdown and maintenance episodes
- Schedule versions
- Priority and due-date changes

An **object-centric event log (OCEL)** is therefore appropriate. A task-start event can be related simultaneously to a job, operation, machine, and operator, while a breakdown is related to a machine rather than being artificially assigned to a job.

Each operation should have a unique identifier such as:

\[
\text{Operation ID}=(\text{Job ID},\text{routing step},\text{rework iteration})
\]

The following lifecycle states should be reconstructed:

1. Operation enabled by completion of predecessors
2. Queue entry
3. Machine assignment, if separate
4. Setup start and end
5. Processing start, suspend, resume, and end
6. Inspection or rejection
7. Rework or route deviation
8. Job completion

Data preparation must also address:

- Duplicate and out-of-order events
- Missing starts or ends
- Clock synchronization
- Shift and holiday calendars
- Simultaneous events
- Rework loops
- Left- and right-censored jobs
- Planned versus unplanned maintenance
- Time-varying due dates and priorities
- Whether planned duration includes or excludes setup
- Resource eligibility and alternate-machine information from routing master data

Schedule adherence analysis requires historical snapshots of planned starts, finishes, and machine assignments. If the MES has not retained schedule versions, due-date adherence can still be measured, but detailed planned-versus-actual schedule adherence cannot be reconstructed reliably.

## 1.2 Reconstruct job flow and machine execution sequences

Two complementary process views are needed.

### Job-centric view

For each job, events are ordered by timestamp to reconstruct:

- Actual routing
- Inter-operation waiting
- Rework and repeated tasks
- Parallel or overlapping operations
- Skipped or out-of-sequence operations
- Final completion

Useful process-mining methods include:

- **Directly-Follows Graphs**, with frequency and performance overlays
- **Inductive Miner or Petri-net discovery** for control-flow structure
- **Variant analysis** by product family, priority, or routing family
- **Alignment-based conformance checking** against master routings
- **Token replay** to identify skipped, repeated, and out-of-order tasks
- **Performance spectrum and dotted-chart analysis** for temporal congestion patterns

Because this is a high-mix shop, a single process map may become a “spaghetti model.” Jobs should therefore be grouped by meaningful attributes such as part family, material, routing family, complexity, or major bottleneck visited.

### Resource-centric view

For each machine, operations are ordered by actual setup or processing start time. This reconstructs:

- The exact job sequence processed on the machine
- Idle gaps
- Setup transitions
- Breakdown interruptions
- Queue conditions when each dispatch decision was made
- Overlaps or resource-allocation anomalies

The machine trace is essential for analyzing sequence-dependent setups and determining whether the chosen job was sensible relative to the jobs that were ready and eligible at that time.

---

## 1.3 Flow-time, lead-time, and makespan metrics

For job \(j\), define:

- \(a_j\): order acceptance time, if available
- \(r_j\): shop-floor release time
- \(C_j\): completion time
- \(d_j\): committed due date

Then:

\[
\text{Shop-floor flow time}_j=C_j-r_j
\]

\[
\text{Customer lead time}_j=C_j-a_j
\]

These should not be conflated. A workload-control policy may reduce shop-floor WIP by delaying release, but that does not necessarily reduce total customer lead time.

Flow time should be decomposed into:

\[
\text{Flow time}
=
\text{processing}
+\text{setup}
+\text{queue waiting}
+\text{transport}
+\text{blocking}
+\text{downtime interruption}
\]

Report distributions rather than averages alone:

- Median
- 75th, 90th, and 95th percentiles
- Coefficient of variation
- Distribution by product family, priority, and route
- Tail conditional averages for severely delayed jobs

Survival-analysis methods can include jobs that were still open at the end of the observation period.

In scheduling, makespan normally refers to a set of jobs:

\[
C_{\max}=\max_{j \in B} C_j-t_0
\]

where \(B\) is a batch or analysis cohort and \(t_0\) is the common schedule origin. Makespan distributions can be computed across weekly cohorts, production campaigns, historical replay windows, or simulation replications. A single job’s release-to-completion span is better termed flow time.

---

## 1.4 Task waiting and queue-time analysis

For operation \(o\) of job \(j\):

\[
Q_{jo} =
\begin{cases}
\text{SetupStart}_{jo}-\text{QueueEntry}_{jo}, & \text{if setup occurs}\\
\text{TaskStart}_{jo}-\text{QueueEntry}_{jo}, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\]

Additional distinctions are useful:

- Waiting for machine
- Waiting for operator
- Waiting for material or tool
- Waiting for predecessor completion
- Waiting after processing because the next buffer is full
- Waiting caused by a breakdown
- Waiting caused by a deliberate batching policy

For each work center and machine, calculate:

- Mean, median, and percentile queue time
- Maximum queue age
- Number of jobs in queue over time
- Workload in hours rather than only job count
- Queue-time contribution to total job lead time
- Queue time by shift, day, priority, product family, and setup family
- Probability of waiting longer than a threshold

Time-varying queue lengths should be reconstructed from queue-entry and service-start events. This enables visualizations of queue buildup around breakdowns, batch releases, and hot-job insertion.

---

## 1.5 Resource utilization and state decomposition

For machine \(m\), let \(A_m\) be scheduled available time, excluding holidays and planned closures. Decompose it into:

- \(P_m\): productive processing
- \(S_m\): setup
- \(D_m\): unplanned downtime
- \(B_m\): blocked time
- \(I_m\): idle time

Then:

\[
U^{productive}_m = \frac{P_m}{A_m},
\qquad
U^{setup}_m = \frac{S_m}{A_m},
\qquad
U^{busy}_m = \frac{P_m+S_m}{A_m}
\]

A state balance should approximately satisfy:

\[
P_m+S_m+D_m+B_m+I_m=A_m
\]

Idle time should be classified further:

- **Starved:** no eligible operation was ready
- **Dispatch idle:** work was ready but no operation was started
- **Operator-constrained:** machine ready, operator unavailable
- **Material/tool constrained**
- **Intentional idle:** waiting for a higher-value batch or maintenance window

The same interval-union logic should be applied to operators to avoid double-counting overlapping records. Operator analysis can identify excessive multitasking, skill bottlenecks, and machines that are nominally available but unusable because a qualified operator is missing.

High utilization alone does not prove that a machine is a bottleneck. A machine with nearly 100% productive-plus-setup utilization, a persistent queue, and a strong effect on downstream throughput is a much stronger bottleneck candidate than a machine that is busy processing low-priority work while critical jobs wait elsewhere.

---

## 1.6 Sequence-dependent setup analysis

For each machine, sort operations by the actual setup or processing sequence. For a transition from predecessor job \(h\) to successor job \(j\), calculate:

\[
s_{h,j,m}=\text{SetupEnd}_{j,m}-\text{SetupStart}_{j,m}
\]

The transition should be described using predecessor and successor attributes such as:

- Material
- Part family
- Tooling family
- Fixture
- Diameter or geometry range
- CNC program family
- Heat-treatment recipe
- Tolerance class
- Cleaning or contamination category

A machine-specific setup matrix can then be estimated:

\[
\hat S_m(f_h,f_j)
\]

where \(f_h\) and \(f_j\) are predecessor and successor setup families.

Because many transitions will be sparse, simple averages are unreliable. Use:

- Median and upper-quantile estimates
- Confidence intervals and support counts
- Hierarchical or partial-pooling models
- Quantile regression or gradient-boosted models
- Machine, operator, shift, and job-complexity effects
- Separate models for internal and external setup where applicable

The analysis should compare:

1. Actual total setup time
2. Expected setup time for the actual sequence
3. Expected setup time for the best feasible sequence among jobs that were ready
4. A global or rolling-horizon optimized sequence

This quantifies the portion of setup time that was potentially avoidable through better scheduling rather than improved physical setup methods.

---

## 1.7 Schedule adherence and tardiness

For each job:

\[
L_j=C_j-d_j
\]

where \(L_j\) is signed lateness, and:

\[
T_j=\max(0,C_j-d_j)
\]

is tardiness.

Key measures are:

- On-time delivery rate
- Mean and median tardiness
- 90th and 95th percentile tardiness
- Maximum tardiness
- Weighted tardiness:

\[
\sum_j w_jT_j
\]

- Percentage of high-priority jobs late
- Earliness, because excessive earliness can also create inventory
- Due-date performance by customer, family, and route

Original and revised due dates should be retained separately. Otherwise repeated due-date extensions may make delivery performance appear better than it really is.

If historical schedule snapshots are available, measure:

\[
\Delta^{start}_{jo}=\text{ActualStart}_{jo}-\text{FrozenPlannedStart}_{jo}
\]

\[
\Delta^{finish}_{jo}=\text{ActualFinish}_{jo}-\text{FrozenPlannedFinish}_{jo}
\]

Also measure:

- Resource-assignment changes
- Sequence changes
- Number of schedule revisions
- Frozen-zone violations
- Schedule “nervousness,” such as the number and magnitude of changes per shift

Comparing actuals against both the last frozen schedule and the latest revised schedule distinguishes poor initial planning from poor execution.

---

## 1.8 Impact of breakdowns and priority changes

For breakdowns, calculate:

- Failure frequency
- Mean time between failures
- Mean and distribution of repair time
- Direct lost capacity
- Jobs interrupted
- Jobs queued at the failed resource
- Downstream machines starved
- Time required for queues and throughput to return to normal
- Tardiness attributable to the disruption window

An event-study approach can compare queue length, WIP, throughput, and tardiness before and after each breakdown. Similar non-disrupted machines or matched time windows can serve as controls.

For hot jobs and priority changes, reconstruct:

- Queue position before and after the change
- Whether the job displaced another operation
- Setup introduced by inserting the hot job
- Delay imposed on other jobs
- Net weighted-tardiness effect
- Recovery time until the prior schedule was restored

Regression, matched-cohort analysis, and difference-in-differences can estimate disruption effects while controlling for load, shift, product mix, and machine. However, causal claims should be validated through simulation because disruptions are rarely random.

# 2. Diagnosing Scheduling Pathologies

The full data may confirm some pathologies and reject others; conclusions should not be inferred from the small sample alone. The following analyses provide defensible evidence.

## 2.1 Dynamic bottlenecks

A bottleneck should be identified from several signals:

- Persistent high workload-to-capacity ratio
- High productive-plus-setup utilization
- Long and aging queues
- A high share of job flow time spent waiting there
- Downstream starvation following its output interruptions
- Strong sensitivity of total throughput to its availability
- Frequent appearance on the critical path of late jobs

A useful load measure is:

\[
\rho_{m,t} =
\frac{\text{expected processing and setup workload due in window }t}
{\text{effective available capacity in window }t}
\]

Values persistently near or above one indicate structural overload. Time-varying analysis is important because bottlenecks may shift by product mix or shift.

Process-mining performance overlays can show whether late variants consistently pass through a particular machine and whether their delays arise before, at, or after it.

## 2.2 Poor prioritization

At every task start, reconstruct the set of jobs that were:

- In the queue
- Precedence-feasible
- Material-ready
- Eligible for the machine and operator

Then compare the selected job with the alternatives. Diagnostics include:

- Rank of selected job by due-date slack
- Rank by priority
- Number of near-due jobs bypassed
- Frequency with which low-priority work was chosen ahead of urgent work
- Local “dispatch regret”: expected KPI difference between the actual choice and a better feasible choice
- Whether high-priority jobs receive attention only after they are already late

Decision mining can infer which attributes actually drove historical choices. For example, a decision tree may reveal that nominal EDD behavior is frequently overridden by queue age or operator preference.

Variant analysis should compare matched on-time and late jobs with similar routing and workload. If late jobs experience more bypassing or lower queue priority, this supports a scheduling-logic diagnosis rather than merely a complexity explanation.

## 2.3 Excessive setup caused by sequencing

Evidence of poor setup sequencing includes:

- High family-switch frequency
- Repeated alternation between setup families
- Short campaigns separated by expensive changeovers
- High observed setup time despite several compatible jobs being ready
- Large gaps between actual and best-feasible transition costs
- Priority changes that trigger a setup and then require a reversal setup

A useful metric is:

\[
\text{Avoidable setup at decision }k
=
\hat s_{\text{actual transition}}
-
\min_{j\in\text{feasible ready set}}\hat s_{\text{transition to }j}
\]

This is only a local estimate because choosing the minimum setup every time can worsen due-date performance. A rolling-horizon comparison is needed for the final judgment.

## 2.4 Downstream starvation and poor coordination

A downstream machine is starved when it is available but no eligible job is ready. Cross-resource event analysis can then determine whether:

- The upstream resource was overloaded
- The upstream queue contained jobs for other downstream routes
- Jobs feeding the starved resource were repeatedly bypassed
- A breakdown interrupted the feeding stream
- Large upstream batches created irregular output pulses

Time-lagged correlations between upstream completions and downstream busy/idle states provide evidence of propagation. Resource-centric animation and WIP heat maps make such patterns visible.

## 2.5 WIP oscillation and bullwhip-like behavior

Reconstruct total and station-level WIP continuously:

\[
WIP(t)=\#\{j:r_j\leq t<C_j\}
\]

Also measure WIP in workload hours. Evidence of scheduling-driven amplification includes:

- Large periodic queue waves
- Batch releases followed by congestion and starvation
- Higher variability in station arrivals than in customer-order arrivals
- Oscillation between overloaded and idle downstream stations
- Strong correlation between hot-job insertion and later recovery congestion

A variability-amplification ratio can compare coefficients of variation of releases, station arrivals, and station departures. Little’s Law provides a consistency check:

\[
WIP \approx Throughput \times FlowTime
\]

## 2.6 Other pathologies to test

Process mining may also reveal:

- Excessive rework loops
- Unofficial routing shortcuts
- Machine specialization beyond what the master data indicates
- Operator-dependent duration or quality effects
- Jobs queued to one machine while eligible alternatives are idle
- Premature release of too many jobs
- Unnecessary schedule changes even when the original plan remains feasible
- Long post-processing blocking because downstream buffers are full

# 3. Root Cause Analysis of Scheduling Ineffectiveness

## 3.1 Limitations of static local rules

FCFS and EDD use only a small portion of the relevant state. They generally ignore:

- Remaining route and processing time
- Setup consequences
- Alternative machine choices
- Downstream congestion
- Bottleneck protection
- Breakdown risk
- Priority weights
- Queue aging and fairness
- Schedule stability

FCFS can protect fairness while allowing near-due jobs to become late. Pure EDD can create excessive setups and may favor a job with an early due date even when it has little remaining work and another job is at much greater risk.

Local dispatch rules can also cause each machine to optimize its own queue while degrading overall shop performance.

## 3.2 Lack of real-time state visibility

If the scheduler uses stale information, it may:

- Send jobs to failed or overloaded machines
- Fail to exploit available alternatives
- Release work into already congested routes
- Feed the wrong downstream machine
- Promise dates based on outdated queue estimates

This can be tested by comparing the state known to the scheduler at each decision point with the state reconstructable from the MES.

## 3.3 Inaccurate duration and setup estimates

For each task family, compare planned and actual durations:

\[
Error_{jo}=Actual_{jo}-Planned_{jo}
\]

Analyze bias, absolute error, and calibration by:

- Machine
- Operator
- Part family
- Complexity
- Shift
- Tool condition
- Lot size
- Rework status

Consistent underestimation creates schedules that are infeasible before execution begins. Even unbiased estimates can be inadequate if their variance is ignored. Setup estimates should be tested similarly, especially by predecessor-successor pair.

## 3.4 Ineffective setup handling

If setup is represented as a fixed average or ignored, the scheduler cannot distinguish a 5-minute compatible transition from a 90-minute family change. This leads to:

- Unnecessary family switching
- Overloaded setup personnel
- Understated machine load
- Inaccurate completion estimates
- Tardiness following seemingly small sequence changes

## 3.5 Poor cross-work-center coordination

A local rule may process jobs that are not useful to the current bottleneck while delaying jobs needed to keep it fed. Conversely, it may flood an already congested downstream station. Evidence is found by linking dispatch decisions to subsequent starvation, blocking, and queue buildup.

## 3.6 Weak disruption response

Common root causes include:

- No rolling rescheduling after breakdowns
- Rescheduling the whole plant after every event, causing nervousness
- No reserved capacity for urgent work
- Uncontrolled preemption
- Inserting hot jobs without measuring the setup and displacement cost
- Returning to the old schedule even when it is no longer feasible

## 3.7 Separating scheduling failure from capacity and variability

Process mining should be combined with capacity analysis and counterfactual optimization.

### Capacity test

Calculate the minimum plausible workload on each resource, including setups. If:

\[
\text{Required workload}>\text{available capacity}
\]

over sustained periods, no dispatch rule can eliminate tardiness. Due dates may also be inherently infeasible if the minimum route duration exceeds the available release-to-due-date window.

### Logic test

For historical jobs, compare:

1. Actual schedule
2. Better online policy using only information available at the time
3. Perfect-information optimized schedule using actual realized durations
4. The same scenarios with additional capacity

Interpretation:

- Large improvement from actual to better online policy indicates poor scheduling logic.
- Large improvement only with perfect foresight indicates uncertainty and variability.
- Little improvement until capacity is added indicates structural capacity shortage.
- Large improvement when breakdowns are removed indicates reliability as the dominant cause.

### Execution versus planning test

- If the frozen schedule was feasible and good but execution deviated because of downtime or material shortages, the problem is execution reliability.
- If the schedule itself understated durations or made poor sequences, the problem is planning.
- If the dispatcher repeatedly chose inferior jobs despite accurate state data, the problem is local scheduling logic.

Regression and predictive models can identify associations, but simulation or natural experiments are needed to establish stronger causality.

# 4. Advanced Data-Driven Scheduling Strategies

The strongest solution is likely a portfolio: global predictive planning, intelligent local execution, setup optimization at bottlenecks, and controlled job release.

## Strategy 1: Dynamic multi-criteria dispatching

### Core logic

At each machine-completion or disruption event, generate the set of feasible jobs and score them dynamically. For operation \(o\) of job \(j\) on machine \(m\):

\[
Slack_j(t)=d_j-t-\widehat{RPT}_j(t)
\]

where \(\widehat{RPT}\) includes predicted remaining processing, setup, and downstream delay.

A generalized score can include:

\[
Score_{j,m} =
\alpha Priority_j
+\beta Urgency_j
+\gamma QueueAge_j
+\delta BottleneckFeed_j
-\eta \widehat{Setup}_{prev,j,m}
-\theta DownstreamCongestion_j
-\kappa FailureRisk_m
\]

An ATCS-style implementation can combine urgency, processing time, and setup cost multiplicatively:

\[
I_{j,m}=
\frac{w_j}{\hat p_{j,m}}
\exp\left(
-\frac{\max(Slack_j,0)}{k_1\bar p}
\right)
\exp\left(
-\frac{\hat s_{prev,j,m}}{k_2\bar s}
\right)
F_{downstream}F_{aging}
\]

For flexible resources, assignment should minimize predicted completion cost across eligible machines, not simply select the first available machine.

### Use of process-mining insights

Process mining supplies:

- Remaining-duration estimates
- Machine-specific setup matrices
- Historically observed bottlenecks
- Queue-delay estimates
- Downstream congestion relationships
- Priority and due-date failure patterns
- Operator and machine compatibility
- Failure-risk estimates

Weights should be tuned through simulation or Bayesian optimization, not learned by imitating historical dispatch decisions, because those decisions may be poor.

### Pathologies addressed

- Poor local prioritization
- Starvation of critical machines
- Excessive setup switching
- Ignoring remaining job complexity
- Sending work into congested downstream queues
- Unfair starvation of old jobs

### Expected KPI impact

- Lower weighted tardiness and fewer severe late jobs
- Reduced queue-time tails
- Lower setup time than EDD alone
- Better bottleneck feeding
- Improved WIP balance

Guardrails should prevent indefinite postponement of low-priority jobs and prohibit unsafe preemption of non-preemptible tasks.

---

## Strategy 2: Predictive, robust rolling-horizon scheduling

### Core logic

Use predictive models to estimate duration distributions rather than fixed averages. Features may include:

- Part and routing family
- Material and geometry
- Machine and operator
- Shift
- CNC program or tooling
- Planned quantity
- Rework status
- Recent machine behavior
- Predecessor setup family

Models should provide quantiles or full distributions, for example:

\[
\hat p_{jo}^{50},\quad \hat p_{jo}^{80},\quad \hat p_{jo}^{95}
\]

Breakdown risk can be modeled using survival or hazard models based on machine age, cycles since maintenance, recent failures, and condition-monitoring data. If only breakdown event history is available, use empirical failure and repair distributions rather than claiming condition-based prediction.

Every fixed interval or major event, solve a finite-capacity rolling-horizon model using CP-SAT, MILP, or a hybrid metaheuristic. The model should include:

- Operation precedence
- Alternative machines
- Sequence-dependent setups
- Machine and operator calendars
- Operator skills
- Non-preemptibility
- Release times
- Due dates and priorities
- Maintenance windows
- Frozen-zone constraints
- Buffer or WIP limits

A representative objective is:

\[
\min
\left[
\sum_j w_jT_j
+\lambda_1\sum setup
+\lambda_2\sum flowtime
+\lambda_3\sum scheduleChanges
\right]
\]

Robustness can be introduced through scenario sampling, quantile durations, protected capacity, or chance constraints.

### Disruption handling

- Freeze tasks already in progress and near-term commitments.
- Reschedule only the affected neighborhood when possible.
- Generate ETA confidence intervals for each job.
- Insert hot jobs based on total weighted impact, not priority alone.
- Trigger preventive maintenance when expected failure cost exceeds the schedule cost of planned downtime.

### Use of process-mining insights

Process mining supplies calibrated duration distributions, setup models, route variants, breakdown behavior, and real queue-delay patterns. Predictive process monitoring estimates the probability that each active job will miss its due date.

### Pathologies addressed

- Unrealistic deterministic schedules
- Failure to account for duration variability
- Slow response to breakdowns
- Poor machine assignment
- Schedule instability
- Inaccurate customer completion estimates

### Expected KPI impact

- Higher on-time delivery
- More reliable completion-time promises
- Lower extreme tardiness
- Better use of alternate machines
- More stable schedules than full rescheduling after every event

---

## Strategy 3: Sequence-dependent setup optimization and dynamic family batching

### Core logic

Use the mined setup-transition matrix to form setup families or a weighted transition graph. At setup-intensive bottlenecks, optimize the sequence of ready and soon-to-arrive jobs.

A rolling-horizon objective can be:

\[
\min
\left[
\sum_j w_jT_j
+\lambda_s\sum_{(h,j)}\hat s_{h,j,m}
+\lambda_w\sum_j Waiting_j
\right]
\]

The sequence can be solved using CP-SAT, dynamic programming for small queues, or traveling-salesperson-style heuristics with due-date penalties.

Dynamic batching rules should include:

- Maximum batch size
- Maximum allowed waiting time
- Slack-based batch breaking
- Urgent-job insertion thresholds
- Forecast of jobs arriving soon from upstream
- Campaign constraints for tools, fixtures, or heat-treatment recipes

Jobs should not be grouped solely by product code. The setup model may reveal that two nominally different parts are setup-compatible, while two parts in the same family require costly transitions.

### Use of process-mining insights

The historical log identifies:

- High-cost transition pairs
- Natural low-setup clusters
- Machine-specific versus universal setup families
- Operator effects
- Family-switch frequency
- Whether setup savings historically caused unacceptable waiting

### Pathologies addressed

- Repeated family switching
- Underestimated setup load
- Bottleneck capacity lost to changeovers
- Hot jobs causing multiple reversal setups
- Fragmented campaigns

### Expected KPI impact

- Lower total setup time
- More productive bottleneck capacity
- Higher throughput
- Lower utilization spent on non-productive setup
- Lower tardiness where setup is a major capacity consumer

The batching tradeoff must be monitored carefully: excessive batching can reduce setup while increasing WIP and delaying urgent jobs.

---

## Strategy 4: WIP-controlled release and bottleneck-oriented coordination

### Core logic

Introduce workload control, CONWIP, or drum-buffer-rope principles. Jobs are released to the floor only when their route-specific workload can be absorbed.

A release condition can be based on:

\[
CurrentWorkload_m + JobWorkload_{j,m}
\leq WorkloadNorm_m
\]

for critical work centers on the job’s route.

The dynamic bottleneck schedule acts as the “drum.” Upstream resources protect its feed buffer, while downstream resources avoid unnecessary accumulation. A small capacity or WIP reserve can be maintained for urgent work.

### Use of process-mining insights

Process mining establishes:

- The empirical relationship between WIP and flow time
- Route-specific workload contributions
- Dynamic bottleneck locations
- Buffer levels associated with starvation versus congestion
- Release patterns that previously created queue waves

### Pathologies addressed

- Excessive WIP
- Unpredictable lead times
- Batch-release congestion
- Downstream starvation alternating with overload
- Premature release of work that cannot be processed soon

### Expected KPI impact

- Lower shop-floor WIP
- Shorter and more predictable flow times
- Reduced queue-time variability
- More stable bottleneck operation
- Better due-date reliability

Performance must still be measured from customer order acceptance, not only from shop-floor release, so that unreleased orders are not hidden as “off-floor WIP.”

# 5. Simulation, Evaluation, and Continuous Improvement

## 5.1 Process-mining-driven discrete-event simulation

A digital twin should be parameterized from the mined event log with:

- Arrival patterns by customer, family, and priority
- Route distributions and rework probabilities
- Conditional processing-time distributions
- Correlation among operations of the same job
- Machine- and operator-specific effects
- Setup-transition distributions
- Shift calendars and operator skills
- Alternative-machine eligibility
- Transport and blocking delays
- Breakdown frequency, repair distributions, and load dependence
- Priority-change and hot-job arrival processes
- Job-release policies

Correlations matter. For example, a difficult job may have long durations at several operations; independently sampling each duration would understate tail risk.

The baseline model should first reproduce historical:

- Throughput
- Utilization decomposition
- Queue lengths
- Setup hours
- Flow-time distributions
- Tardiness
- Breakdown recovery behavior

Calibration and validation should use separate chronological periods.

## 5.2 Experimental design

Compare:

1. Historical FCFS/EDD baseline
2. Dynamic dispatching
3. Predictive rolling-horizon scheduling
4. Setup-optimized sequencing
5. WIP-controlled release
6. Combinations of the above

Use common random numbers and many replications so that policies face the same demand and disruption realizations. Report confidence intervals, not just point estimates.

### Scenarios to test

- Normal historical load
- Demand increases of 10%, 20%, and 30%
- Bottleneck utilization near and above 100%
- High setup-intensity product mix
- Clustered order releases
- Tighter due dates
- Increased hot-job frequency
- Increased breakdown frequency
- Longer repair times
- Operator absenteeism
- Loss of an alternative machine
- Duration-estimation bias
- Product-mix drift
- Combined worst-case stress scenarios

### Evaluation metrics

- On-time delivery rate
- Mean and percentile tardiness
- Weighted and maximum tardiness
- Median and 90th percentile lead time
- Total and station-level WIP
- Throughput
- Productive, setup, idle, blocked, and downtime utilization
- Number and duration of setups
- Bottleneck starvation
- Schedule-change frequency
- ETA accuracy and prediction-interval coverage
- Computational response time
- Performance by priority and customer class

Results should be analyzed as a Pareto frontier because minimizing setup, tardiness, WIP, and schedule changes simultaneously may be impossible.

## 5.3 Deployment approach

A controlled implementation should proceed through:

1. **Shadow mode:** generate recommendations without controlling the shop.
2. **Planner review:** capture accept, reject, and override reasons.
3. **Pilot work center:** preferably a major bottleneck with reliable data.
4. **Switchback or phased trial:** compare equivalent shifts or periods.
5. **Expansion:** add other centers and release control after validation.

Human overrides should be logged as events. Repeated valid overrides may reveal constraints missing from the scheduling model.

## 5.4 Continuous process-mining and adaptation

A streaming state model should continuously update:

- Machine status
- Queue lengths and queue ages
- Job progress
- Remaining workload
- Predicted completion dates
- Late-job risk
- Current setup state
- Breakdown hazard
- Bottleneck location

Operational dashboards should distinguish leading from lagging indicators.

### Leading indicators

- Workload-to-capacity ratio
- Queue-age percentiles
- Predicted late-job probability
- Bottleneck buffer depletion
- Family-switch frequency
- Setup-time residuals
- Machine failure risk
- Schedule-deviation growth

### Lagging indicators

- On-time delivery
- Weighted tardiness
- Lead time
- WIP
- Throughput
- Setup hours
- Productive utilization
- Customer service performance

Conformance checking should continuously compare:

- Actual routing against the approved route
- Actual dispatch decisions against policy
- Actual starts and finishes against the frozen schedule
- Actual setup and processing times against predictions

## 5.5 Drift detection and governance

Automated drift detection should monitor:

- Processing-time distributions
- Setup-transition times
- Arrival rates and product mix
- Routing variants
- Breakdown and repair behavior
- Operator effects
- Queue behavior
- Prediction calibration

Suitable methods include control charts, CUSUM, change-point detection, population-stability indices, and distribution-distance measures. Triggers should initiate:

- Model retraining
- Setup-matrix recalibration
- Dispatch-weight retuning
- Simulation revalidation
- Workload-norm adjustment
- Planner review

Models, data, and policy versions should be retained for auditability. A champion–challenger framework can test updated models in shadow simulation before promotion.

## Overall recommendation

Precision Parts should not replace FCFS/EDD with a single more complicated rule. It should establish a closed-loop scheduling system consisting of:

1. Object-centric process mining for accurate state and performance diagnosis
2. Predictive duration, setup, queue, and failure models
3. Robust rolling-horizon finite-capacity scheduling
4. Dynamic local dispatching for real-time execution
5. Setup-aware sequencing at bottleneck machines
6. WIP-controlled job release
7. A process-mining-driven digital twin for policy testing
8. Continuous drift detection and policy recalibration

This architecture directly links historical evidence to scheduling decisions while recognizing the job shop’s uncertainty, sequence dependence, shifting bottlenecks, and frequent disruptions.
