# Queue Mining for Outpatient Flow Optimization — Analytical Approach & Recommendations

---

## 0. Preliminaries: Building a "Queue-Enabled" Event Log

Before any queue metric is credible, the raw log must be transformed into an **activity-instance table**:

| case_id | activity | resource | specialty | patient_type | urgency | t_start | t_complete | service_time | predecessor | t_queue_entry | wait_time |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Steps:

1. **Pivot START/COMPLETE pairs** into single activity instances (match on case + activity + resource; handle interleaving with a nearest-open-start rule).
2. **Data quality screening** — this is non-negotiable in healthcare logs:
   - Orphan STARTs / COMPLETEs (staff forgetting to close a task)  these inflate service times and must be flagged, not silently dropped.
   - Retrospective/batch data entry (multiple COMPLETEs recorded at 17:00 by one resource)  detectable via timestamp granularity clustering and identical timestamps; such records are unusable for queue analysis.
   - Negative or zero durations, overlapping instances for the same resource (multitasking vs. error).
   - Missing "arrival" event — critical, since the *first* queue (arrival  registration) is often the largest. If a kiosk/check-in event exists, use it; otherwise use **scheduled appointment time** as the reference and treat the result as "appointment lateness" rather than true wait.
3. **Enrich** with the appointment schedule (scheduled time, slot length, booking channel, no-show flag), staff rosters/shift plans, and room inventory. Queue mining without the *planned* capacity is half-blind.

## 1. Queue Identification and Characterization

### 1.1 Defining waiting time

The event log records **service** intervals, not queue intervals. Queue entry must therefore be **inferred**. For a case *c* and activity *a*:

$$W_c(a) = t^{start}_c(a) - t^{queue\_entry}_c(a)$$

where `t_queue_entry` is derived by rule:

| Situation | Queue entry definition |
|---|---|
| Sequential handover (Registration  Nurse) | `t_complete` of the predecessor activity |
| First activity of the visit | Kiosk check-in event; else max(scheduled time, arrival proxy) |
| Join after parallel branches (e.g., Doctor Review needs Blood + X-Ray) | `max(t_complete)` over all required predecessors (AND-join) |
| Rework / re-entry (Doctor  ECG  Doctor Review) | `t_complete` of the test — but this is a **re-entry queue** and must be measured separately from the first-visit doctor queue |
| Externally constrained wait (lab result turnaround, contrast timing, fasting) | Queue entry = specimen collection completion, but classify as **process delay**, not resource queue |

**Crucial decomposition.** The inter-activity gap is not all queueing:

$$\underbrace{t^{start}_c(a) - t^{complete}_c(pred)}_{\text{inter-activity gap}} = \underbrace{\tau_{transfer}}_{\text{walking, gowning, prep}} + \underbrace{W^{queue}_c(a)}_{\text{congestion}} + \underbrace{\delta_{patient}}_{\text{patient-caused, e.g. toilet, phone call}}$$

I estimate _transfer per activity pair as the **5th percentile of the observed gap distribution** (the best-case, uncongested transition — effectively the physical floor of the transition). Everything above that floor is treated as addressable congestion. This prevents us from "optimizing" the time it takes to walk from the lab to Room 3.

### 1.2 Characterization metrics

**Per queue (activity-transition), segmented by specialty / patient_type / urgency / hour-of-day / day-of-week / resource:**

| Metric | Formula / definition | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency *n* | # cases traversing the transition | Scale of exposure |
| Mean, median wait | — | Median resists outliers; mean drives total delay |
| p90 / p95 / max | — | Patient experience is driven by the tail, not the mean |
| CV of wait = / | — | Unpredictability is a distinct dissatisfaction driver |
| **Total delay contribution** | `n × mean wait` (hours/month) | The Pareto ranking metric — where the aggregate time is lost |
| SLA breach rate | % cases with wait > threshold (e.g. 15 min nurse, 20 min doctor) | Management-facing, actionable |
| Flow efficiency (per case) | ` service time / (t_checkout_complete  t_arrival)` | Typically 15–30% in outpatient clinics; the headline number for management |
| **Inferred queue length** L(t) | # cases with `t_queue_entry  t < t_start` | Turns queues into a *time series* — reveals morning surge, post-lunch collapse, sawtooth batching |
| Arrival rate (t) | queue entries per 15-min bin | Demand profile |
| Resource utilization _r(t) | busy time ÷ rostered time per 30-min bin | The single strongest predictor of wait |
| Little's Law consistency | `L   · W` | Sanity check that queue inference is sound; large mismatch  logging gaps or abandonment |
| Non-FIFO / overtaking rate | Spearman  between queue-entry rank and service-start rank; % of cases overtaken | Detects informal prioritization and "forgotten patient" injustice |
| Abandonment (LWBS) | cases with queue entry but no subsequent activity | Severe dissatisfaction signal |

### 1.3 Prioritizing the *critical* queues

I would rank queues on a **composite, multi-criteria score** rather than a single metric, because "longest average wait" alone misleads:

1. **Aggregate burden (weight ~35%)** — `n × mean wait`. A 40-min wait affecting 3% of patients matters less than a 12-min wait affecting 100%.
2. **Tail severity (~20%)** — p95 and SLA breach rate. This is what generates complaints.
3. **Critical-path share (~15%)** — the share of the queue's wait that lies on the patient's critical path (waits that overlap other productive activity are cheaper). Computed by aligning per-case timelines.
4. **Variability (~10%)** — high CV queues are both the most fixable (variability reduction is often free) and the most disliked.
5. **Clinical/equity risk (~15%)** — waits experienced by *urgent* patients, elderly, or those who abandon. Any urgent-flagged case waiting > clinical threshold is an automatic escalation regardless of statistics.
6. **Actionability (~5%)** — is the constraint internal (staffing, scheduling) or external (external lab contract)?

**Deliverable:** a bubble chart — x = frequency, y = mean wait, bubble size = p95, colour = downstream resource utilization — plus a Pareto chart of total delay contribution. In practice, 2–4 queues will typically account for 60–80% of all non-value-added time; my expectation in a clinic like this is (a) arrival  Registration, (b) Nurse Assessment  Doctor Consultation, and (c) the diagnostic re-entry loop Test  Doctor Review.

## 2. Root Cause Analysis

Localizing a queue is trivial; explaining it is the value-add. I would run six complementary analyses.

### 2.1 Capacity–demand mismatch (the most common cause)
Overlay (t) (queue entries per 15 min) against **staffed capacity c(t)**, where c(t) is derived from the log itself (first event – last event per resource per day, minus inferred break gaps) *and* cross-checked against the roster. Typical finding: a 08:30–10:00 arrival surge (block-booked "9:00" appointments) served by flat staffing, with clerks idle after 14:00.

Use **Kingman's approximation** to show non-linearity and set utilization targets:

$$W_q \approx \left(\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}\right)\left(\frac{c_a^2 + c_s^2}{2}\right)E[S]$$

This makes the business case quantitatively: at  = 0.90 the wait is roughly **twice** that at  = 0.82. Both  and the CVs (c_a, c_s) are directly estimable from the log.

### 2.2 Resource behaviour analysis
- **Utilization heatmaps** by resource × hour × weekday  identify individually overloaded staff, unequal load distribution (Dr. Smith at 0.94, Dr. Jones at 0.61  pooling/routing problem, not a capacity problem).
- **Multitasking detection**: overlapping activity instances for the same resource  service times are inflated and effective capacity overstated (a nurse "doing" three assessments at once is really slowing all three).
- **Batching detection**: sawtooth L(t) and clustered start times  the tech runs ECGs in blocks of four, adding structural wait.
- **Break/absence inference**: gaps > 20 min in a resource's activity stream, correlated with queue spikes  uncoordinated lunch breaks collapsing capacity at 12:00–13:00.
- **Fatigue / end-of-session effects**: regress service time on position-in-shift and current queue length. Finding *shorter* service under load is a **quality risk flag**, not a win.

### 2.3 Service-time variability & activity-duration analysis
Fit distributions of service time per (activity, specialty, patient_type, resource). Expect New patients to require 1.5–2× the time of Follow-ups. If the appointment slot is uniform (e.g., 15 min) while E[S|New] = 24 min, delay accumulates deterministically through the session — testable by **regressing appointment lateness on slot index within the session**; a positive slope of, say, +2.5 min per slot is smoking-gun evidence of slot-length misspecification, not of "slow doctors."

### 2.4 Scheduling policy conformance
- Punctuality: distribution of (actual queue entry  scheduled time); early arrivals inflate measured "wait" and mask true performance.
- Overbooking and double-booking incidence vs. realized no-show rate.
- **Conformance checking** against the intended scheduling rules (e.g., "no more than 2 New patients per hour per physician") reveals violations correlated with wait spikes.
- No-show clustering: a no-show creates idle capacity that is *not* recoverable later  asymmetric loss, argues for predictive overbooking.

### 2.5 Variant & control-flow analysis
- Discover the DFG / process model with **performance overlay** to locate the longest-duration edges.
- **Variant analysis**: compare the fast decile of cases vs. the slow decile. Slow variants frequently contain the **Doctor  Test  Doctor Review re-entry loop**, where the patient re-enters a queue behind newly arrived, freshly scheduled patients — an unmanaged, unscheduled second queue. Quantify: share of cases with the loop × mean re-entry wait.
- Detect **rework**, unnecessary handovers, and hidden sequential dependencies that could be parallelized (e.g., Blood Test and X-Ray always sequential though independent).

### 2.6 Explanatory modelling (decision mining)
Train an interpretable model (gradient boosting + SHAP, or a regression tree) with target = wait time for the critical queue and features = {queue length at entry, resource on duty, hour, weekday, patient_type, urgency, specialty, upstream delay, staffing level, no-show count that session}. This yields a **ranked, quantified causal-candidate list** ("queue length at entry and #nurses-on-duty explain 61% of variance in the doctor queue; patient_type adds 9%") and disciplines the discussion away from anecdote. Confirm directionality with a **discrete-event simulation** calibrated on the log before committing to changes.

## 3. Data-Driven Optimization Strategies

### Strategy 1 — Demand-matched, staggered staffing at the front end (+ pre-registration)

- **Target queue:** Arrival  Registration (and its knock-on to Nurse Assessment).
- **Root cause:** Flat clerk staffing against a bimodal arrival profile (08:00–10:00 surge, 13:00 trough); long service times for New patients doing full data entry at the desk.
- **Data support:** (t) vs. c(t) overlay; _clerk  0.93 at 09:00 vs. 0.45 at 14:00; L(t) peak of 9–11 patients; service time E[S|New]  6.5 min vs. E[S|Follow-up]  3.5 min.
- **Intervention:** Re-cut the same clerk FTE hours — e.g., 4 clerks 07:45–11:00, 2 clerks 11:00–16:00 — with staggered breaks enforced so capacity never drops below 2. In parallel, deploy online/SMS pre-registration for Follow-ups and a self-service kiosk lane, converting ~40% of transactions into a 60–90-second identity check.
- **Expected impact:** Reducing peak  from 0.93  0.78 with modest service-time reduction yields, via Kingman and confirmed by simulation, a **50–65% reduction in mean registration wait** and a larger p90 reduction — **at zero incremental FTE cost**. Downstream, the nurse queue's arrival burstiness (c_a²) falls, giving a second-order benefit.

### Strategy 2 — Empirically calibrated, patient-type-aware appointment slots and sequencing

- **Target queue:** Nurse Assessment  Doctor Consultation (the largest tail).
- **Root cause:** Uniform slot length vs. bimodal service-time distribution; block booking at the top of the hour; lateness accumulation within sessions.
- **Data support:** Per-physician, per-patient-type service-time distributions; slope of lateness vs. slot index (+2–3 min/slot); no-show rate by patient segment and lead time.
- **Intervention:**
  1. **Variable slot lengths** set from the empirical distribution (e.g., slot = median + 0.5 by type/specialty), not a clinic-wide default.
  2. **Individual (staggered) rather than block booking** — smooth arrivals across the hour.
  3. **Sequencing rule:** front-load low-variance Follow-ups early in the session and interleave high-variance New patients, keeping a small "recovery" buffer slot mid-session (a Bailey–Welch style rule tuned by simulation).
  4. **Predictive, targeted overbooking** using a no-show model (features: lead time, history, age, weather, day) applied only to slots with predicted no-show probability > 0.35.
- **Expected impact:** Simulation of comparable redesigns typically yields **25–40% reduction in mean doctor wait and 40%+ reduction in p90**, with physician utilization held constant or slightly improved (no-show gaps recovered offsets slot lengthening).

### Strategy 3 — Nurse pooling with cross-training and a queue-triggered float resource

- **Target queue:** Registration  Nurse Assessment.
- **Root cause:** Nurses dedicated to individual specialty pods  many small, uncorrelated queues; a busy pod cannot borrow idle capacity. High CV of wait, low mean utilization (~0.65) yet long waits — the classic signature of **fragmented capacity**.
- **Data support:** Resource–activity matrix showing near-zero cross-pod activity; simultaneous L(t) > 4 in Cardio while Cardio-adjacent pods sit at L(t) = 0 for the same 30-min bin (quantify as "co-idle-while-congested minutes per week").
- **Intervention:** Cross-train nurses for a common vitals/intake protocol and pool 60–70% of assessment capacity, keeping specialty-specific tasks dedicated. Add a **real-time escalation rule**: when inferred queue length for any pod exceeds 3 patients or predicted wait exceeds 15 min, the float nurse is dispatched (dashboard alert).
- **Expected impact:** Pooling *c* independent single-server queues into one *c*-server queue is one of the highest-leverage, lowest-cost moves in queueing: expect **30–50% mean wait reduction and a sharp CV reduction** with no added FTE. Validate with simulation before rollout.

### Strategy 4 — Protocol-based front-loading and parallelization of diagnostics

- **Target queue:** Test  Doctor Review (the re-entry loop) and Doctor  Test.
- **Root cause:** Strictly sequential Consult  Test  Review flow; the returning patient joins the back of a queue populated by scheduled arrivals. Independent tests (Blood, X-Ray) executed serially.
- **Data support:** Variant analysis showing, e.g., 78% of New Cardiology patients receive an ECG and 65% a basic blood panel; conditional probability of test given (specialty, patient_type, referral reason) is high and predictable. Mean re-entry wait  22 min, affecting ~30% of visits.
- **Intervention:**
  1. **Standing orders / protocol-driven pre-consultation testing** for clearly defined cohorts, ordered at booking or at nurse assessment, so results are available *before* the consultation — eliminating the loop entirely for those cases.
  2. **Parallelize independent tests** (route to whichever of Blood/X-Ray is free — "shortest-queue-first" routing rather than fixed order).
  3. **Priority re-entry lane**: returning patients get a reserved fraction of physician capacity (e.g., protected 5-minute review micro-slots) so they are not penalized for having been sent away.
- **Expected impact:** Removing the loop for ~50–60% of loop cases plus priority re-entry for the remainder  **estimated 15–25 minutes off total visit duration** for affected patients and a measurable drop in overall p90 visit length. **Governance requirement:** protocols must be authored and owned by clinicians to avoid low-value testing (see §4).

### Strategy 5 — Real-time queue-aware coordination layer (predictive flow board + patient notification)

- **Target queues:** All; particularly perceived wait and the "forgotten patient."
- **Root cause:** No visibility of real-time congestion; non-FIFO overtaking; patients physically waiting for delays that are already known.
- **Data support:** Non-FIFO overtaking rate; feasibility demonstrated by training a **delay-prediction model** on historical logs (features = queue length, staffing, hour, case attributes) — typical achievable accuracy: MAE of 4–7 minutes for next-step wait.
- **Intervention:** A flow board showing per-stage inferred queue length, longest-waiting patient, and predicted wait; automated SMS to patients ("your consultation is now expected at 10:40 — feel free to step out until 10:25"); a WIP cap so registration does not push patients into a saturated nurse stage; a hard alert for any patient waiting > 30 min or any urgent patient waiting > clinical threshold.
- **Expected impact:** Modest effect on *actual* wait (5–10% via better dispatch and WIP control) but a large effect on **perceived** wait and satisfaction — the literature consistently shows that communicated, explained waits are tolerated far better than unexplained ones. Also directly reduces the worst-case tail.

## 4. Trade-offs and Constraints

| Strategy | Principal risks / side effects | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — Staggered front-end staffing | **Bottleneck migration**: faster registration simply delivers patients to the nurse queue sooner, converting one wait into another and possibly worsening it. Staff dissatisfaction with earlier start times; union/contract limits. | Always evaluate end-to-end in the simulation, not per stage. Sequence rollout with Strategy 3 so downstream capacity is ready. Negotiate shift changes with staff; rotate early shifts fairly. |
| 2 — Slot redesign & overbooking | Longer slots reduce sessions' nominal capacity  **throughput/revenue loss** unless offset. Overbooking, if no-show prediction underperforms, produces *longer* waits and **staff overtime**. | Offset slot lengthening with recovered no-show capacity; cap overbooking at 1 per session initially; monitor overtime and session over-run as hard guardrails; run as an A/B pilot on two physicians before clinic-wide rollout. |
| 3 — Nurse pooling | Loss of **continuity of care** and specialty-specific expertise; cross-training cost and temporary competence dip; risk of "generalist" assessments missing specialty nuance. | Pool only the standardized intake component; retain dedicated specialty nurses for complex tasks. Formal competency sign-off. Monitor assessment quality indicators and physician-reported rework. |
| 4 — Front-loaded diagnostics | **Over-testing**: tests ordered on patients who would not have needed them  direct cost, incidental findings, false positives, patient harm. This is the most clinically dangerous proposal in the set. | Restrict to cohorts where the observed conditional test probability is very high (>75%) *and* a clinician-approved guideline exists. Track ordering rate and yield (% abnormal/actionable) as an explicit guardrail KPI; auto-suspend the protocol if yield degrades. |
| 5 — Flow board / notifications | Surveillance perception among staff; the metric becomes a target (**Goodhart's law**) — e.g., staff record START on arrival at the room rather than at contact, artificially shrinking measured wait. Digital exclusion of patients without smartphones. | Position the board as team-level flow support, never individual performance ranking; audit timestamp integrity (compare against door-sensor/kiosk data samples); maintain a non-digital pathway. |
| **Cross-cutting** | Optimizing the mean while the tail worsens; improvements for Follow-ups achieved at the expense of New/complex patients; **quality erosion** as clinicians unconsciously compress consultations under visible queue pressure. | Report p90 alongside means; stratify every KPI by patient_type and urgency; treat **mean consultation duration** as a *guardrail that must not fall*, and monitor 7/30-day return rates. |

### Balancing conflicting objectives

I would frame this explicitly as **constrained optimization rather than trade-off haggling**:

> *Minimize* total patient waiting time (p50 and p90), *subject to*: staffing cost  baseline + 2%; mean consultation duration  current baseline; diagnostic ordering rate  baseline + 5%; no increase in 30-day return rate; staff utilization  0.85; p95 urgent-patient wait  clinical threshold.

Under these constraints, **variability-reduction and pooling measures (Strategies 1, 2, 3) are strongly preferred**, because they buy waiting-time reduction with *coordination* rather than money or clinical time. Capacity purchases (extra FTE, extra rooms) are the last resort, justified only by an explicit business case (cost per hour of patient wait avoided, plus throughput revenue). Where genuine value conflicts remain — e.g., slot length vs. throughput — the decision belongs to a **governance group including clinicians, operations, and a patient representative**, informed by simulated Pareto frontiers (wait vs. cost vs. consultation length) so the trade is made with numbers rather than intuition.

## 5. Measuring Success

### 5.1 KPI framework

**Tier 1 — Patient experience & flow (primary)**
- Mean, median, **p90** total waiting time per visit; and per critical queue.
- **Total visit duration** (arrival  check-out complete): mean and p90.
- **Flow efficiency** =  service time / visit duration (target: from ~25%   40%).
- % of patients whose wait for any stage exceeds 15 / 30 minutes (SLA breach rate).
- **Appointment start punctuality**: distribution of (consult start  scheduled time); % starting within 10 min.
- LWBS / abandonment rate; non-FIFO overtaking rate; longest single wait per session.
- Patient-reported: wait-specific satisfaction item + NPS (link survey to case ID for per-case correlation).

**Tier 2 — Operational efficiency**
- Patients completed per session per physician (throughput).
- Resource utilization by role and hour (target band 0.70–0.85 — *not* maximized).
- Session over-run minutes and overtime hours.
- Room/equipment utilization; queue length L(t) peak and time-above-threshold.
- No-show rate and realized overbooking accuracy (predicted vs. actual).

**Tier 3 — Quality & safety guardrails (must not degrade)**
- Mean consultation duration by patient type (anti-compression guard).
- 7- and 30-day unplanned return / re-consultation rate.
- Diagnostic ordering rate **and yield** (% actionable results) — the over-testing guard.
- Documentation completeness; clinical incident reports; complaint volume by theme.
- Staff experience: workload survey, break compliance, turnover/absence.

**Tier 4 — Financial**
- Cost per completed visit; overtime cost; net effect of throughput change.

Every KPI is defined with an explicit formula, data source, owner, baseline (six-month pre-intervention log), and target, and is **stratified** by specialty, patient type, urgency, weekday, and time-of-day to avoid Simpson's-paradox misreads.

### 5.2 Ongoing monitoring architecture

1. **Automated pipeline:** nightly ETL from the clinic information system into the same activity-instance schema, re-running the queue-inference logic. The analysis becomes a *product*, not a one-off project.
2. **Dashboards at three cadences:**
   - *Real-time* (operational): live queue lengths, predicted waits, longest-waiting-patient alerts — used by the flow coordinator during the session.
   - *Weekly* (tactical): Pareto of total delay contribution by queue, SPC charts of the top KPIs, exception list of visits > p95 duration for case review in a 15-minute team huddle.
   - *Monthly/quarterly* (strategic): full process map with performance overlay, variant analysis, resource utilization heatmaps, re-calibrated simulation, KPI vs. target scorecard.
3. **Statistical rigour in attribution:** use **statistical process control** (X-S charts for continuous KPIs, p-charts for breach rates) with Western Electric rules to distinguish signal from noise; use **interrupted time-series / difference-in-differences** (e.g., pods that adopted pooling vs. those not yet) rather than naive before/after comparison, controlling for seasonality and case-mix drift.
4. **Pilot-first discipline:** each strategy is deployed as a controlled pilot (two physicians, or Tuesdays/Thursdays) with pre-registered primary KPI, minimum detectable effect, duration, and **stopping rules** tied to the guardrail KPIs. Scale only on demonstrated effect.
5. **Concept-drift detection:** monitor the discovered process model, service-time distributions, and arrival profile for drift; when the model that underpins the slot lengths or staffing curve degrades (e.g., case-mix shift, new specialty added), trigger re-estimation. Wait times regress without maintenance — the schedule and roster should be re-optimized on a rolling quarterly basis.
6. **Closing the loop with patients and staff:** publish a simple monthly "you said / we did / here's the measured result" summary. Sustained improvement in healthcare processes is as much about the credibility of the feedback loop as about the algorithms behind it.

### Summary

The approach is: (1) **reconstruct** queue entry/exit from START/COMPLETE pairs and separate genuine congestion from transfer time; (2) **rank** queues by aggregate delay burden, tail severity, critical-path share, and clinical risk; (3) **explain** them via capacity–demand overlays, utilization and pooling analysis, service-time variability, scheduling conformance, and variant analysis, confirmed by a calibrated simulation; (4) **intervene** primarily through variability reduction, capacity pooling, demand-matched rostering, empirically calibrated slots, and selective parallelization — all of which buy waiting-time reduction without buying capacity; and (5) **sustain** the gains through automated re-analysis, SPC-based monitoring, and quality guardrails that make it impossible to "win" on wait times by quietly degrading care.
