## 1. Queue Identification and Characterization

### 1.1 Construct activity instances and define waiting time

The event rows should first be converted into activity instances:

\[
(c,\; a,\; S_{c,a},\; C_{c,a},\; resource,\; patient\ attributes)
\]

where \(S\) and \(C\) are the matched start and completion times. Repeated activities, such as a second blood test, require an occurrence or instance identifier so that starts and completions are paired correctly.

For a serial transition \(A \rightarrow B\):

- **Service time of \(A\):**
  \[
  D_{c,A}=C_{c,A}-S_{c,A}
  \]

- **Transition waiting time:**
  \[
  W_{c,A\rightarrow B}=S_{c,B}-C_{c,A}
  \]

This measures the time during which the patient has completed \(A\) but has not started \(B\).

For the conceptual V1001 example:

- Registration complete to nurse start: 6 minutes 35 seconds
- Nurse complete to doctor start: 20 minutes 45 seconds
- Doctor complete to ECG start: 11 minutes 45 seconds
- ECG complete to check-out start: 7 minutes 55 seconds

The observed inter-activity waiting time is therefore 47 minutes. The service times sum to 52 minutes 55 seconds, giving 99 minutes 55 seconds from registration start to check-out completion.

However, this excludes waiting before registration because a clinic-arrival or check-in timestamp is not shown.

### 1.2 Distinguish a true queue from a transition delay

A start/completion log provides a useful proxy, but \(C_A\) is not always the true time at which the patient entered the queue for \(B\).

Ideally, queue time is:

\[
W_{c,B}=S_{c,B}-E_{c,B}
\]

where \(E_{c,B}\) is the time at which the patient became **ready or enabled** for \(B\).

Examples:

- For a diagnostic test, the queue may begin when the physician places the order, not when the consultation is completed.
- If a task requires several predecessors, such as both registration and insurance approval, then:
  \[
  E_{c,B}=\max(C_{c,A_1},C_{c,A_2},...)
  \]
- If two activities can occur in parallel, simply sorting events by timestamp and subtracting consecutive events will produce misleading or negative waits.
- A transition gap can contain transport, room cleaning, preparation, intentional observation, or clinical decision time—not only queuing.

Thus, the initial analysis should call these values **inter-activity delays**. They can be classified as true queueing, transport, planned delay, or other non-service time after reviewing the process model and adding readiness/order timestamps where necessary.

Negative values should not be silently set to zero. They may represent:

- Legitimate parallel work
- Incorrect activity sequencing
- Clock synchronization errors
- Incorrectly paired lifecycle events
- Ambiguous timestamp semantics

### 1.3 Special boundaries

Additional timestamps are required to measure the full patient experience:

- **Arrival-to-registration wait:** registration start minus physical arrival/check-in
- **Appointment delay:** relevant service start minus scheduled appointment time
- **Departure delay:** physical departure minus completion of the last clinical or administrative activity
- **Abandonment:** queue entry without a corresponding service start

Calendar waiting and operational waiting should also be separated. For example, an overnight interval before a planned next-day test should not necessarily be treated as an active clinic queue. Both elapsed patient time and staffed-business-time metrics may be useful.

### 1.4 Reconstruct queue length over time

For each destination activity \(B\), each patient has:

- Queue-entry/readiness time \(E_{c,B}\)
- Service-start time \(S_{c,B}\)
- Service-completion time \(C_{c,B}\)

The estimated number waiting at time \(t\) is:

\[
Q_B(t)=\sum_c I(E_{c,B}\leq t<S_{c,B})
\]

This allows the clinic to generate queue-length profiles by 15- or 30-minute interval, day of week, specialty, urgency, and resource pool.

Queue lengths should be reconstructed separately where resources are not interchangeable. For example, cardiology consultation, orthopedics consultation, and general medicine should not be treated as one doctor queue unless cross-coverage is operationally possible.

### 1.5 Metrics for each queue

| Metric | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Mean waiting time | Overall average burden, but sensitive to outliers |
| Median waiting time | Typical patient experience |
| 75th, 90th, and 95th percentiles | Reliability and long-tail waits |
| Maximum wait | Useful for investigating extreme cases, but unstable as a primary KPI |
| Queue occurrence count | Number of times the transition occurred |
| Queue frequency | Percentage of eligible visits that entered or experienced the queue |
| SLA breach rate | Percentage with \(W>\tau_q\), where \(\tau_q\) is the threshold for queue \(q\) |
| Number of affected visits | Unique cases with at least one excessive wait |
| Total waiting burden | \(\sum W\), expressed as patient-hours |
| Excess waiting burden | \(\sum \max(W-\tau_q,0)\) |
| Average and peak queue length | Operational congestion at the station |
| Percentage of time queue exceeds a threshold | Persistence of congestion |
| Arrival rate \(\lambda\) | Number becoming ready per unit of time |
| Service rate and service-time distribution | Capacity and variability |
| Resource utilization | Busy time divided by rostered available time |
| Flow efficiency | Service time divided by end-to-end visit duration |

Metrics should be reported:

- Per queue occurrence
- Per eligible visit
- Per completed visit
- By hour and day
- By specialty and provider
- By new versus follow-up
- By urgency
- By route or process variant

Averages alone can hide serious problems. For example, a queue with a 10-minute mean but a 75-minute 90th percentile may be less reliable than a queue with a stable 15-minute wait.

### 1.6 Prioritize the most critical queues

Queues should not be ranked solely by longest mean wait. A practical prioritization framework should combine:

1. **Total burden:** total patient-hours lost in the queue
2. **Tail severity:** 90th/95th percentile and maximum validated wait
3. **Frequency:** percentage and number of visits affected
4. **SLA breaches:** prevalence and amount by which thresholds are exceeded
5. **Contribution to total visit duration**
6. **Clinical importance:** especially waiting experienced by urgent or vulnerable patients
7. **Equity:** whether particular patient groups consistently wait longer
8. **Downstream effects:** whether the queue causes missed tests, staff overtime, or later congestion
9. **Actionability and data confidence**

A Pareto analysis is useful. For example, a small number of transition queues may account for 70–80% of all waiting hours. Those should generally receive priority, while rare extreme waits should be separately investigated for safety or process failures.

## 2. Root Cause Analysis

### 2.1 Resource capacity and utilization

Long waits may indicate insufficient staff, rooms, or equipment, but utilization must be interpreted carefully.

For each resource, calculate:

- Busy intervals from activity start to completion
- Concurrent work
- Busy time by hour and day
- Number of available servers
- Rostered or scheduled capacity
- Overtime and late clinic completion
- Queue length while the resource is busy or idle

Resource availability data should be joined to the event log. Event records alone do not show whether a person was off duty, on break, performing non-logged work, or available but idle.

Typical diagnostic patterns include:

| Observed pattern | Likely hypothesis |
|---|---|
| Queue grows while all qualified resources are continuously busy | Insufficient or temporally misaligned capacity |
| Long queue while a qualified resource appears idle | Handover, dispatch, room, information, or queue-management problem |
| Low daily utilization but high utilization during short peaks | Capacity is scheduled at the wrong times |
| Staff available but rooms/equipment unavailable | Physical-resource bottleneck |
| Staff and rooms individually available but not simultaneously | Synchronization problem |
| One provider has longer queues after case-mix adjustment | Provider-specific workflow, scheduling, or service-time variation |

Resource names such as “Tech X / Room 3” should preferably be split into separate staff and room fields so the limiting resource can be identified.

### 2.2 Activity dependencies and handovers

Some delays arise from poor coordination rather than raw capacity:

- Test order not transmitted promptly
- Nurse does not know that a room is available
- Doctor is waiting for a test result while the patient appears to be waiting for the doctor
- Patient is transferred between departments without ownership
- Check-out staff receive a burst of patients after several consultations end together
- Results are reviewed in batches rather than continuously

A handover matrix can compare waiting times by:

- Origin activity and destination activity
- Origin resource and destination resource
- Department pair
- Time of day
- Patient type

If a particular resource or department pair repeatedly generates long waits, the problem may be communication or coordination rather than staffing.

### 2.3 Service-time variability

Queueing delays grow nonlinearly as resource utilization and service-time variability increase. Two resources with the same average service time may have very different queues if one has a highly variable or long-tailed service distribution.

For every activity and resource, calculate:

- Mean, median, and percentile service times
- Standard deviation and coefficient of variation
- Service-time distribution by patient type
- Outlier frequency
- Variability by provider, room, day, and process variant

Examples of causes include:

- New patients placed in slots designed for follow-ups
- Complex cases mixed randomly with simple cases
- Incomplete pre-visit information
- Repeated history collection
- Unavailable test results
- Room setup or equipment problems
- Rework caused by missing orders or specimen errors

Long clinical encounters should not automatically be labeled inefficient. The analysis must distinguish clinically appropriate complexity from preventable nonclinical variation.

### 2.4 Appointment scheduling policies

The event log should be joined with scheduled appointment times, slot lengths, arrival times, cancellations, and no-shows.

Questions include:

- Are many patients booked at the same time, creating a “wave”?
- Are slot lengths aligned with actual service-time distributions?
- Are new and follow-up patients scheduled identically despite different needs?
- Are urgent add-ons inserted into already full periods?
- Do providers start clinics late?
- Is overbooking based on current no-show patterns, or on outdated assumptions?
- Are diagnostics coordinated with consultation times?
- Are breaks scheduled during peak arrival periods?

A heatmap of scheduled arrivals, actual arrivals, queue entries, service starts, and available capacity can reveal mismatches.

### 2.5 Patient arrival patterns

Physical arrival timestamps are needed to distinguish appointment-system problems from downstream process problems. Analyze:

- Arrivals by 15- or 30-minute interval
- Early, on-time, and late arrival rates
- Walk-in volume
- Day-of-week and seasonal patterns
- Arrival bursts after transport services or clinic opening
- Variability rather than only average volume

A clinic can have enough daily capacity but still experience long queues if demand arrives in short bursts.

### 2.6 Patient type, urgency, and route complexity

Waiting and service times should be stratified by:

- New versus follow-up
- Specialty
- Urgency
- Need for diagnostics
- Number of departments visited
- Age or mobility needs where legally and ethically appropriate
- Language or assistance requirements

Urgent patients may legitimately overtake normal-priority patients. Queue-mining should therefore reconstruct priority classes and compare patients within the same priority where appropriate.

A long normal-priority wait may be caused by frequent urgent insertions rather than simple understaffing. Conversely, urgent patients with long waits may indicate a safety problem requiring immediate action.

### 2.7 Process-mining techniques to pinpoint causes

Several techniques should be used together:

1. **Performance-annotated process discovery**  
   Build a directly-follows graph or process model showing:
   - Activity frequency
   - Service duration
   - Transition waiting time
   - Rework and loops
   - Common and exceptional routes

2. **Variant analysis**  
   Compare routes such as:
   - Registration  nurse  doctor  check-out
   - Registration  nurse  doctor  ECG  doctor review  check-out
   - Repeated diagnostics or consultation loops  
   
   Identify variants with disproportionate waiting or visit duration.

3. **Conformance checking**  
   Compare actual traces with the intended workflow. Detect:
   - Skipped steps
   - Unplanned rework
   - Out-of-order activities
   - Patients returning to earlier stages
   - Deviations that cause downstream congestion

4. **Resource-centric process mining**  
   Analyze workload, utilization, handovers, resource pooling, and provider-specific performance after adjusting for patient type and complexity.

5. **Queue-discipline analysis**  
   Compare queue-entry order with service-start order to identify:
   - Urgency-based prioritization
   - Overtaking
   - Batching
   - Preferential selection of simpler cases
   - Potential fairness issues

6. **Statistical and predictive analysis**  
   Model waiting time as a function of:
   - Time of day and day of week
   - Current queue length
   - Resource availability
   - Patient type and urgency
   - Prior activity duration
   - Provider
   - Process variant  
   
   Mixed-effects, quantile, or survival models can be more appropriate than ordinary average-based regression.

7. **Discrete-event simulation**  
   Calibrate a simulation with empirical arrival patterns, service-time distributions, routes, priorities, and resource calendars. Use it to test proposed changes before disrupting clinic operations.

Process mining can identify strong associations and operational mechanisms, but a controlled pilot is still needed to establish whether an intervention causes the expected improvement.

## 3. Data-Driven Optimization Strategies

Because the full six-month dataset has not been supplied, exact percentage reductions cannot be credibly claimed in advance. Each strategy should be evaluated in a calibrated simulation and then piloted. The predicted reduction can be reported as:

\[
Reduction_q=\frac{W_{q,baseline}-W_{q,scenario}}{W_{q,baseline}}\times100\%
\]

### Strategy 1: Align staffing and resource schedules with measured demand peaks

**Queues targeted**

- Registration-to-nurse
- Nurse-to-doctor
- Doctor-to-diagnostic test
- Diagnostic-to-review
- Final activity-to-check-out

**Root cause addressed**

- Daily staffing is adequate in aggregate but not aligned with peak queue-entry rates
- Breaks or shift changes occur during peak demand
- Rooms, technicians, and staff are not synchronized
- Some resources are overloaded while equivalent resources are underused

**Data basis and actions**

Use hourly or 30-minute profiles of:

- Queue entries
- Queue lengths
- Resource utilization
- Service completions
- Staffing and room availability

Possible actions include:

- Staggering clerk, nurse, and technician start times
- Moving breaks away from recurring peaks
- Creating a cross-trained float role for predictable peak periods
- Pooling interchangeable rooms or technicians
- Assigning short periods of cross-coverage between registration and check-out
- Adding limited peak-only capacity rather than a full additional shift

The first preference should be reallocation of existing paid hours. Permanent staffing should be added only if sustained queues occur while all relevant resources are highly utilized.

**Potential impact**

A simulation may show, for example, that moving one existing nurse-hour from a low-demand period to 9:00–11:00 reduces a modeled 90th-percentile wait from 30 to 20 minutes—a 33% reduction with unchanged total staffing hours. Such a number would be a scenario result, not an assumption.

The whole patient flow must be simulated because relieving the nurse queue may simply move congestion to doctors or diagnostics.

---

### Strategy 2: Redesign appointment templates and smooth arrivals

**Queues targeted**

- Arrival-to-registration
- Registration-to-nurse
- Nurse-to-doctor
- Doctor-to-diagnostic test

**Root cause addressed**

- Wave scheduling
- Slot lengths that do not reflect actual service needs
- Uncontrolled urgent add-ons
- Inappropriate overbooking
- Simultaneous release of many patients into downstream services

**Data basis and actions**

Use actual service-time distributions by specialty and patient type rather than one average slot length.

Actions can include:

- Longer slots for new or historically complex visits
- Shorter but appropriately spaced follow-up slots
- Avoiding many appointments at the same clock time
- Limiting the number of high-complexity visits in one period
- Reserving protected capacity for urgent add-ons
- Dynamically adjusting overbooking based on current no-show and lateness patterns
- Coordinating diagnostics with consultation slots
- Asking patients to arrive at differentiated times instead of uniformly “15 minutes early”

**Potential impact**

Arrival smoothing often reduces waiting disproportionately because queueing increases nonlinearly near capacity. The simulation should report:

- Mean and 90th-percentile wait reduction
- Effect on provider idle time
- Throughput
- Overtime
- Number of unused reserved slots

A practical pilot target might be at least a 15–20% reduction in the targeted queue’s 90th percentile without reducing completed visits or worsening urgent-patient performance.

---

### Strategy 3: Redesign the flow by removing unnecessary serial steps

**Queues targeted**

- Arrival-to-registration
- Registration-to-nurse
- Doctor-to-test
- Test-to-specialist-review
- Final clinical activity-to-check-out

**Root cause addressed**

- Activities performed serially even when they are independent
- Repeated data collection
- Unnecessary handoffs
- Mixing simple and complex visits in one flow
- Administrative work performed only while the patient is physically present

**Data basis and actions**

Variant analysis can identify routes with long delays but little clinical value added. Potential redesigns include:

- Online or telephone pre-registration
- Pre-visit questionnaires and medication reconciliation
- Insurance verification before the visit
- Direct rooming for fully pre-registered patients
- Pre-scheduling protocol-approved diagnostics when clinically appropriate
- Parallel preparation of test orders or discharge instructions
- A fast-track flow for simple follow-up visits
- Point-of-care or electronic check-out
- Scheduling future appointments electronically rather than requiring a physical check-out queue

Only clinically independent activities should be parallelized. Tests should not be advanced merely to reduce waits if clinical review is required first.

**Potential impact**

If a redesigned step is adopted by proportion \(a\), its approximate mean saving is:

\[
a \times (current\ mean\ queue\ time-residual\ redesigned\ time)
\]

For example, electronic check-out can nearly eliminate the physical check-out queue for eligible patients, although digital completion time and patient effort should still be measured. Pre-registration may shift effort away from the clinic rather than eliminate it, so total patient burden should be monitored.

---

### Strategy 4: Introduce real-time pull-based coordination and queue visibility

**Queues targeted**

- Nurse-to-doctor
- Doctor-to-diagnostic test
- Test-to-specialist-review
- Room assignment and handover queues

**Root cause addressed**

- Patients are ready but the next resource is unaware
- Departments operate from separate worklists
- Queue ownership is unclear
- Rooms or resources are assigned manually and slowly
- Patients are selected inconsistently from the queue

**Data basis and actions**

This strategy is most appropriate when analysis shows long waits despite an available receiver.

Actions include:

- Recording explicit “ordered,” “ready,” and “result available” timestamps
- A shared queue dashboard showing readiness, urgency, wait age, and dependencies
- Automatic notifications when the preceding activity finishes
- Dynamic assignment to the next qualified provider or room
- FIFO within urgency classes, with escalation for excessive waits
- Alerts for patients approaching stage-specific thresholds
- A single pooled queue where providers are clinically interchangeable

**Potential impact**

The potential saving is the portion of delay during which the patient was ready and an appropriate resource was available. That avoidable idle-gap can be estimated retrospectively. A pilot can then measure what percentage is removed.

A dashboard will not solve a true capacity shortage, but it can substantially reduce information and handover latency.

---

### Strategy 5: Reduce preventable service-time variability and rework

**Queues targeted**

- Nurse and doctor queues
- Diagnostic testing queues
- Specialist-review queues
- Repeated-activity loops

**Root cause addressed**

- Missing information
- Inconsistent room preparation
- Equipment downtime
- Repeated documentation
- Test or specimen errors
- Unstructured mixing of short and long cases
- Nonclinical work occurring during scarce clinician time

**Data basis and actions**

Identify activities and resources with high service-time coefficients of variation, long tails, or repeated loops. Then investigate the corresponding cases.

Possible interventions include:

- Pre-visit chart and referral review
- Standardized room setup and stocking
- Diagnostic equipment maintenance based on downtime patterns
- Standardized nonclinical portions of visits
- Separating short routine follow-ups from complex consultations
- Support staff preparing forms, orders, and educational materials
- Error-proofing specimen and test-order processes
- Removing duplicate documentation

Clinical judgment and necessary consultation time must not be capped merely to improve throughput.

**Potential impact**

Near high utilization, even modest reductions in preventable service-time variation can materially reduce queues. The simulation should use full service-time distributions, not only average durations, and report whether the improvement comes from reduced mean time, reduced variability, or reduced rework.

## 4. Trade-offs and Constraints

### 4.1 Bottleneck migration

Reducing one queue may move the bottleneck downstream:

- More rapid registration can overload nurses
- More rapid nurse assessment can increase the doctor queue
- Faster consultation throughput can overload diagnostics or check-out
- Pre-scheduled tests can create a new early-morning diagnostic peak

Therefore, interventions should be assessed against end-to-end visit duration and all major queue KPIs, not only the targeted queue.

### 4.2 Utilization versus waiting

Maximizing staff utilization is not the same as optimizing flow. Running a variable service system near 100% utilization causes unstable and rapidly growing queues. Some peak-period capacity buffer is operationally necessary.

At the same time, excessive buffer capacity raises cost. The clinic should identify the least-cost point on the trade-off between:

- Patient waiting
- Staff idle time
- Overtime
- Throughput
- Safety and quality

### 4.3 Staff workload and well-being

Staggered schedules, cross-training, and flex assignments may:

- Disrupt preferred work patterns
- Reduce break flexibility
- Increase cognitive load
- Create role ambiguity
- Cause burnout if implemented as permanent peak pressure

Staff workload, overtime, break compliance, and satisfaction should be balancing measures. Front-line staff should participate in process redesign.

### 4.4 Patient choice, access, and equity

Appointment smoothing may reduce the number of preferred appointment times. Online pre-registration and electronic check-out may disadvantage patients with limited digital access, language barriers, or disabilities.

Mitigations include:

- Assisted digital options
- Telephone alternatives
- In-person pathways
- Monitoring adoption and wait improvement by patient group

Fast-track pathways should be based on clinical and operational criteria, not on the ability to navigate technology.

### 4.5 Urgent versus routine patients

Protected urgent capacity may sometimes remain unused, while urgent insertions can lengthen routine queues. The clinic should use historical urgent-arrival distributions to choose the reserve level and allow safe release of unused capacity near the service time.

Urgent wait standards should generally be treated as hard constraints rather than traded against average performance.

### 4.6 Quality and clinical thoroughness

Reducing service times must not encourage:

- Incomplete assessments
- Premature consultation closure
- Unnecessary pre-ordering of tests
- Fragmentation of care
- Increased diagnostic errors or revisits

The focus should be on non-value-adding time, preventable rework, information delays, and better coordination—not indiscriminate shortening of clinical encounters.

### 4.7 Balancing objectives

A formal multi-objective optimization can minimize:

\[
\alpha(\text{total patient waiting})
+\beta(\text{tail/SLA penalties})
+\gamma(\text{labor and overtime cost})
+\delta(\text{quality or safety penalties})
\]

subject to constraints such as:

- Urgent-patient wait limits
- Minimum staffing and break rules
- Required competencies
- Room and equipment limits
- No deterioration in quality indicators
- Maximum budget increase

Management can then compare a Pareto frontier of options rather than assume there is one universally optimal configuration. Low-cost changes—schedule shifts, pooling, pre-registration, and handover improvements—should generally be tested before adding permanent capacity.

## 5. Measuring Success

### 5.1 Primary flow KPIs

| KPI | Definition |
|---|---|
| End-to-end visit duration | Physical arrival to departure/check-out completion |
| Observed visit duration | Registration start to final completion if arrival is unavailable |
| Total waiting time per visit | Sum of valid queue intervals, adjusted for parallelism |
| Stage-specific waiting time | Readiness for activity to activity start |
| Median and 90th/95th percentile waits | Typical and tail performance |
| SLA compliance | Percentage starting within the stage-specific target |
| Excess-waiting burden | Patient-minutes above queue thresholds |
| Flow efficiency | Patient-facing service time divided by total visit duration |
| Throughput | Completed visits per clinic session or day |
| Work in process | Number of patients currently in the clinic or active process |

For parallel pathways, total waiting time should not be calculated by blindly summing overlapping intervals. End-to-end elapsed time and the union of patient-facing service intervals provide a safer basis.

### 5.2 Operational and resource KPIs

- Mean and peak queue length by station
- Percentage of time queue length exceeds a threshold
- Resource utilization by time of day
- Overtime and late clinic completion
- Room and equipment utilization
- Service-time median, 90th percentile, and variability
- Number of handovers
- Rework and repeat-test rate
- On-time clinic start
- Appointment start delay
- No-show, cancellation, and abandonment rates
- Cost per completed visit
- Labor hours per visit

### 5.3 Quality and balancing measures

Wait reduction should be accepted only if balancing measures remain stable or improve:

- Unplanned return visits
- Diagnostic or medication errors
- Repeat tests caused by process failure
- Adverse events or urgent escalations
- Clinical outcome indicators appropriate to each specialty
- Patient complaints
- Patient satisfaction and perceived waiting
- Staff satisfaction and burnout
- Consultation duration for clinically complex cases
- Equity of waiting times across patient groups

Quality outcomes and survey data will usually have to be joined to the process event log.

### 5.4 Continuous process monitoring

The clinic should establish an automated monitoring pipeline using the same start/complete event structure, enhanced with:

- Physical arrival and departure
- Scheduled appointment time
- Order and readiness timestamps
- Results-available timestamps
- Staff rosters and breaks
- Room and equipment availability
- Cancellation and abandonment events
- Unique activity-instance identifiers

A monitoring dashboard should show:

- Current and historical queue lengths
- Mean, median, and percentile waits
- SLA breaches
- Bottlenecks by time and specialty
- Resource utilization
- Process variants
- Patient-group comparisons

Statistical process-control charts can distinguish sustained improvement from random daily variation. Alerts should be based on persistent threshold breaches rather than isolated events.

### 5.5 Evaluation design

Before implementation:

1. Establish a six-month baseline by specialty, patient type, urgency, day, and time.
2. Define target KPIs and balancing measures.
3. Validate a discrete-event simulation against observed performance.
4. Select a clinic, specialty, or time block for a controlled pilot.

After implementation:

- Compare with matched historical periods or unaffected clinics
- Adjust for case mix, seasonality, holidays, and volume
- Use interrupted time-series or difference-in-differences analysis where feasible
- Report confidence intervals, not only point estimates
- Monitor whether a bottleneck has shifted downstream
- Recalibrate the simulation using post-change data

### 5.6 Data-quality governance

Sustained queue mining requires routine checks for:

- Missing starts or completions
- Incorrectly matched lifecycle events
- Duplicate records
- Negative durations
- Implausible overlaps
- Clock or time-zone inconsistencies
- Changed activity names or timestamp definitions
- Unlogged work
- Incomplete or abandoned cases

Timestamp meanings should be operationally standardized and preferably captured automatically to reduce manual entry and KPI gaming.

---

The overall approach is to separate service from waiting, reconstruct queue entry and queue length, prioritize queues by both burden and risk, test root-cause hypotheses using process and resource evidence, and evaluate interventions in an end-to-end simulation before piloting them. This makes it possible to reduce patient waiting through better scheduling, coordination, and flow design while adding capacity only where the data demonstrates a genuine and persistent shortfall.
