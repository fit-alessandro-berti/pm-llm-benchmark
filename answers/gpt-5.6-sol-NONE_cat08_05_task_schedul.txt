# 1. Analyze historical scheduling performance and dynamics

## 1.1 Build a process-mining-ready event model

The MES data should first be converted into an event model that supports both **job-centric flow analysis** and **resource-centric sequencing analysis**. A single flat case table is insufficient because machine failures, operators, setups, and jobs interact in many-to-many relationships. An object-centric event log is preferable.

### Core objects

- **Job/order:** job ID, release time, due date, priority, customer, product family, quantity.
- **Operation:** operation ID, job ID, routing position, work center, eligible machines, predecessor relationships.
- **Machine:** machine ID, capabilities, calendar, availability state.
- **Operator:** operator ID, skills, shift/calendar.
- **Setup:** machine, preceding job/family, succeeding job/family, start/end.
- **Disruption:** breakdown, maintenance, material shortage, rework, priority change.

### Event lifecycle

For every operation, reconstruct:

1. Operation enabled by completion of predecessors
2. Queue entry
3. Setup start/end
4. Processing start/end
5. Suspension/resumption, if applicable
6. Inspection/rework
7. Transfer to the next operation

Resource events should include:

- Shift start/end
- Available/idle
- Breakdown start/end
- Planned maintenance
- Starved
- Blocked
- Operator unavailable

Timestamps must be normalized, duplicates resolved, missing lifecycle events repaired only under explicit rules, and machine/operator calendars applied. Planned and actual fields must remain separate.

A critical distinction is:

\[
\text{Operation readiness} \neq \text{queue entry} \neq \text{processing start}
\]

Without this distinction, delays caused by transport, material, release policies, and resource contention will be incorrectly combined.

---

## 1.2 Reconstruct actual job and machine flows

### Job-centric mining

Use:

- Directly-Follows Graphs
- Performance-annotated process maps
- Petri nets or process trees
- Inductive Miner for readable discovered models
- Heuristics Miner where logs are noisy
- Token-based replay and alignments for conformance analysis
- Object-centric process mining for interactions among jobs, machines, and operators

These reveal actual routings, loops, skipped operations, rework, out-of-order execution, and unauthorized routing substitutions.

Variant analysis should group cases by factors such as:

- Product or routing family
- On-time versus late completion
- Priority
- Complexity
- Rework status
- Breakdown exposure
- Machine assignment

### Resource-centric mining

Sort setup and processing events chronologically for each machine. This reconstructs the actual machine sequence:

\[
J_{m,1} \rightarrow J_{m,2} \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow J_{m,n}
\]

This view is necessary for detecting sequence-dependent setups, dispatching behavior, queue-jumping, starvation, and machine contention. A job-centric trace alone does not reveal the complete sequence on a machine.

A resource-interaction or handover network can also expose poor synchronization between upstream and downstream centers.

---

## 1.3 Performance metrics

### Flow time, lead time, and makespan

Definitions must be standardized because “lead time” is often used ambiguously.

For job \(j\):

\[
\text{Shop flow time}_j=C_j-R_j
\]

where \(R_j\) is release time and \(C_j\) is completion time.

If order acceptance time is available:

\[
\text{Customer lead time}_j=C_j-A_j
\]

Job makespan is the elapsed time from its first operation start to its final operation completion:

\[
M_j=\max_k(C_{jk})-\min_k(S_{jk})
\]

Also calculate:

- Total touch time
- Total setup time
- Total queue time
- Transport/hold time
- Rework time
- Flow efficiency:

\[
\text{Flow efficiency}_j =
\frac{\text{productive processing time}_j}
{\text{elapsed shop flow time}_j}
\]

Report distributions rather than only averages: median, P75, P90, P95, variance, coefficient of variation, and tails by routing, priority, and product family. High-mix operations often have heavily skewed distributions.

### Queue and waiting time

For operation \(o\):

\[
W_o=S_o-Q_o
\]

where \(Q_o\) is queue-entry time and \(S_o\) is setup or processing start.

When predecessor completion is available, decompose it further:

\[
\text{Pre-queue delay}=Q_o-E_o
\]

\[
\text{Resource queue delay}=S_o-Q_o
\]

where \(E_o\) is the time the operation became technically enabled.

For each machine/work center, measure:

- Mean and percentile queue time
- Queue length over time
- Queue workload in hours, not just job counts
- Age of oldest queued job
- Frequency and duration of blocking/starvation
- Waiting time by priority, shift, product family, and load band
- Share of lead time spent waiting

Time-weighted WIP should be reconstructed from release and completion events. Little’s Law provides a consistency check:

\[
WIP \approx Throughput \times FlowTime
\]

Large deviations can indicate data quality or boundary problems.

### Machine and operator utilization

For machine \(m\) during available calendar time \(T_m\):

\[
U_m^{prod}=\frac{T_m^{processing}}{T_m}
\]

\[
U_m^{setup}=\frac{T_m^{setup}}{T_m}
\]

\[
U_m^{idle}=\frac{T_m^{available}-T_m^{processing}-T_m^{setup}}{T_m}
\]

Distinguish:

- Productive processing
- Setup
- Planned maintenance
- Breakdown
- Idle while no job is ready—starvation
- Idle while work is queued—dispatching, operator, material, or tool delay
- Blocked because downstream capacity/storage is unavailable
- Off-shift time

Useful measures include:

- Effective utilization
- OEE components, where quality counts are available
- Mean time between failures
- Mean time to repair
- Operator-machine overlap and skill shortages
- Utilization by hour, shift, and demand state

High utilization at a bottleneck is not automatically desirable. Utilization approaching 100% can create nonlinear growth in queues and lead-time variability.

### Sequence-dependent setup analysis

For each machine sequence, derive the predecessor-to-successor feature transition:

\[
x_{i} \rightarrow x_{j}
\]

Features might include:

- Material family
- Geometry
- Tool package
- Fixture
- Diameter range
- Tolerance class
- Heat-treatment recipe
- Cleanliness requirement
- Color/coating

Estimate:

\[
\hat{s}_{ijm}=E[S \mid \text{from family }i,\text{ to family }j,\text{ machine }m]
\]

Produce setup matrices by machine and family transition, with counts, medians, P80/P90 values, variance, operator effects, and shift effects. Sparse transitions should be modeled with hierarchical or regularized regression rather than unreliable cell means.

A predictive model can take the form:

\[
S=f(m,\text{prior attributes},\text{next attributes},
\text{operator},\text{shift},\text{elapsed idle time})
\]

Compare actual sequencing against a feasible low-setup sequence to quantify avoidable setup:

\[
\text{Avoidable setup}=
S_{\text{actual}}-S_{\text{best feasible benchmark}}
\]

The benchmark must respect due dates, priorities, machine eligibility, and precedence constraints; otherwise it will overstate savings.

### Schedule adherence and tardiness

For job \(j\) with due date \(d_j\):

\[
L_j=C_j-d_j
\]

\[
T_j=\max(0,C_j-d_j)
\]

\[
E_j=\max(0,d_j-C_j)
\]

Measure:

- On-time delivery rate
- Mean and P90 tardiness
- Maximum tardiness
- Weighted tardiness:

\[
\sum_j w_jT_j
\]

- Percentage of high-priority jobs late
- Schedule attainment by shift/day
- Start-time and completion-time deviations from plan
- Sequence adherence on critical machines
- Number of reschedules or queue jumps
- Plan stability, such as changes within frozen horizons

Due dates should be translated to exact contractual timestamps and calendars. Measuring against midnight dates can create misleading results.

### Impact of disruptions

Link jobs to disruptions through temporal and resource overlap. For breakdown \(b\), identify:

1. Jobs processing on the failed machine
2. Jobs queued for it
3. Downstream jobs indirectly delayed
4. Jobs displaced by rescheduling
5. Additional setups caused by sequence changes

Use:

- Before/after analysis
- Matched cohorts
- Difference-in-differences where appropriate
- Survival analysis for completion risk
- Event studies around breakdowns and priority changes
- Counterfactual simulation

Metrics include incremental queue time, tardiness, WIP, setup time, rescheduling frequency, and time to recover. Priority changes should be assessed for both the hot job’s gain and the delay imposed on other jobs.

# 2. Diagnose scheduling pathologies

## 2.1 Bottlenecks and shifting constraints

A bottleneck is not merely the machine with the highest average utilization. Evidence should combine:

- Persistent queue workload
- High wait-to-process ratio
- High effective utilization
- Low capacity cushion
- Tardiness sensitivity to its delay
- Throughput response to additional capacity
- Frequency of being on jobs’ critical paths

Process-mined waiting networks and queue heat maps can reveal both permanent and shifting bottlenecks.

For each late job, compute the critical chain of actual waiting and processing intervals. Aggregate the machines responsible for the largest shares of lateness. This converts “MILL-03 looks busy” into evidence such as “MILL-03 contributed 38% of the critical-path delay among late jobs.”

## 2.2 Poor prioritization

Reconstruct each dispatch decision: at every machine release time, identify the set of jobs eligible and waiting, then determine which job was selected.

This allows calculation of:

- Frequency with which a lower-priority job was chosen over an urgent job
- Frequency with which a near-due job was bypassed
- Slack at dispatch:

\[
Slack_j=d_j-t-\widehat{RPT}_j
\]

- Critical ratio:

\[
CR_j=\frac{d_j-t}{\widehat{RPT}_j}
\]

- Tardiness attributable to repeated bypassing
- Fairness and aging behavior

The chosen sequence can be compared with EDD, weighted shortest processing time, apparent tardiness cost, and simulation-derived policies.

## 2.3 Excess setup caused by poor sequencing

Flag transitions where:

- The actual setup is substantially above the machine/family median
- A compatible job was waiting but not selected
- A family was split into multiple small runs
- Hot-job insertion caused multiple additional changeovers

Quantify the trade-off between setup savings and due-date risk. Excessive batching can reduce setup time while increasing WIP and tardiness, so the objective should not be setup minimization alone.

## 2.4 Downstream starvation and upstream overproduction

Correlate upstream completion bursts with:

- Downstream queue growth
- Idle/starved intervals
- Blocked intervals
- WIP accumulation between specific centers

Process mining can identify repeated patterns such as:

\[
\text{Large cutting batch}
\rightarrow \text{Milling queue surge}
\rightarrow \text{Grinding starvation}
\]

Resource calendars and token-replay waiting times help distinguish genuine starvation from off-shift or operator-unavailability effects.

## 2.5 WIP oscillation and “bullwhip-like” behavior

Construct time series for:

- Released WIP
- WIP by routing stage
- Queue workload
- Throughput
- Release rates
- Completion rates
- Schedule changes

Use autocorrelation, spectral analysis, variance amplification, and cross-correlation to identify cyclical surges. For example, aggressive releases after a low-output day may overload bottlenecks several days later.

## 2.6 Variant and contention analysis

Compare on-time and late variants while controlling for job complexity and routing:

- Median waiting at each stage
- Number of queue bypasses
- Breakdown exposure
- Setup-transition quality
- Rework frequency
- Machine substitutions
- Priority changes
- Release-to-due-date allowance

Resource contention episodes can be identified when queue workload exceeds a threshold. The outcomes of different dispatch decisions during statistically similar contention episodes can then be compared.

# 3. Root-cause analysis

## 3.1 Likely causes

### Static local dispatching in a dynamic system

FCFS and EDD optimize only local order. They ignore:

- Remaining routing
- Downstream congestion
- Alternative machines
- setup consequences
- bottleneck protection
- stochastic durations
- breakdown risk
- system-wide tardiness

A choice that appears locally reasonable may create a downstream overload or waste a favorable setup state.

### Weak real-time visibility

If dispatchers do not see actual queues, remaining processing times, machine condition, operator availability, and downstream capacity, decisions are based on stale assumptions. Manual priority changes then become reactive and destabilizing.

### Inaccurate planning parameters

Systematic differences between planned and actual duration lead to infeasible schedules and unreliable promises. Analyze residuals:

\[
\epsilon_o=p_o^{actual}-p_o^{planned}
\]

Stratify by machine, operator, product family, shift, quantity, rework, and setup class. Bias suggests parameter problems; high conditional variance suggests inherent uncertainty.

### Ignoring sequence-dependent setup

A single standard setup value hides transition asymmetry. Changing from family A to B may require 15 minutes, while B to A requires 60 minutes. Ignoring this can invalidate machine sequences and capacity estimates.

### Poor coordination between centers

Local utilization targets encourage upstream machines to keep producing even when downstream queues are excessive. This increases WIP without increasing system throughput.

### Inadequate disruption response

Priority changes and breakdowns may trigger full manual resequencing, unstable queues, and cascading setups. No frozen horizon or explicit recovery objective means every disruption propagates broadly.

---

## 3.2 Distinguish scheduling problems from capacity and variability

Process mining alone establishes strong associations; combining it with queueing analysis, causal methods, optimization, and simulation provides better attribution.

### Indicators of poor scheduling logic

- Similar jobs under similar load have very different outcomes depending on selected sequence.
- A feasible alternative sequence would have reduced weighted tardiness or setups.
- Late jobs were repeatedly bypassed despite sufficient remaining capacity.
- Machines idle while eligible work existed.
- Large setup losses occur despite compatible queued jobs.
- Avoidable downstream overload follows upstream release decisions.

### Indicators of capacity shortage

- Demand workload persistently exceeds effective available capacity.
- The bottleneck remains saturated even under near-optimal sequencing.
- Queue growth persists across all reasonable dispatch policies.
- Counterfactual optimization cannot produce a feasible on-time schedule.
- Marginal capacity at one resource materially improves throughput and tardiness.

### Indicators of inherent variability

- Conditional process-time or repair-time distributions remain broad after observable factors are included.
- Tail events dominate tardiness even under good schedules.
- Outcomes vary significantly under stochastic replications with identical policies.

### Recommended attribution approach

1. Fit predictive duration, setup, and failure models.
2. Reconstruct historical system state at each decision point.
3. Replay the actual policy.
4. Run counterfactual policies using the same arrivals and disruption realizations.
5. Add capacity in separate experiments.
6. Reduce variability in separate experiments.
7. Decompose KPI gains into scheduling, capacity, and variability effects.

# 4. Advanced data-driven scheduling strategies

The strategies are complementary rather than mutually exclusive.

## Strategy 1: Dynamic multi-attribute dispatching

### Core logic

At each dispatch opportunity, score every eligible job-machine assignment:

\[
Score(j,m,t)=
\alpha_1 Urgency_j+
\alpha_2 Priority_j+
\alpha_3 Aging_j+
\alpha_4 BottleneckProtection_j
-\alpha_5 Setup_{prev,j,m}
-\alpha_6 DownstreamCongestion_j
-\alpha_7 DurationRisk_j
\]

Possible components include:

- Slack or critical ratio
- Remaining processing time
- Customer/priority weight
- Queue age to prevent starvation
- Estimated sequence-dependent setup
- Downstream workload
- Probability of tardiness
- Alternative-resource flexibility
- Whether the operation feeds or consumes the bottleneck

A practical urgency term is an apparent-tardiness-cost style function:

\[
Urgency_j=
\frac{w_j}{p_{jm}}
\exp\left(
-\frac{\max(0,d_j-t-\widehat{RPT}_j)}
{k\bar p}
\right)
\]

The score can be extended with setup-aware and downstream-load terms.

### Use of process-mining evidence

- Waiting-time decomposition sets urgency weights.
- Historical dispatch/outcome data identify harmful queue-jumping patterns.
- Setup transition models estimate changeover cost.
- Bottleneck analysis identifies resources requiring special treatment.
- Variant analysis estimates the characteristics of late jobs.
- Simulation or offline policy learning tunes weights.

### Pathologies addressed

- Local myopia
- Poor priority treatment
- Long queues at constraints
- Downstream congestion
- Excessive changeovers
- Starvation of older jobs

### Expected impact

- Lower weighted tardiness and fewer critically late jobs
- Better on-time delivery
- Lower queue time and WIP
- Reduced setup burden
- More balanced utilization

### Safeguards

Use hard constraints for machine eligibility, precedence, operator skills, and maintenance. Add an aging term, frozen intervals, and explainable reason codes so that the policy is operationally acceptable.

---

## Strategy 2: Predictive, rolling-horizon robust scheduling

### Core logic

Every defined interval—or after a significant event—re-optimize a finite horizon using the latest shop state.

The optimization model may be a:

- Flexible job-shop mixed-integer program
- Constraint-programming model
- CP-SAT model
- Decomposition or large-neighborhood-search heuristic
- Digital-twin scheduler for very large instances

It should include:

- Operation precedence
- Alternative machines
- Machine and operator calendars
- Sequence-dependent setups
- Release and due dates
- Non-preemption or allowable interruption
- Maintenance windows
- Queue/WIP constraints
- Frozen near-term commitments

A multi-objective function could be:

\[
\min \left[
\lambda_1\sum_j w_jT_j+
\lambda_2\sum s_{ijm}+
\lambda_3 WIP+
\lambda_4\text{schedule instability}+
\lambda_5\text{overtime}
\right]
\]

### Predictive inputs

Use process-mined historical data to estimate conditional distributions for:

- Processing duration
- Setup duration
- Transport time
- Rework probability
- Machine failure hazard
- Repair duration
- Operator availability

For example:

\[
P_{o}\sim F(\text{machine},\text{family},\text{quantity},
\text{operator},\text{shift},\text{complexity})
\]

Predict completion-time distributions rather than a single point estimate. Use quantiles or scenario-based stochastic optimization for high-priority jobs.

Predictive maintenance models can reserve capacity when failure hazard is high and schedule maintenance during lower-impact periods.

### Rescheduling policy

Trigger rescheduling when:

- A bottleneck breaks down
- A duration overrun exceeds a threshold
- A hot job arrives
- Predicted tardiness crosses a risk threshold
- Queue workload departs materially from plan

Preserve a frozen horizon to prevent “schedule nervousness.” Re-optimize only affected machines and jobs where possible.

### Pathologies addressed

- Unrealistic deterministic plans
- Weak response to disruptions
- Uncoordinated work centers
- Capacity overload discovered too late
- Unreliable delivery promises

### Expected impact

- Lower tardiness tail and more reliable lead times
- Earlier detection of infeasible due dates
- Reduced emergency expediting and overtime
- Better machine and operator coordination
- Better recovery after breakdowns

---

## Strategy 3: Setup-aware family batching and optimized sequencing

### Core logic

At setup-intensive or bottleneck machines:

1. Classify queued operations into setup-compatible families.
2. Use the learned setup transition matrix.
3. Create limited “campaigns” or families of similar work.
4. Optimize family sequence and within-family job sequence.
5. Apply due-date and maximum-wait constraints.

A machine-level optimization may minimize:

\[
\min\left[
\beta_1\sum_j w_jT_j+
\beta_2\sum_{(i,j)}s_{ijm}+
\beta_3\sum_j Waiting_j
\right]
\]

This resembles an asymmetric traveling-salesperson problem with release dates and tardiness penalties, solved using CP-SAT, dynamic programming for small queues, or heuristics for large queues.

### Use of process-mining evidence

- Historical transition matrices identify favorable and costly changes.
- Actual-versus-benchmark comparisons estimate setup-reduction potential.
- Due-date analysis establishes maximum safe batch windows.
- Queue patterns identify machines where batching is beneficial.
- Setup variance indicates where conservative buffers are needed.

### Pathologies addressed

- Repeated family switching
- Excess setup at bottlenecks
- Fragmented campaigns
- Capacity loss caused by hot-job insertions

### Expected impact

- Lower total setup time
- Higher effective bottleneck capacity and throughput
- Less WIP upstream of the bottleneck
- Lower tardiness if capacity gains exceed batching delays

### Safeguards

Use dynamic family closure conditions:

- Stop waiting for a batch when slack becomes critical.
- Limit maximum batch size and maximum job age.
- Reserve insertion slots for urgent jobs.
- Evaluate the setup savings against the tardiness penalty.

---

## Strategy 4: Bottleneck-controlled release and WIP regulation

This strategy directly addresses high WIP and unstable lead time.

### Core logic

Use a CONWIP, workload-control, or drum-buffer-rope mechanism:

- Maintain a target workload before the current constraint.
- Release new jobs only when workload capacity becomes available.
- Prioritize release based on due-date feasibility and routing.
- Protect the bottleneck with a time/work buffer.
- Avoid releasing work that will only wait upstream.

A release criterion can use normalized workload:

\[
WL_m=\sum_{j\in released}
\frac{\widehat p_{jm}}{\text{effective capacity}_m}
\]

Release a job only if it does not violate workload norms at critical centers.

### Use of process-mining evidence

- WIP-flow-time curves establish appropriate WIP caps.
- Bottleneck maps identify protected resources.
- Historical starvation episodes size buffers.
- Routing and duration distributions estimate workload contributions.
- Lead-time distributions calibrate release timing.

### Expected impact

- Lower WIP and more stable lead times
- Less congestion and fewer priority interventions
- Higher flow efficiency
- Potentially lower utilization at non-bottlenecks without reducing throughput

---

## Strategy 5: Learning-assisted dispatching under strict constraints

As a later maturity step, contextual bandits or reinforcement learning can choose among approved dispatch actions using the digital twin.

State variables could include:

- Queue workloads
- Machine setup states
- Remaining job slack
- Breakdown risk
- Downstream congestion
- WIP and bottleneck buffers

The reward would penalize weighted tardiness, setups, WIP, and instability. Training should occur in simulation, with hard feasibility constraints and comparison against transparent heuristics. This should not be the first deployment due to explainability and safety concerns.

# 5. Simulation, evaluation, and continuous improvement

## 5.1 Build a process-mining-calibrated digital twin

A discrete-event simulation should represent:

- Actual routing variants and precedence networks
- Alternative machine eligibility
- Machine/operator calendars
- Conditional processing-time distributions
- Sequence-dependent setup models
- Transport and queue delays
- Rework and scrap loops
- Breakdowns and repair distributions
- Preventive maintenance
- Job arrivals, due dates, and priorities
- Hot-job arrivals and priority changes
- Dispatch/release/rescheduling logic

Use fitted distributions or empirical resampling. Preserve correlations—for example, difficult jobs may have both long processing times and high rework probability. Independent sampling could understate risk.

Validate the model against held-out historical periods using:

- Throughput
- WIP distribution
- Queue-time distributions
- Utilization and setup shares
- On-time delivery
- Tardiness distribution
- Breakdown recovery profiles
- Machine-level sequence patterns

The baseline simulation must reproduce current performance sufficiently well before evaluating alternatives.

---

## 5.2 Experimental scenarios

Evaluate baseline FCFS/EDD and each proposed strategy under common random numbers and many replications.

Test at least:

1. **Normal demand and current variability**
2. **High load:** 10%, 20%, and 30% demand increases
3. **Demand-mix shift:** more jobs requiring bottleneck operations
4. **Frequent failures:** shorter MTBF or longer MTTR
5. **Hot-job surge:** increased urgent-order frequency
6. **Duration uncertainty:** heavier processing-time tails
7. **Operator shortage:** reduced skill availability
8. **Maintenance outage at a critical machine**
9. **Setup-intensive mix**
10. **Due-date compression**
11. **Alternative capacity scenarios:** overtime, extra shift, or added machine
12. **Combined stress scenario**

Include sensitivity analysis for dispatch weights, WIP caps, batch windows, buffer sizes, and rescheduling frequency.

---

## 5.3 Evaluation criteria

Compare:

- On-time delivery rate
- Mean, P90, and maximum tardiness
- Weighted tardiness
- Mean and P90 lead time
- Lead-time variance
- WIP and queue workload
- Throughput
- Productive, setup, idle, blocked, and starved time
- Number of setups and setup hours
- Overtime and operating cost
- Hot-job response time
- Schedule stability
- Recovery time after disruptions
- Fairness across priority/customer classes

Report confidence intervals and paired differences, not just point estimates. Pareto fronts should show trade-offs—for example, setup reduction versus tardiness, or utilization versus lead-time stability.

A strategy should also be tested for robustness to prediction errors. A policy that is excellent only with perfect forecasts is unsuitable.

---

## 5.4 Deployment approach

Use phased implementation:

1. **Shadow mode:** generate recommendations without controlling machines.
2. **Dispatcher review:** compare recommendation and actual choice.
3. **Pilot:** one bottleneck family or work center.
4. **Human-in-the-loop execution:** operators can override with reason codes.
5. **Controlled expansion:** extend after KPI improvement is verified.
6. **Closed-loop integration:** feed MES state into the scheduler and return dispatch lists.

Capture every recommendation, action, override, and resulting outcome. This creates a decision log for policy evaluation and user trust.

---

## 5.5 Continuous monitoring and adaptation

### Operational dashboards

Monitor at job, machine, and system levels:

- Current and predicted tardiness risk
- Queue workload and WIP
- Bottleneck utilization
- Setup share
- Breakdown status
- Schedule adherence
- Jobs approaching critical slack
- Actual versus predicted completion distributions

### Continuous process mining

Regularly refresh:

- Discovered process models
- Routing variants
- Conformance deviations
- Bottleneck and waiting-time maps
- Setup-transition matrices
- Dispatch-decision patterns
- On-time versus late case comparisons

### Drift detection

Detect:

- **Data drift:** product mix, routing, operator, or machine-use changes
- **Concept drift:** changed relationship between job features and duration
- **Performance drift:** worsening tardiness, queue time, or setup share
- **Process drift:** new routing variants or rework loops
- **Resource drift:** declining reliability or capability

Methods may include control charts, CUSUM, Page-Hinkley tests, population stability indexes, residual monitoring, and change-point detection.

### Automatic response

When drift exceeds thresholds:

1. Validate data quality.
2. Re-estimate processing, setup, and failure models.
3. Recalibrate dispatch weights or WIP caps in simulation.
4. Compare the updated policy against the incumbent.
5. Deploy only if improvement is statistically and operationally credible.
6. Retain rollback capability and model/version governance.

---

# Recommended integrated operating model

The strongest practical design is a layered system:

- **Process mining** provides empirical process models, parameters, bottleneck evidence, and conformance monitoring.
- **Workload-controlled release** limits WIP.
- **Rolling-horizon optimization** coordinates work centers and predicts due-date risk.
- **Dynamic dispatching** handles real-time local decisions.
- **Setup-aware sequencing** protects setup-intensive bottlenecks.
- **Discrete-event simulation** tests policies and capacity decisions.
- **Continuous mining and drift detection** sustain performance.

This architecture addresses both planning and execution. It avoids the common mistake of replacing FCFS with one more static rule while leaving inaccurate durations, excessive releases, sequence-dependent setups, and disruption handling unresolved. The target is not maximum utilization everywhere; it is reliable flow, protected bottleneck throughput, controlled WIP, and minimum weighted tardiness under realistic uncertainty.
