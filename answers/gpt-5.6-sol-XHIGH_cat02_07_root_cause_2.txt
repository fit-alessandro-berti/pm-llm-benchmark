## 1. Cases with extended lead times

Lead time is measured from **Submit Claim** to **Close Claim** using elapsed calendar time.

| Case | Region | Complexity | Document Requests | Lead Time | Main delay |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 2004 | B | Low | 0 | **1h 25m** | None; straight-through |
| 2001 | A | Low | 0 | **1h 30m** | None; straight-through |
| 2002 | B | Medium | 1 | **25h 55m** | Evaluate-to-approve phase: 24h 15m |
| 2003 | A | High | 2 | **48h 20m** | Document cycle plus 17h approval-to-payment wait |
| 2005 | B | High | 3 | **77h 05m** | Repeated document cycle; evaluate-to-approve took 72h 10m |

Thus:

- **Case 2005 is the most severe performance issue.**
- **Case 2003 is also significantly delayed.**
- **Case 2002 is moderately delayed** compared with the low-complexity baseline.
- Cases **2001 and 2004** are normal, fast, straight-through cases.

The initial evaluation and final closing steps are generally fast across all cases. Most delay occurs between **Evaluate Claim** and **Approve Claim**, especially when additional documents are requested.

## 2. Attribute and root-cause analysis

### A. Complexity is the strongest explanatory attribute

| Complexity | Cases | Average Lead Time | Average Document Requests |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Low | 2001, 2004 | **1h 27m 30s** | 0 |
| Medium | 2002 | **25h 55m** | 1 |
| High | 2003, 2005 | **62h 42m 30s** | 2.5 |

There is a clear monotonic pattern:

- Low complexity: no document requests and completion in about 1.5 hours.
- Medium complexity: one request and completion the next day.
- High complexity: multiple requests and completion after two to three days.

The repeated **Request Additional Documents** activities appear to be the primary mechanism behind the long lead times:

- Case 2003 had two requests.
- Case 2005 had three requests.
- Case 2005 spent **70h 30m from its first document request to approval**.

Likely explanations include:

- Incomplete documentation at submission.
- Missing items being identified incrementally rather than in one consolidated review.
- More coverage, fraud, medical, legal, or valuation checks for high-complexity claims.
- Slow customer or external-party responses.
- Internal queues after documents are supplied.

The log records document requests but not document receipts, so it is not possible to determine whether the waiting time is mainly attributable to the customer, an external provider, or internal processing.

### B. Region B is associated with the worst case, but the evidence is confounded

The crude average lead times are:

- Region A: **24h 55m**
- Region B: approximately **34h 48m**

However, this does not establish Region B as a general root cause:

- Low-complexity cases are nearly identical:
  - Region A, Case 2001: 1h 30m
  - Region B, Case 2004: 1h 25m
- Both regions have a long high-complexity case.
- The Region B high-complexity case, 2005, is **28h 45m slower** than Region A’s high-complexity Case 2003.

This suggests a possible Region B issue specifically for difficult claims, such as:

- Lower specialist capacity.
- Slower local document channels.
- Additional regional regulatory requirements.
- Longer queues or handoffs.
- More fragmented customer communication.

Nevertheless, there is only one high-complexity case per region, so this remains a hypothesis rather than a proven regional effect.

### C. Resource effects are visible but cannot be separated from region and complexity

#### Adjusters

- **Adjuster_Mike** handled Cases 2001 and 2003 in Region A.
- **Adjuster_Lisa** handled Cases 2002, 2004, and 2005 in Region B.

Lisa is associated with the longest case, but she also handled Case 2004, the fastest case in the log. Mike similarly handled one fast low-complexity case and one slow high-complexity case.

Therefore, the data does not support a general conclusion that Lisa is slower. The adjuster assignment is confounded with both region and complexity. Still, the three requests in Case 2005 could indicate:

- Piecemeal identification of missing documents.
- A particularly difficult claim.
- Higher workload or limited availability.
- Different documentation practices in Region B.

#### Managers

Manager_Bill approved both high-complexity cases, while Manager_Ann handled the low- and medium-complexity cases. This looks like an association between Bill and long duration, but it is probably a **selection effect**: high-complexity claims appear to be routed to Bill.

The elapsed time from the final document request to approval was:

- Case 2002, Manager_Ann: 20h
- Case 2003, Manager_Bill: 23h
- Case 2005, Manager_Bill: 19h

These values are similar, providing no clear evidence that Manager_Bill is the root cause.

#### Finance resources

Case 2003 had a notable **17-hour approval-to-payment delay**:

- Approved at 16:00
- Paid at 09:00 the next day

This may indicate a payment batch cutoff or overnight scheduling issue rather than a Finance_Alan performance problem, because Finance_Alan paid Case 2001 in only 15 minutes.

Case 2005 had a four-hour approval-to-payment delay, but its main bottleneck was still the document cycle.

#### CSR resources

Payment-to-close time is only 15–30 minutes in every case. Therefore, CSR resources do not appear to be causing the performance problems.

---

## 3. Most likely root causes

In order of evidential strength:

1. **High claim complexity**
   - Strongly correlated with longer durations and more document requests.

2. **Repeated document-request loops**
   - The clearest direct process bottleneck.
   - Especially severe in Case 2005.

3. **Possible Region B or Adjuster_Lisa interaction for high-complexity work**
   - Case 2005 is much slower than the Region A high-complexity case.
   - Not enough data to distinguish region, resource workload, or claim-specific differences.

4. **Payment scheduling or cutoff delay**
   - Contributes materially to Case 2003’s duration.

5. **Manager assignment is unlikely to be the primary cause**
   - Bill receives only high-complexity claims, and final-request-to-approval times are comparable with Ann’s medium case.

## 4. Recommended mitigations

### Reduce document rework

- Introduce complexity-specific document checklists at claim submission.
- Validate mandatory documents before accepting the claim.
- Consolidate missing-document requests into one complete request rather than issuing requests incrementally.
- Provide a customer upload portal with clear status and automated reminders.
- Escalate cases after a second document request.

### Improve handling of high-complexity claims

- Route high-complexity claims to a dedicated specialist queue.
- Assign a senior adjuster early rather than after rework begins.
- Perform coverage, fraud, valuation, and documentation checks in parallel where possible.
- Set separate service-level targets for high-complexity claims.

### Investigate Region B without prematurely blaming individuals

- Compare more high-complexity cases across Regions A and B.
- Review staffing, workload, local rules, and external document turnaround in Region B.
- Examine whether Region B adjusters make more sequential document requests.
- Rebalance workloads or provide backup specialists if a capacity issue is found.

### Remove approval-to-payment scheduling delays

- Trigger payment automatically upon approval where controls permit.
- Increase payment-batch frequency.
- Review end-of-day cutoffs, particularly for approvals late in the afternoon.
- Track approval-to-payment time separately from the rest of the claim.

### Improve the event data

Add events and attributes for:

- Documents received.
- Case placed in and removed from a queue.
- Actual work start and completion.
- Resource workload at assignment.
- Reason for each additional-document request.
- Payment batch or cutoff information.

This would distinguish customer waiting time from internal processing time.

## Conclusion

The strongest finding is that **complexity drives repeated document requests, and those document loops drive the long lead times**. Cases 2003 and especially 2005 are the primary problem cases, while Case 2002 shows the same pattern at a lower severity. Region B and Adjuster_Lisa are associated with the worst outcome, but the small and confounded sample is insufficient to label either as the cause. The immediate improvement priority should be first-time-right document collection and specialized handling of high-complexity claims.
