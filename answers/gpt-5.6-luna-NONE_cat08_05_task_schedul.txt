# 1. Historical Scheduling Performance and Dynamics

## 1.1 Construct a reliable event log

The first step is to convert MES records into a process-mining event log with:

- **Case ID:** job number, such as `JOB-7001`
- **Activity:** operation, setup, queue entry, breakdown, inspection, release, completion
- **Timestamp:** preferably start and completion timestamps for each activity
- **Resource:** machine and, where relevant, operator
- **Attributes:** due date, priority, product family, routing, planned duration, actual duration, setup indicator, predecessor job, disruption type

A job-level process model should represent the routing from release to final completion. A machine-level model should separately represent the actual sequence processed by each resource, including setups, idle periods, breakdowns, and rework.

The log must first be cleaned and reconciled:

- Match task starts and ends.
- Remove duplicate or technical MES events.
- Identify missing or overlapping timestamps.
- Correct clock inconsistencies.
- Distinguish planned, scheduled, and actual times.
- Reconstruct implicit queue intervals when only completion and start events are available.
- Validate routing logic against manufacturing bills of process.

For example, the queue time before Cutting for `JOB-7001` is approximately:

\[
09{:}15{:}22 - 08{:}30{:}40 = 44.7 \text{ minutes}
\]

Its actual setup duration is:

\[
09{:}38{:}55 - 09{:}15{:}22 = 23.5 \text{ minutes}
\]

and its actual cutting duration is:

\[
10{:}45{:}30 - 09{:}39{:}05 = 66.4 \text{ minutes}
\]

These distinctions are essential because a job may appear late due to queueing, setup, processing variability, breakdowns, or upstream delays.

## 1.2 Process discovery and conformance analysis

Useful process-mining techniques include:

### Process discovery

Apply directly-follows graphs, inductive mining, or Petri-net discovery to identify:

- Actual job-routing variants.
- Unplanned rework or inspection loops.
- Skipped or repeated operations.
- Routing differences by product family.
- Frequent deviations from the standard process.

A discovered process model should be compared with the intended routing. This can reveal whether apparent scheduling problems are actually caused by routing instability, rework, or missing operations.

### Conformance checking

Compare actual traces with:

- Planned routing.
- Planned machine assignment.
- Planned start and completion times.
- Promised due dates.
- Scheduled job sequence.

Conformance metrics include:

- Alignment cost.
- Missing or extra activities.
- Resource-assignment deviations.
- Start-time deviations.
- Schedule adherence by operation and machine.

### Variant analysis

Group cases by:

- Routing variant.
- Product family.
- Priority.
- Machine path.
- Setup family.
- On-time versus late completion.
- Disruption exposure.

This helps distinguish systemic effects from isolated exceptions.

## 1.3 Flow time, lead time, and makespan

For each job \(j\), calculate:

\[
\text{Flow Time}_j = C_j - R_j
\]

where \(R_j\) is release time and \(C_j\) is final completion time.

Useful measures include:

- Mean and median flow time.
- P50, P80, P90, and P95 flow time.
- Mean and percentile queue time.
- Processing-time-to-flow-time ratio.
- Makespan by production batch, shift, product family, and bottleneck.
- Time in value-added versus non-value-added states.

For an individual operation \(o\):

\[
\text{Operation Lead Time}_{jo}
=
\text{Completion}_{jo} - \text{Queue Entry}_{jo}
\]

This can be decomposed as:

\[
\text{Operation Lead Time}
=
\text{Queue Time}
+
\text{Setup Time}
+
\text{Processing Time}
+
\text{Delay/Blocked Time}
\]

A major diagnostic metric is the **queue-to-processing ratio**. If Milling has an average processing time of 40 minutes but an average queue time of 210 minutes, the principal problem is not machining speed but flow control and capacity allocation.

---

## 1.4 Waiting and queue-time analysis

For each machine and operation, calculate:

- Average and percentile queue time.
- Maximum queue time.
- Queue time by shift and day.
- Queue time by priority.
- Queue time by product family.
- Queue time before and after disruptions.
- Queue time caused by machine unavailability.
- Queue time caused by downstream blocking.

Queue-time heat maps by hour and work center can identify recurring congestion. Directly-follows analysis can also reveal whether certain machines repeatedly feed work into already overloaded resources.

Useful measures include:

\[
\text{Queue Share}_{m}
=
\frac{\text{Total waiting time before machine }m}
{\text{Total job lead time}}
\]

and:

\[
\text{Priority Service Level}
=
\frac{\text{Jobs of priority class completed on time}}
{\text{Total jobs in that class}}
\]

These should be reported separately for urgent, high, medium, and normal jobs.

---

## 1.5 Machine and operator utilization

For each machine, divide the observed time into mutually exclusive states:

- Productive processing.
- Setup.
- Planned maintenance.
- Unplanned breakdown.
- Idle and available.
- Starved: no input available.
- Blocked: output cannot be transferred.
- Changeover waiting.
- Unknown or data-quality time.

For machine \(m\):

\[
\text{Utilization}_m
=
\frac{\text{Processing Time}_m}
{\text{Available Time}_m}
\]

Also calculate:

\[
\text{Setup Share}_m
=
\frac{\text{Setup Time}_m}
{\text{Available Time}_m}
\]

\[
\text{Availability}_m
=
\frac{\text{Available Time}_m - \text{Breakdown Time}_m}
{\text{Scheduled Time}_m}
\]

\[
\text{OEE-like Effectiveness}
=
\text{Availability}
\times
\text{Performance}
\times
\text{Quality}
\]

where quality may include first-pass yield if rework data is available.

Machine utilization should not be interpreted in isolation. A machine operating at 95% utilization may be a bottleneck, while one operating at 55% may be starved by an upstream resource. Operator utilization should likewise distinguish productive work, setup work, waiting for machines, waiting for material, and indirect activities.

---

## 1.6 Sequence-dependent setup analysis

The event log contains the predecessor job, which allows construction of machine-level sequences:

\[
J_{m,1} \rightarrow J_{m,2} \rightarrow J_{m,3}
\]

For every transition \(i \rightarrow j\) on machine \(m\), calculate:

\[
S_{m,i,j}
=
\text{Setup End}_{j}
-
\text{Setup Start}_{j}
\]

Then create a transition matrix by:

- Product family.
- Material.
- Part geometry.
- Tooling.
- Fixture.
- Surface finish.
- Heat-treatment requirement.
- Dimension or machine program.

Analyze:

- Mean setup time by transition.
- P50/P90 setup time.
- Setup-time variance.
- Frequency of transitions.
- Total setup burden by machine.
- Avoidable versus technically necessary setups.
- Difference between planned and actual setup times.

A setup penalty matrix can be used in scheduling:

\[
\hat{S}_{m,i,j}
=
f(\text{family}_i,\text{family}_j,\text{tooling}_i,\text{tooling}_j,\text{operator},\text{shift})
\]

If the current sequence frequently alternates between incompatible families, process mining will show high setup-time clusters and excessive transition costs.

---

## 1.7 Schedule adherence and tardiness

For each job:

\[
\text{Tardiness}_j
=
\max(0, C_j - D_j)
\]

where \(D_j\) is the due date.

Also calculate:

- On-time completion percentage.
- Mean tardiness.
- P90 and P95 tardiness.
- Maximum tardiness.
- Number of late jobs.
- Weighted tardiness by priority.
- Earliness, where premature completion creates WIP.
- Due-date reliability by customer and product family.
- Operation-level lateness propagation.

For operation \(o\):

\[
\text{Operation Delay}_{jo}
=
\text{Actual Completion}_{jo}
-
\text{Planned Completion}_{jo}
\]

Decompose final lateness into:

- Release delay.
- Upstream lateness.
- Queue delay.
- Setup overrun.
- Processing-time overrun.
- Breakdown delay.
- Priority interruption.
- Rework or inspection delay.

This identifies whether late jobs are primarily scheduled late or become late through execution instability.

---

## 1.8 Disruption analysis

Breakdowns, urgent orders, and priority changes should be modeled as event streams linked to affected jobs and resources.

For each breakdown, calculate:

- Jobs directly interrupted.
- Jobs indirectly delayed.
- Lost capacity.
- Queue growth before and after the event.
- Recovery time.
- Tardiness added.
- WIP added.
- Utilization shift to alternative machines.

For urgent jobs, measure:

- Number of jobs displaced.
- Time removed from incumbent jobs.
- Change in tardiness of displaced jobs.
- Whether the urgent job actually achieved its target.
- Frequency of repeated resequencing.

Event-study analysis can compare KPIs in windows before and after an event. A process graph can show disruption propagation through downstream queues. This is particularly useful for distinguishing a localized breakdown from a system-wide bottleneck.

# 2. Diagnosing Scheduling Pathologies

## 2.1 Bottleneck resources

A bottleneck is not necessarily the machine with the highest utilization; it is the resource whose constrained capacity most limits throughput.

Evidence includes:

- Sustained high utilization.
- Persistent queue accumulation.
- High downstream starvation or upstream blocking.
- Large marginal effect of downtime on tardiness.
- High share of jobs waiting for that resource.
- Frequent schedule changes involving that resource.
- Large difference between demand load and available capacity.

Estimate workload:

\[
\text{Required Load}_m
=
\sum_j
\left(
\text{Expected Processing}_{jm}
+
\text{Expected Setup}_{jm}
\right)
\]

Compare it with effective capacity after maintenance, breaks, and reliability losses.

A bottleneck impact analysis can simulate or statistically compare outcomes when the machine is available, down, or operating above a threshold. This identifies whether MILL-02, for example, is a true system constraint or merely experiencing temporary congestion.

## 2.2 Poor prioritization

Compare the actual service levels of jobs by priority and urgency:

- High-priority versus medium-priority on-time rates.
- Due-date slack at dispatch.
- Number of times jobs are bypassed.
- Time spent waiting despite being near due.
- Priority changes after the job was already late.

A job-level dispatch audit can reconstruct the available candidates whenever a machine became free and determine whether the selected job had:

- Greater or lower urgency.
- Greater or lower downstream criticality.
- Higher setup cost.
- More remaining work.
- More disruption exposure.

This can demonstrate whether local FCFS/EDD decisions cause globally poor outcomes.

## 2.3 Suboptimal sequencing

Use transition matrices and counterfactual sequence analysis to identify:

- Repeated high-cost family changes.
- Small jobs inserted between long runs requiring the same setup.
- Setup-heavy alternation at bottleneck machines.
- Sequences that increase tardiness despite reducing local queue time.
- Operators or shifts associated with unusually high setup duration.

The relevant metric is not just average setup time but total sequence burden:

\[
\text{Total Setup Burden}_m
=
\sum_{k=2}^{n} S_{m,J_{k-1},J_k}
\]

A sequence may have acceptable average setup duration but still impose excessive cumulative cost because it contains too many transitions.

## 2.4 Starvation and blocking

Cross-resource process mining should identify:

- Downstream machines idle while upstream queues are empty.
- Upstream machines blocked because downstream buffers are full.
- High variation in completion rates between linked operations.
- Jobs waiting for material, inspection, tooling, or transport rather than machine capacity.

A machine-state timeline synchronized with job-flow timelines can show whether a machine is idle because of poor dispatching, lack of work, maintenance, or upstream lateness.

## 2.5 WIP amplification and bullwhip effects

Plot WIP by work center over time and align it with:

- Release rates.
- Completion rates.
- Machine breakdowns.
- Priority changes.
- Dispatching-rule changes.
- Queue-time percentiles.

Patterns of concern include:

- Large release batches followed by congestion.
- Releasing jobs before bottleneck capacity is available.
- Sudden priority changes causing waves of resequencing.
- Upstream production continuing while downstream capacity is unavailable.
- Highly variable intercompletion times.

A useful measure is:

\[
\text{Flow Efficiency}
=
\frac{\text{Total Processing Time}}
{\text{Total Flow Time}}
\]

Low flow efficiency indicates that WIP consists mainly of waiting inventory.

# 3. Root Cause Analysis

## 3.1 Limits of local dispatching rules

FCFS and EDD are local rules. They typically ignore:

- Remaining routing.
- Downstream bottlenecks.
- Setup-transition costs.
- Current machine reliability.
- Job-specific processing uncertainty.
- The consequences of delaying other jobs.
- System-wide WIP and release levels.

A job with the earliest local due date may consume a costly setup, block a bottleneck, and delay three more urgent downstream jobs. Process mining can reconstruct these decisions and compare their actual outcomes with alternative candidate selections.

## 3.2 Lack of real-time visibility

Static schedules become obsolete when:

- A machine breaks down.
- A job runs longer than expected.
- An urgent job arrives.
- A job is rejected or sent for rework.
- Queue lengths change rapidly.

If schedule deviations consistently increase after such events, the problem is not only the initial schedule but the absence of a closed-loop rescheduling mechanism.

## 3.3 Inaccurate time estimates

Compare planned and actual durations by:

- Task type.
- Product family.
- Machine.
- Operator.
- Shift.
- Setup family transition.
- Job size and complexity.

Use bias and error measures:

\[
\text{Bias} = \text{Mean}(\text{Actual} - \text{Planned})
\]

\[
\text{MAPE},\quad \text{RMSE},\quad P90(\text{Actual Duration})
\]

If planners use averages while actual durations are highly skewed, schedules will systematically underestimate capacity requirements. Setup underestimation is especially damaging at bottleneck machines.

## 3.4 Poor handling of sequence-dependent setup

If the schedule treats setup as a fixed constant, it ignores the state of the machine. Process mining can show that actual setups vary materially by predecessor-successor pair. This creates hidden capacity loss and explains why nominally feasible schedules fail in execution.

## 3.5 Poor coordination between work centers

Local optimization often creates:

- Excessive releases into overloaded centers.
- Starvation of flexible downstream machines.
- Synchronization failures between operations.
- Misaligned batch sizes.
- Early completion that becomes WIP.

A causal or dependency analysis should trace the relationship between queue buildup at one resource and starvation or tardiness at another.

## 3.6 Inadequate disruption response

Breakdown and hot-job analysis should separate:

- Direct lost processing time.
- Rescheduling delay.
- Queue reordering delay.
- Setup losses from interruption.
- Secondary effects on downstream machines.

If tardiness rises much more than the direct downtime would imply, the response logic is amplifying the disruption.

## 3.7 Separating scheduling logic from capacity and variability

Process mining can help classify causes using controlled comparisons:

- Same product and routing on different machines.
- Same machine under different dispatching conditions.
- Similar jobs with and without breakdown exposure.
- Planned sequences versus actual sequences.
- Operator and shift comparisons.
- High-load versus normal-load periods.

Regression, survival analysis, and causal-style matched comparisons can estimate the effect of queue length, setup transition, priority, downtime, and processing variability while controlling for job complexity.

For example:

\[
\text{Tardiness}
=
\beta_0
+\beta_1(\text{bottleneck load})
+\beta_2(\text{setup transition})
+\beta_3(\text{breakdown exposure})
+\beta_4(\text{priority})
+\beta_5(\text{processing variance})
+\epsilon
\]

This does not prove causality automatically, but it provides a disciplined basis for separating capacity shortages from poor sequencing and poor control policies.

# 4. Advanced Data-Driven Scheduling Strategies

## Strategy 1: Dynamic bottleneck-aware dispatching

### Core logic

At every machine decision point, score eligible jobs using a composite priority:

\[
\text{Score}_{j,m}
=
w_1 \cdot \text{Urgency}_j
+
w_2 \cdot \text{Remaining Critical Path}_j
+
w_3 \cdot \text{Downstream Risk}_j
+
w_4 \cdot \text{Customer Priority}_j
-
w_5 \cdot \text{Setup Cost}_{m,\text{current},j}
-
w_6 \cdot \text{Estimated Processing Time}_j
\]

Possible components include:

- Slack time:

\[
\text{Slack}_j
=
D_j
-
\text{Current Time}
-
\text{Estimated Remaining Work}_j
\]

- Probability of missing the due date.
- Remaining work on the critical path.
- Queue length and workload at downstream bottlenecks.
- Setup penalty from the current machine state.
- Job priority and contractual penalty.
- Age or waiting time to prevent starvation.

The rule should be event-driven rather than recalculated only once per shift. Trigger rescheduling when:

- A machine becomes free.
- A breakdown occurs.
- A hot job arrives.
- A job finishes early or late.
- Queue length crosses a threshold.
- Predicted due-date risk changes materially.

### Process-mining inputs

Weights and thresholds should be learned from historical outcomes:

- Which features distinguished on-time from late jobs?
- How much did high downstream load increase lateness?
- What was the historical penalty of each setup transition?
- Which level of bottleneck utilization caused instability?
- Which priority classes were underserved?

Weights can initially be determined by operations experts and then calibrated using simulation or machine learning.

### Pathologies addressed

- Myopic FCFS/EDD decisions.
- Late handling of urgent jobs.
- Ignoring downstream congestion.
- Starvation and poor coordination.
- Excessive setup penalties where the setup saving does not justify delay.

### Expected impact

- Lower weighted tardiness.
- Better due-date reliability.
- Reduced unnecessary WIP release.
- Improved bottleneck throughput.
- More balanced queue lengths.

This strategy should include guardrails: jobs cannot be indefinitely bypassed, urgent jobs must satisfy defined escalation rules, and critical customer commitments may override normal scoring.

---

## Strategy 2: Predictive scheduling with duration and reliability models

### Core logic

Replace single-point planned durations with predictive distributions. For each operation, estimate:

\[
P(T_{j,o} \leq t \mid X)
\]

where \(X\) may include:

- Product family.
- Material and dimensions.
- Quantity.
- Machine.
- Operator.
- Shift.
- Setup family.
- Historical rework.
- Job complexity.
- Current machine condition.

Use median or quantile estimates for scheduling, such as P50 for normal work and P80 or P90 for high-risk jobs.

A predictive schedule can use:

- Expected processing time.
- P90 completion time.
- Probability of due-date violation.
- Remaining-time estimates for jobs already in progress.
- Predicted machine availability.
- Expected breakdown risk.

For machine reliability, derive from logs:

- Mean time between failures.
- Mean time to repair.
- Failure frequency by machine and operating conditions.
- Duration since last maintenance.
- Breakdown risk by shift or workload.

If sensor data is available, integrate condition-monitoring features; otherwise, use event-log-derived reliability indicators.

### Process-mining inputs

- Actual-versus-planned duration distributions.
- Resource-specific duration effects.
- Setup duration distributions.
- Rework and interruption history.
- Breakdown and repair distributions.
- Routing-specific completion probabilities.

Process mining supplies the empirical distributions and identifies the contexts in which predictions should differ.

### Pathologies addressed

- Optimistic schedules.
- Hidden processing-time variability.
- Unplanned bottleneck overload.
- Poor anticipation of breakdowns.
- Inaccurate customer lead-time promises.

### Expected impact

- More realistic due dates and completion estimates.
- Fewer cascading schedule failures.
- Better buffer placement.
- Lower P90 lead time and tardiness.
- More stable utilization.

The schedule should use uncertainty explicitly. For example, high-risk jobs can receive earlier start times or protected capacity, while low-risk work can fill available slack.

---

## Strategy 3: Setup-aware sequencing and intelligent batching

### Core logic

At each bottleneck machine, optimize the sequence using a transition-cost matrix. The objective can be:

\[
\min
\left[
\alpha \sum_j \text{Tardiness}_j
+
\beta \sum_j \text{Flow Time}_j
+
\gamma \sum \text{Setup Time}
+
\delta \sum_j \text{WIP}_j
\right]
\]

subject to:

- Routing precedence.
- Machine eligibility.
- Due dates.
- Priority constraints.
- Setup compatibility.
- Maximum batch size.
- Maximum waiting time.
- Maintenance windows.

Group jobs into families with similar tooling, fixtures, materials, or programs, but avoid unrestricted batching. A batch should be formed only when setup savings outweigh:

- Additional tardiness.
- WIP holding cost.
- Downstream starvation.
- Customer priority penalties.
- Risk of large delayed batches.

A rolling-horizon optimizer can sequence the next several jobs while preserving flexibility for new orders and disruptions. Suitable methods include mixed-integer programming for small horizons, constraint programming, large-neighborhood search, or hybrid metaheuristics.

### Process-mining inputs

- Empirical setup-transition matrix.
- Setup variance and P90 setup duration.
- Family compatibility.
- Historical effect of batching on tardiness.
- Machine-specific tooling and fixture requirements.
- Historical sequence patterns associated with high throughput.

### Pathologies addressed

- Excessive family switching.
- Ignoring previous-job state.
- High setup burden at bottlenecks.
- Batch sizes that create WIP and late jobs.
- Unnecessary setup variability.

### Expected impact

- Lower setup time.
- Higher effective bottleneck capacity.
- Better throughput and utilization.
- Lower WIP where batching is controlled.
- Potentially lower tardiness, provided due-date constraints are included.

Setup reduction should not become the sole objective. A sequence that saves one hour of setup but makes a high-priority job two days late is not an improvement.

---

## Strategy 4: Predictive rolling-horizon rescheduling and capacity protection

A fourth strategy is recommended for the disruption environment.

### Core logic

Maintain a continuously updated schedule over:

- A frozen near-term horizon, such as the next 1–2 hours.
- A flexible planning horizon, such as the next 1–3 days.
- A longer capacity-planning horizon for releases and maintenance.

When disruptions occur, re-optimize only the affected region unless system-wide conditions require broader changes. Reserve a controlled amount of capacity at bottlenecks for:

- Hot jobs.
- Recovery from breakdowns.
- High-penalty orders.
- Variability buffers.

Use alternative machines where technically feasible and quantify the effect on setup and processing times.

### Process-mining inputs

- Historical disruption frequency and duration.
- Recovery patterns.
- Hot-job arrival rates.
- Typical rescheduling damage.
- Alternative-resource performance.
- Effective capacity by shift.

### Pathologies addressed

- Schedule instability.
- Excessive reaction to every exception.
- Disruption propagation.
- Uncontrolled priority insertion.
- Lack of recovery capacity.

### Expected impact

- Faster recovery after breakdowns.
- Less secondary tardiness.
- More predictable lead times.
- Greater schedule stability.
- Better service for urgent orders without completely disrupting normal work.

# 5. Simulation, Evaluation, and Continuous Improvement

## 5.1 Discrete-event simulation model

Build a digital representation of the shop using:

- Actual job arrival distributions.
- Routing probabilities and variant frequencies.
- Processing-time distributions.
- Setup-transition distributions.
- Machine eligibility.
- Operator availability.
- Maintenance calendars.
- Breakdown and repair distributions.
- Rework probabilities.
- Priority-change behavior.
- Buffer and WIP constraints.
- Dispatching and rescheduling rules.

The model should be calibrated against historical observations:

- Throughput.
- Average and percentile lead time.
- WIP by work center.
- Machine utilization.
- Setup share.
- On-time completion.
- Queue-time distributions.
- Breakdown impact.

Validation should use holdout periods, not only the data used for calibration.

## 5.2 Strategies to compare

At minimum, compare:

1. Existing local FCFS/EDD baseline.
2. Dynamic bottleneck-aware dispatching.
3. Predictive scheduling.
4. Setup-aware sequencing.
5. Combined rolling-horizon strategy.

Use multiple replications with different random seeds and confidence intervals.

## 5.3 Scenarios to test

Test both normal and stressed conditions:

### Demand and load

- Normal utilization.
- High load approaching bottleneck capacity.
- Demand surges.
- Mix shifts toward setup-intensive jobs.
- Large urgent-order arrivals.

### Reliability

- Frequent breakdowns at the primary bottleneck.
- Long repair times.
- Simultaneous failures.
- Planned maintenance during peak demand.

### Variability

- Processing times above P90.
- Setup-time overruns.
- High rework rates.
- Operator shortages.
- Routing deviations.

### Policy robustness

- Different weights for tardiness versus setup reduction.
- Different hot-job reservation capacities.
- Alternative batch-size limits.
- Frozen versus fully flexible rescheduling horizons.
- Data-prediction errors.

## 5.4 Evaluation KPIs

Evaluate:

- Mean, P90, and P95 tardiness.
- On-time delivery by priority.
- Mean and percentile lead time.
- WIP and average queue length.
- Bottleneck throughput.
- Machine productive utilization.
- Setup time and setup frequency.
- Starvation and blocking.
- Schedule stability.
- Number of rescheduling actions.
- Recovery time after breakdown.
- Customer promise accuracy.

A strategy should not be accepted solely because it improves utilization. The preferred policy is usually the one that achieves the best service-flow tradeoff while remaining stable and operationally understandable.

## 5.5 Pilot deployment

Use a controlled pilot on:

- One bottleneck machine.
- One product family.
- One shift or planning horizon.

Initially operate in **shadow mode**: generate recommendations without controlling execution. Compare recommendations with actual operator choices and measure predicted versus realized outcomes.

Then deploy with:

- Human override.
- Explanation of each recommendation.
- Explicit priority and customer constraints.
- Emergency fallback to a stable rule.
- Logging of every scheduling decision and override.

## 5.6 Continuous process-mining control loop

The operating loop should be:

1. Capture new MES events.
2. Reconstruct current cases, queues, and resource states.
3. Update duration, setup, reliability, and routing models.
4. Recalculate predicted completion and bottleneck risk.
5. Generate or revise dispatching recommendations.
6. Execute with controlled overrides.
7. Compare predicted and actual outcomes.
8. Detect drift and retrain or recalibrate.

A monitoring dashboard should show:

- On-time delivery by priority.
- Current and forecast bottleneck load.
- WIP by work center.
- Queue-time percentiles.
- Setup-time trend.
- Planned-versus-actual duration bias.
- Breakdown frequency and repair time.
- Hot-job disruption impact.
- Schedule adherence.
- Prediction error.

## 5.7 Drift and anomaly detection

Automatically flag:

- A sustained increase in setup time.
- New routing variants.
- A machine’s utilization or failure rate changing materially.
- Rising prediction error.
- A new product family with no reliable model.
- Queue-time distribution shifts.
- Falling service levels for a priority class.
- More frequent operator overrides.
- WIP growth without corresponding demand growth.

Control charts, statistical process control, change-point detection, and model-performance monitoring can identify when the scheduling policy is no longer operating under the conditions for which it was designed.

## 5.8 Governance and improvement cadence

Establish:

- Daily exception review for active bottlenecks.
- Weekly KPI and schedule-quality review.
- Monthly model recalibration.
- Quarterly policy and objective-weight review.
- Version-controlled scheduling logic.
- Audit trails for decisions, overrides, and model changes.

The ultimate objective is not merely to discover that jobs are late. It is to create a closed-loop system in which historical process mining explains why performance occurs, predictive models anticipate what will happen, optimization selects better actions, simulation validates those actions, and ongoing event-log analysis verifies that the improvement persists.
