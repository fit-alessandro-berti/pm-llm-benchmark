## 1. Queue Identification and Characterization

### 1.1 Prepare and validate the event log

Transform the raw START/COMPLETE records into one activity instance per visit:

| Visit | Activity | Start | Complete | Resource | Attributes |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| V1001 | Registration | 09:02:15 | 09:08:45 | Clerk A | New, Normal |
| V1001 | Nurse Assessment | 09:15:20 | 09:25:10 | Nurse 1 | New, Normal |

Before analysis:

- Match START and COMPLETE events by visit, activity, and activity-instance identifier where repeated activities are possible.
- Standardize time zones and timestamps.
- Detect missing, duplicated, or reversed timestamps.
- Distinguish clinical activities from administrative events and passive delays.
- Preserve specialty, urgency, appointment time, arrival/check-in time, resource, room, and day-of-week attributes.
- Treat simultaneous or overlapping activities explicitly rather than forcing a false sequential order.

An activity-instance ID is important because a patient may undergo multiple blood tests, consultations, or reviews in one visit.

### 1.2 Define service, transition, and waiting times

For activity \(i\):

\[
ServiceTime_i = Complete_i - Start_i
\]

For two consecutive activities \(i\) and \(j\) in the same visit:

\[
InteractivityDelay_{i\rightarrow j} = Start_j - Complete_i
\]

This interval is a practical proxy for queue time: the elapsed time after the predecessor finishes and before the next activity begins.

For example, V1001 completes registration at 09:08:45 and starts nurse assessment at 09:15:20:

\[
Wait_{Registration\rightarrow Nurse} = 6\text{ min }35\text{ sec}
\]

However, not all interactivity delay is avoidable waiting. It can include:

- Walking or transport time.
- Required preparation or recovery.
- Specimen processing.
- Deliberate clinical observation.
- Time awaiting an external result.
- Administrative batching.
- Patient-caused delay.

Therefore, I would classify intervals as:

1. **Queue delay:** patient ready, but resource unavailable.
2. **Transfer/preparation delay:** necessary movement or setup.
3. **Clinical dependency delay:** next activity cannot begin until a result or condition is available.
4. **Patient-caused delay:** patient unavailable or late.
5. **Unclassified interactivity delay.**

With only activity start and completion times, queue time is estimated rather than observed directly. The clinic should ideally add events such as:

- `READY_FOR_DOCTOR`
- `REQUEST_CREATED`
- `RESULT_AVAILABLE`
- `PATIENT_ARRIVED`
- `ROOM_READY`
- `CALLED`
- `RESOURCE_ASSIGNED`

Then a more precise measure is:

\[
QueueTime_j = Start_j - ReadyTime_j
\]

Negative gaps caused by overlapping activities should not be converted into waits. They indicate concurrency, data errors, or an incorrectly inferred sequence.

For the first activity, initial wait should be measured as:

\[
InitialWait = Start_{first} - ArrivalTime
\]

For appointment-based analysis, separately calculate early/late arrival and delay relative to the scheduled appointment.

### 1.3 Reconstruct actual pathways

A process discovery model—such as a directly-follows graph, Petri net, or BPMN-oriented model—would reveal:

- Common pathways through the clinic.
- Rework and repeated tests.
- Optional diagnostics.
- Loops such as test  doctor review  repeat test.
- Skipped activities.
- Cross-specialty transfers.
- Parallel versus sequential work.
- Deviations from the intended pathway.

Queue metrics should be attached to specific process edges, such as:

- Arrival  Registration
- Registration  Nurse Assessment
- Nurse Assessment  Cardiology Consultation
- Consultation  ECG
- ECG  Specialist Review
- Final clinical activity  Check-out

Queues should also be segmented by destination specialty. “Nurse  doctor” may hide fundamentally different cardiology, orthopedics, and dermatology queues.

### 1.4 Queue metrics

For each transition and relevant subgroup, calculate:

#### Waiting-time distribution

- Mean wait.
- Median wait.
- 75th, 90th, 95th, and 99th percentiles.
- Maximum wait, with data-quality review.
- Standard deviation and interquartile range.
- Proportion exceeding operational or clinical thresholds.
- Proportion with no material wait, such as under five minutes.

The median represents a typical patient; P90/P95 exposes poor tail performance that averages can conceal.

#### Frequency and exposure

- Number and percentage of visits using the transition.
- Number of patients joining the queue per hour.
- Number and percentage experiencing excessive waits.
- Total patient-hours spent waiting:

\[
WaitBurden_q = \sum_{c \in q} Wait_{c,q}
\]

- Queue contribution to total visit duration:

\[
Contribution_q =
\frac{\sum Wait_{c,q}}{\sum VisitDuration_c}
\]

A moderate but frequent queue can create more dissatisfaction than a severe but rare one.

#### Throughput and capacity

- Arrival rate by 15-, 30-, or 60-minute interval.
- Completion rate.
- Service-time distribution.
- Estimated resource utilization.
- Time-varying work-in-process.
- Estimated queue length over time.
- Abandonment, cancellation, or visit interruption where observable.

An estimated queue length for activity \(j\) at time \(t\) is:

\[
Q_j(t)=
\#\{c: Ready_{c,j}\le t < Start_{c,j}\}
\]

This is most accurate when readiness timestamps exist.

#### Equity and clinical relevance

Stratify metrics by:

- New versus follow-up patients.
- Urgency.
- Specialty and provider.
- Age or mobility category, where appropriate and permissible.
- Visit complexity.
- Diagnostic pathway.
- Day, time, and session.
- Scheduled versus walk-in status.
- On-time versus late arrivals.

### 1.5 Prioritize critical queues

Do not prioritize on mean wait alone. Use a weighted score such as:

\[
Priority_q =
w_1(\text{P90 wait})+
w_2(\text{total wait burden})+
w_3(\text{frequency})+
w_4(\text{SLA breach rate})+
w_5(\text{clinical/equity risk})
\]

A critical queue typically has several of these properties:

- High median and P90 wait.
- High patient volume.
- Large total patient-hours lost.
- High variation and unpredictability.
- High contribution to end-to-end visit duration.
- Disproportionate impact on urgent or vulnerable patients.
- Recurrent peaks rather than isolated incidents.
- Evidence that demand regularly exceeds effective capacity.

For example, a 25-minute doctor wait affecting 70% of visits may deserve higher priority than a 50-minute specialist-review wait affecting 3%, unless the latter creates clinical risk.

## 2. Root Cause Analysis

### 2.1 Resource bottlenecks

Potential causes include:

- Too few doctors, nurses, clerks, technicians, rooms, or devices during peak demand.
- Staff schedules misaligned with arrival patterns.
- A nominally available provider being unavailable because of meetings, documentation, interruptions, or work at another location.
- Shared diagnostic equipment serving several specialties.
- Room turnover or cleaning delays.
- Skills constraints: only certain staff can perform a task.

#### Analysis

For each resource and resource pool, calculate:

- Busy time and estimated utilization.
- Number of simultaneous activities.
- Service-rate variation.
- Idle intervals while patients are waiting.
- Queue length while the resource is active versus unavailable.
- Performance by resource, adjusted for case mix.
- Handover volume and cross-resource transfers.

A pattern of long queues plus persistently high utilization suggests insufficient or badly timed capacity. Long queues combined with substantial apparent idle time suggests coordination failures, incorrect readiness assumptions, missing data, batching, or dependency constraints.

Utilization approaching 100% is generally unstable under variable arrivals and service times. Some buffer capacity is necessary.

### 2.2 Activity dependencies and handovers

Queues may result from patients waiting for:

- A physician to place an order.
- A diagnostic result before specialist review.
- Room assignment.
- Physical transfer.
- A staff member to notice that the patient is ready.
- Paperwork or authorization.
- A second consultation after testing.

#### Analysis

Use the discovered process model to identify:

- High-delay process edges.
- Repeated handoffs.
- Transitions involving changes in department, resource, or room.
- Long intervals after predecessor completion despite downstream availability.
- Loops and rework.
- Results completed well before anyone reviews them.

Conformance checking can compare actual behavior with the intended pathway. Recurrent deviations may identify unclear routing rules, unnecessary approvals, or poor notification practices.

### 2.3 Variability in service duration

Even when average capacity is sufficient, variability creates queues. Examples include:

- New-patient consultations lasting much longer than follow-ups.
- Unpredictable urgent cases.
- Complex diagnostic procedures.
- Provider-dependent practice patterns.
- Rework due to incomplete histories or documentation.

#### Analysis

For each activity, model service time by patient type, provider, specialty, urgency, and time of day. Examine:

- Mean, median, P90, and coefficient of variation.
- Outliers and long-tail behavior.
- Case-mix-adjusted resource differences.
- Correlation between long service times and the waits of subsequent patients.
- Whether scheduled slot lengths correspond to observed service distributions.

A 15-minute slot is not appropriate if new cardiology consultations have a median of 24 minutes and a P90 of 40 minutes.

### 2.4 Appointment scheduling policies

Possible problems include:

- Too many appointments at the top of each hour.
- Identical slot lengths for different case types.
- Intentional overbooking without evidence-based no-show estimates.
- Diagnostics scheduled after rather than before consultation.
- Follow-up demand added informally.
- Breaks, meetings, and shift changes coinciding with demand peaks.
- Urgent add-ons inserted without protected capacity.

#### Analysis

Join the event log with scheduled appointment and arrival data. Compare:

- Scheduled time, arrival time, and actual activity start.
- Demand and capacity by interval.
- Overbooking rates and no-show rates.
- Queue accumulation after morning or lunch starts.
- Provider lateness at session start.
- Delay propagation from one appointment to later appointments.

A cumulative-flow chart can reveal whether queues grow continuously, peak predictably, or arise after disruptions.

### 2.5 Patient arrival patterns

Queues can arise from:

- Early arrivals clustering before sessions.
- Late arrivals disrupting the intended sequence.
- Walk-in surges.
- Seasonal or day-of-week patterns.
- Batch arrivals from transport services.
- Registration opening shortly before many simultaneous appointments.

Use arrival-rate heat maps by day, hour, specialty, and patient class. Compare arrival rates with completion capacity. Distinguish appointment scheduling demand from actual arrival demand.

### 2.6 Patient type, urgency, and routing rules

New patients often require more registration, assessment, and consultation time. Urgent cases may bypass queues and delay normal patients. Some patients require additional diagnostics.

Use variant and cohort analysis to compare:

- New versus follow-up.
- Urgent versus normal.
- Simple versus diagnostic-intensive visits.
- Specialty-specific pathways.
- On-time versus late arrivals.
- Cases with and without rework.

Apply statistical or survival models to estimate the independent effect of these attributes on waiting time while controlling for hour, day, specialty, and workload. This avoids blaming patient type when the actual cause is that those patients are concentrated in overloaded sessions.

### 2.7 Distinguish root-cause patterns

Illustrative diagnostic patterns include:

| Observed pattern | Likely interpretation |
|---|---|
| High queue, high utilization, demand exceeds completions | Capacity shortage or schedule mismatch |
| High queue, resource apparently idle | Coordination, batching, readiness, or data issue |
| Queue follows a few very long services | High service-time variability |
| Queue spikes at session start | Batch scheduling or provider late start |
| Normal patients wait after urgent arrivals | No protected urgent capacity |
| Long delay at department boundary | Handover or transport failure |
| Long test-to-review wait despite result availability | Notification or review batching |
| High wait only for one variant | Routing or case-mix issue |
| Repeated tests or consultations | Rework or poor first-time quality |

## 3. Data-Driven Optimization Strategies

Targets and expected impacts should be finalized through simulation or a controlled pilot. The percentages below are plausible planning ranges, not guaranteed results.

### Strategy 1: Align staffing and room capacity with interval-level demand

**Target queues**

- Registration  nurse.
- Nurse  doctor.
- Consultation  diagnostic test.
- Final activity  check-out.

**Root cause addressed**

Capacity is available in aggregate but not when demand peaks; breaks, shift changes, or room availability may be poorly aligned.

**Data support**

Use 15- or 30-minute profiles of:

- Arrivals.
- Queue length.
- Service starts/completions.
- Resource utilization.
- Service-time distribution.
- Specialty and patient mix.

If registration demand peaks from 08:00–09:30 while clerical staffing peaks later, move shifts rather than add full-time positions. Cross-train a clerk or nurse for short peak coverage and stagger breaks. For diagnostics, align technician and room schedules with consultation completion patterns.

**Potential impact**

A queueing or discrete-event simulation could estimate impacts before implementation. Where mismatch is the primary cause, targeted peak coverage may reduce mean waits by approximately 15–35% and P90 waits by 20–40%, often with limited labor-cost increase.

**Practical actions**

- Stagger shift starts and breaks.
- Create short “flex” assignments during predictable peaks.
- Cross-train staff for registration/check-out.
- Reserve a room or technician block when downstream diagnostic demand peaks.
- Pool functionally equivalent staff where specialty constraints permit.

---

### Strategy 2: Introduce case-mix-aware appointment templates and demand smoothing

**Target queues**

- Arrival  registration.
- Registration/nurse  doctor.
- Doctor  diagnostics.
- Delays accumulating later in each provider session.

**Root cause addressed**

Batch scheduling, inappropriate slot lengths, overbooking, and insufficient allowance for new, complex, or urgent patients.

**Data support**

Estimate service-time distributions for:

- New versus follow-up patients.
- Specialty.
- Provider.
- Visit purpose.
- Diagnostic likelihood.
- Urgency.

Identify overbooking levels that exceed historical no-show rates and sessions where cumulative delay grows systematically.

**Potential impact**

Replacing uniform slots with case-mix-aware templates and spreading arrivals can plausibly reduce peak queue length by 20–40% and doctor-wait P90 by 15–30%, while preserving daily throughput.

**Practical actions**

- Assign longer slots to new or complex visits.
- Avoid scheduling many new patients consecutively.
- Spread appointments across the hour rather than at the hour.
- Reserve protected urgent slots based on historical urgent demand.
- Limit evidence-unsupported overbooking.
- Use reminders and targeted arrival instructions to reduce excessive early-arrival clustering.
- Add a recovery buffer after historically variable appointments.

This should not merely lengthen all appointments; the objective is to match slots to observed workload while maintaining throughput.

---

### Strategy 3: Redesign diagnostics and clinical work for parallel or pre-visit completion

**Target queues**

- Doctor  blood test/X-ray/ECG.
- Diagnostic completion  specialist review.
- Overall visit duration for diagnostic-intensive pathways.

**Root cause addressed**

Sequential processing and unnecessary dependency: patients first see the doctor, then wait for tests, then wait again for review.

**Data support**

Variant analysis can identify common predictable pathways, such as a high proportion of new cardiology patients receiving an ECG. Analyze whether orders can be protocol-driven and whether tests could safely occur before consultation.

**Potential impact**

For eligible pathways, pre-visit or pre-consultation testing could remove one queue and reduce end-to-end visit duration by 20–45 minutes or approximately 15–30%, depending on current waits.

**Practical actions**

- Pre-order standard diagnostics under approved clinical protocols.
- Complete questionnaires and history collection digitally before arrival.
- Schedule common tests before consultation.
- Parallelize administrative tasks with waiting for results.
- Route completed results electronically to the appropriate review queue.
- Create a fast pathway for patients requiring only a test and brief review.

**Safeguard**

Only clinically predictable activities should be moved earlier. Protocols require governance to prevent unnecessary testing or missed contraindications.

---

### Strategy 4: Implement real-time queue orchestration and readiness notifications

**Target queues**

- Nurse  doctor.
- Doctor  diagnostics.
- Test completion  review.
- Final clinical activity  check-out.

**Root cause addressed**

Resources do not know that a patient is ready; work is batched; patients are assigned to fixed queues even when another qualified resource is available.

**Data support**

This is especially appropriate where the log shows:

- Long interactivity gaps.
- Downstream resources with apparent idle capacity.
- Test results completed well before review.
- Large performance differences between equivalent queues.
- Delays at handoff boundaries.

**Potential impact**

Reducing coordination delay can plausibly lower targeted handoff waits by 10–25% without adding clinical staff.

**Practical actions**

- Display real-time queue status by patient readiness, urgency, wait age, and required skill.
- Send automatic alerts when tests are complete.
- Assign patients dynamically to the next qualified resource.
- Escalate when waits approach service thresholds.
- Use a digital “ready” event rather than verbal or paper handoffs.
- Route check-out to any available qualified clerk.

Urgency rules must remain explicit; “longest waiting first” alone is unsuitable in healthcare.

---

### Strategy 5: Create differentiated pathways and pooled services

**Target queues**

- Registration and nurse assessment for simple follow-ups.
- Doctor waits for low-complexity visits.
- Diagnostic queues shared across specialties.

**Root cause addressed**

All patients follow the same pathway despite different needs, and resources may be siloed.

**Data support**

Variant analysis may show stable, high-volume, low-complexity cohorts with shorter service requirements and fewer diagnostic dependencies.

**Potential impact**

A fast-track pathway for eligible follow-ups could reduce their total duration by 20–40%, while freeing capacity for complex patients. Pooling equivalent diagnostic or administrative resources may also reduce variability-induced waits.

**Practical actions**

- Pre-register returning patients digitally.
- Use a streamlined assessment for protocol-defined follow-ups.
- Offer virtual follow-up when clinically appropriate.
- Pool registration and check-out clerks.
- Pool interchangeable diagnostic rooms or technicians.
- Route complex cases to dedicated staff and longer slots.

Eligibility rules and audit processes are essential to prevent under-assessment.

## 4. Trade-offs and Constraints

### 4.1 Bottleneck shifting

Reducing registration time may move congestion to nurse assessment. Faster consultations may increase diagnostic demand. Pre-visit testing may create a result-review backlog.

**Mitigation:** evaluate the whole pathway through simulation and monitor downstream queue length, not only the targeted queue.

### 4.2 Cost versus responsiveness

Extra peak staffing or additional equipment can reduce waits but increase cost. Conversely, very high utilization appears cost-efficient but produces unstable queues.

**Mitigation:**

- First optimize schedules, pooling, cross-training, and coordination.
- Add capacity only where persistent demand-capacity imbalance remains.
- Compare incremental cost with patient-hours saved, additional completed visits, overtime reduction, and avoided abandonment.
- Maintain targeted rather than uniform buffer capacity.

### 4.3 Staff workload and burnout

Dynamic assignment and flex staffing can increase interruptions, workload unpredictability, or perceived surveillance.

**Mitigation:**

- Co-design changes with staff.
- Track workload, overtime, missed breaks, and staff satisfaction.
- Set safe workload limits.
- Ensure dashboards support decisions rather than punish individuals.
- Adjust comparisons for case mix.

### 4.4 Care quality and safety

Shorter service time is not automatically better. Pressure to reduce queues could lead to rushed consultation, missed assessment, inappropriate testing, or reduced communication.

**Mitigation:** treat clinical quality as a constraint, not a secondary KPI. Monitor service duration together with outcomes, rework, repeat visits, incidents, and adherence to clinical protocols.

### 4.5 Fairness and prioritization

Fast-tracking simple cases can make complex patients wait longer. Strict first-come-first-served rules can conflict with urgency.

**Mitigation:** apply explicit priority classes, maximum-wait safeguards, and equity monitoring. Compare improvements across urgency, patient type, specialty, and vulnerable groups.

### 4.6 Technology and data constraints

Real-time orchestration requires reliable status events and system integration. Incorrect timestamps can generate misleading recommendations.

**Mitigation:**

- Pilot in one specialty.
- Validate event capture against direct observation.
- Provide manual fallback procedures.
- Maintain privacy, access controls, and audit logs.
- Monitor missing-event and timestamp-quality rates.

### 4.7 Patient behavior and preference

Earlier tests, digital check-in, and virtual follow-ups may not suit all patients.

**Mitigation:** offer assisted alternatives, consider accessibility and digital literacy, and avoid penalizing patients unable to use technology.

### 4.8 Balancing objectives formally

Use a multi-objective scorecard rather than optimizing only average wait:

\[
\min \left(
\text{Mean/P90 Wait},
\text{Visit Duration},
\text{Cost},
\text{Overtime},
\text{Inequity}
\right)
\]

subject to:

- Minimum quality and safety thresholds.
- Staffing and room constraints.
- Maximum acceptable staff utilization/workload.
- Urgency-specific maximum waits.
- Required throughput.

Candidate interventions should be tested in a discrete-event simulation using empirical arrival and service-time distributions. Pilot changes through controlled phases, comparing intervention sessions with matched baseline sessions where feasible.

## 5. Measuring Success

### 5.1 Patient-flow KPIs

For each major transition and subgroup:

- Mean, median, P90, and P95 waiting time.
- Percentage exceeding queue-specific targets.
- Total waiting time per visit.
- Total patient-hours waiting per day.
- End-to-end visit duration:
  
\[
VisitDuration = LastComplete - Arrival
\]

- Touch time versus idle time:

\[
TouchRatio =
\frac{\sum ServiceTime}{VisitDuration}
\]

- Throughput per session or day.
- Queue length over time.
- Work-in-process.
- Time from diagnostic completion to clinical review.
- Percentage of visits completed within target duration.
- Abandonment, cancellation, or incomplete-visit rate.

Tail metrics are essential because an intervention can improve the mean while leaving a minority of patients with extreme waits.

### 5.2 Operational KPIs

- Resource utilization by interval and resource pool.
- Overtime and missed-break frequency.
- Service-time distributions.
- Schedule adherence and session start delay.
- Room/equipment utilization.
- Number of handoffs per visit.
- Rework and repeated-activity rate.
- On-time completion rate.
- Cost per completed visit.
- Cost per patient-hour of waiting avoided.

### 5.3 Quality, safety, and experience KPIs

- Patient satisfaction and wait-time-specific survey scores.
- Complaints about delay or communication.
- Repeat visits or unplanned returns.
- Diagnostic or ordering errors.
- Protocol compliance.
- Incident and near-miss rates.
- Consultation thoroughness proxies, used carefully.
- Staff satisfaction and burnout indicators.

### 5.4 Equity KPIs

Compare all principal KPIs across:

- New and follow-up patients.
- Urgent and normal patients.
- Specialty.
- Age or accessibility group where legally and ethically appropriate.
- Digital versus assisted check-in.
- Complex versus simple pathways.

Track both absolute waits and disparities between groups.

### 5.5 Ongoing process monitoring

Build a process-mining dashboard from the same event structure, enhanced with readiness, arrival, schedule, and result-availability timestamps. It should provide:

- Daily and weekly queue heat maps.
- Directly-follows models with wait times overlaid.
- Trend charts for median and P90 waits.
- Cumulative-flow diagrams.
- Resource utilization and demand-capacity profiles.
- Variant frequencies and performance.
- Threshold alerts.
- Cohort and equity comparisons.
- Conformance deviations from redesigned pathways.

Use statistical process control charts to distinguish normal fluctuation from sustained changes. Compare:

1. A six-month baseline.
2. Pilot period.
3. Post-implementation stabilization period.
4. Ongoing performance.

Adjust comparisons for seasonality, day of week, specialty mix, urgency, and patient complexity. A simple before-and-after comparison may falsely attribute changes to an intervention.

### 5.6 Governance cycle

A practical review cycle is:

1. **Weekly operational review:** inspect alerts, queues, staffing mismatch, and data quality.
2. **Monthly process review:** examine variants, bottlenecks, equity, quality, and cost.
3. **Quarterly redesign review:** update appointment templates, resource plans, and pathway rules.
4. **Continuous validation:** periodically compare system-derived waits with direct observations.

Define explicit success criteria before deployment, for example:

- Reduce median total waiting time by at least 20%.
- Reduce P90 visit duration by at least 15%.
- Reduce excessive-wait breaches by at least 30%.
- Maintain or improve throughput.
- Avoid material increases in cost per visit, overtime, adverse events, or rework.
- Avoid worsening waits for urgent or complex patients.

The central principle is to optimize the end-to-end patient journey, not isolated activity speed. Queue mining identifies where time accumulates; resource, variant, conformance, and simulation analyses determine why it accumulates and which interventions are likely to improve flow safely and economically.
