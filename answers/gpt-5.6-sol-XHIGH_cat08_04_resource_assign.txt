A sound approach should separate three possible causes of delay: **routing errors, insufficient skill capacity, and inefficient execution after a correct assignment**. Process mining can distinguish these by reconstructing assignment episodes, working intervals, handovers, and SLA clocks for every ticket.

No numerical benefit should be claimed from the snippet alone; the calculations below would be applied to the full-year log.

## 1. Analyzing Resource Behavior and Assignment Patterns

### 1.1 Prepare a resource-aware event log

Before mining, transform the raw events into a consistent analytical model.

For every event, retain or derive:

- Ticket ID, activity, timestamp, and lifecycle state.
- Event performer, separately from the **target assignee**. For example, the dispatcher performs “Assign L2,” but Agent B12 is the target resource.
- Previous and new assignee/pool/tier.
- Assignment reason, escalation reason, and override reason.
- Ticket priority, category, channel, configuration item, and description.
- Required skill as it was known at the time of each decision.
- Agent skills and proficiency levels as of that date.
- Agent schedule, shift, absence, and availability.
- SLA deadline and business-time calendar.
- Waiting states that stop the SLA clock, such as “Waiting for Customer” or “Waiting for Vendor.”
- Resolution, closure, and reopen events.

Each assignment should become an **assignment episode**:

1. Assignment time.
2. Assigned resource or pool.
3. First work start.
4. One or more active work intervals.
5. End through resolution, escalation, or reassignment.
6. Outcome and handover reason.

This enables separation of:

- **Queue time:** assignment to work start.
- **Touch time:** actual work-start to work-end intervals.
- **Handover latency:** escalation/reassignment to the next productive work start.
- **End-to-end resolution time.**

Several data-quality issues visible in the example need attention:

- `DB-SQL` and `Database-SQL` should be normalized to one skill taxonomy.
- B12 appears to possess `DB-SQL` but the ticket is reassigned because it “needs different skill (DB).” This could represent a taxonomy problem, insufficient proficiency, or an inaccurate reassignment reason.
- A change from `App-CRM` to `Database-SQL` may be a legitimate discovery during diagnosis, not necessarily an initial routing error.
- Assignment targets should be structured fields rather than parsed from notes.
- The log must be sorted by ticket and timestamp rather than relying on row order.

### 1.2 Agent- and tier-level performance metrics

Metrics should be calculated by agent, tier, skill, priority, category, channel, and time period. Medians and upper percentiles should be reported alongside averages because incident times are normally skewed.

#### Workload and capacity

- Tickets assigned and completed.
- Active touch hours.
- Average and peak work in progress.
- Number of concurrent tickets.
- Weighted workload based on expected effort, rather than raw ticket count.
- Scheduled available hours.
- Utilization:

\[
\text{Utilization} =
\frac{\text{union of active working minutes}}
{\text{scheduled available minutes}}
\]

Using the union of intervals avoids double-counting concurrent tickets.

- Workload distribution using coefficient of variation, Gini coefficient, and highest-to-lowest workload ratios.
- Overtime and out-of-hours work.
- Queue size and age by resource pool and skill.

Raw ticket counts are misleading. Ten simple access tickets may require less capacity than one complex network incident.

#### Speed and flow efficiency

- Time from creation to first assignment.
- Assignment-to-first-work time.
- Active handling time per activity.
- Handover time between agents and tiers.
- Total waiting time versus total touch time.
- Resolution time and SLA attainment.
- P50, P75, P90, and P95 times.
- Repeated diagnostic effort after a handover.

For L2 and L3, queue time should be separated from actual specialist work. A specialist may appear slow when the real issue is that tickets wait several hours before the specialist can begin.

#### Routing and ownership

- Number of assignments per ticket.
- Percentage of tickets reassigned at least once or more than once.
- Same-tier versus cross-tier transfers.
- Escalation, de-escalation, and “ping-pong” rates.
- First-assignment resolution rate.
- Initial skill-match rate.
- Percentage of assignments made to an unavailable or overloaded agent.
- Escalation acceptance rate: the percentage resolved by the receiving tier without another transfer.
- Ownership continuity: percentage of touch time performed by the primary owner.

#### L1 effectiveness

For phone incidents:

\[
\text{FCR} =
\frac{\text{tickets resolved during the initial L1 contact, with no subsequent escalation or reopen}}
{\text{eligible initial L1 contacts}}
\]

For email and portal cases, use a comparable **first-contact resolution** definition.

Also measure:

- L1 escalation rate by agent and ticket type.
- Time spent before escalation.
- Diagnostic completeness at escalation.
- Percentage of L1 escalations for ticket types that other L1 agents routinely resolve.
- Reopen rate after L1 resolution.
- FCR adjusted for channel and case complexity.

A high FCR should not be rewarded if it is accompanied by high reopen rates or customers repeatedly contacting the desk.

#### Quality and outcomes

- SLA compliance by priority.
- Reopen and repeat-incident rates.
- Customer satisfaction, if available.
- Resolution without reassignment.
- Percentage of tickets requiring correction of category or skill.
- Normalized success rate after controlling for case mix.

Individual performance should be risk-adjusted. An agent handling mostly P2 security incidents should not be compared directly with an agent handling P4 password requests.

### 1.3 Discover the actual assignment process

#### Process discovery and variant analysis

Discover separate but connected views of:

- Ticket lifecycle.
- Assignment and reassignment lifecycle.
- Productive work lifecycle.
- Escalation path.
- Resolution and reopening.

Directly-Follows Graphs, Petri nets, or BPMN-like models can reveal paths such as:

- L1  L2  resolved.
- L1  L2  another L2  L3.
- L1  L2  L1  L2.
- Direct assignment to L2 or L3.
- Multiple assignments before any work starts.
- Reassignment after substantial work has already occurred.

Overlay the discovered model with:

- Frequency.
- Mean and P90 waiting time.
- SLA breach rate.
- Skill-match rate.
- Ticket category and priority.

This shows whether the dominant flow follows the intended tier model or whether exceptions have effectively become the normal process.

#### Conformance checking against intended logic

Translate the intended assignment policy into explicit rules, for example:

- L1 receives all eligible initial tickets except defined specialist exceptions.
- Assigned agents must meet minimum required skill proficiency.
- P2 tickets must be accepted within a specified time.
- Escalations must include required diagnostics.
- Round-robin selection applies only among available, eligible agents.
- Certain ticket types may skip L1.

For every assignment, reconstruct the eligible candidate set using information available at that moment. Compare:

- Actual assignee versus expected round-robin assignee.
- Actual skill match versus required proficiency.
- Actual queue/load versus other eligible agents.
- Actual escalation path versus allowed path.
- Frequency and outcome of manual overrides.

This distinguishes justified deviations from uncontrolled routing.

#### Resource interaction and social network analysis

Construct several organizational networks:

- **Handover-of-work network:** Agent A  Agent B when B is the next person to work on the ticket.
- **Subcontracting network:** A starts a case, B performs work, and the case returns to A.
- **Working-together network:** agents who frequently participate in the same tickets.
- **Tier handover network:** L1  L2, L2  L3, and reverse flows.

The dispatcher should not be treated as the receiving resource merely because the dispatcher performed the assignment event.

Network measures reveal:

- Agents with high betweenness who act as routing or knowledge bottlenecks.
- Specialists receiving a disproportionate share of handovers.
- Reciprocal edges indicating ticket ping-pong.
- Teams that rarely collaborate despite complementary skills.
- Single-person dependencies for particular technologies.

#### Role discovery

Build a resource profile from each agent’s:

- Activities performed.
- Ticket categories and required skills handled.
- Escalation and resolution behavior.
- Frequency of collaboration.
- Resolution success and handling-time profile.

Clustering these profiles may reveal de facto roles that differ from formal tiers, such as:

- An L1 agent functioning as an access-management specialist.
- An L2 agent handling mostly basic work.
- A small group acting as informal triage coordinators.
- An L3 specialist regularly receiving tickets that other L2 agents could resolve.

### 1.4 Analyze skill utilization

Create an agent-skill-ticket matrix containing:

- Documented proficiency.
- Minimum proficiency required.
- Number of tickets handled.
- Touch hours.
- Successful resolutions without handover.
- Handling time and reopen rate.
- Availability by shift.

Key measures include:

1. **Skill-match rate:** assignments where the agent meets all mandatory requirements.
2. **Partial-match rate:** some but not all requirements met.
3. **Skill-specific success rate:** adjusted for priority and complexity.
4. **Skill demand-to-capacity ratio:**

\[
\rho_{s,t} =
\frac{\text{expected touch hours arriving for skill }s}
{\text{qualified available hours for skill }s}
\]

5. **Skill concentration:** how much demand depends on one or two people.
6. **Unused capability:** qualified agent hours not used while matching work is queued elsewhere.
7. **Specialist leverage:** percentage of specialist touch time spent on tickets genuinely requiring that expertise.
8. **Overqualification share:** specialist time spent on work that qualified L1/L2 agents could perform.

A specialist should only be classified as inappropriately used when:

- The ticket did not require the specialist’s proficiency;
- A less specialized qualified agent was available or could have been scheduled; and
- The specialist’s scarce-skill queue or other demand created an opportunity cost.

## 2. Identifying Resource-Related Bottlenecks and Issues

### 2.1 Skill-capacity bottlenecks

A genuine shortage of a skill will normally show:

- High demand-to-capacity ratio for that skill.
- High and increasing skill-specific queue time.
- Eligible agents operating near sustainable utilization.
- Similar delays even when routing is correct.
- Concentration of work on very few qualified people.
- Strong time-of-day or shift-related coverage gaps.

If skill-specific queue time is high while qualified agents are underutilized, the likely cause is routing or visibility rather than capacity.

Useful outputs include:

- P90 wait time by required skill and shift.
- Hours per week where no qualified agent is available.
- Percentage of P2/P3 demand arriving during coverage gaps.
- Number of qualified agents per critical skill.
- Forecast demand minus capacity by skill.

### 2.2 Reassignment and escalation delay

For each reassignment \(j\), calculate the direct handover latency:

\[
H_j =
\text{next productive work start}_j -
\text{reassignment timestamp}_j
\]

Use business time and exclude valid SLA-paused states.

Then calculate:

- Average and P90 handover latency.
- Total ticket time spent waiting after reassignments.
- Additional repeated touch time, such as a second diagnostic cycle.
- Percentage of total resolution time attributable to handover states.
- Incremental delay associated with the first, second, and subsequent reassignments.

A stronger estimate of incremental impact compares reassigned tickets with similar non-reassigned tickets:

\[
\text{Incremental delay} =
E[T\mid\text{reassigned}] -
E[T_{\text{counterfactual}}\mid\text{similar case, no reassignment}]
\]

Matching or regression should control for priority, category, complexity, channel, creation time, and required skill. Otherwise, complex tickets may make reassignment look more damaging than it actually is.

Separate:

- Necessary escalation caused by newly discovered complexity.
- Avoidable same-tier reassignment.
- Wrong-skill routing.
- Capacity-driven transfer.
- Ownership changes with no productive work before the transfer.

### 2.3 Incorrect initial assignments

For every initial assignment, compare the skill requirement known at that moment with the assigned agent’s profile.

Classify outcomes as:

- Correct and resolved by the assigned resource.
- Correct but reassigned because of workload or absence.
- Mismatched despite the requirement being known.
- Requirement changed after diagnosis.
- Category or requirement was initially wrong.
- Skill profile was missing or stale.

Measure:

- Initial skill-match rate.
- Reassignment probability after a match versus mismatch.
- SLA breach probability after a match versus mismatch.
- Time lost before the mismatch was recognized.
- Percentage of tickets assigned to an ineligible resource while an eligible resource was available.
- Accuracy of initial category and required-skill predictions.

The eventual resolver’s skill should not automatically be treated as the original ground truth. Some tickets legitimately reveal a new technical cause during diagnosis.

### 2.4 Overloaded, underutilized, and apparently underperforming resources

Create a resource performance matrix:

| Pattern | Likely interpretation |
|---|---|
| High utilization, high queue, normal risk-adjusted touch time | Capacity overload |
| High utilization, high touch time, high reopen rate | Possible complexity, training, or quality issue |
| Low utilization while eligible work is queued | Routing, schedule, or availability-data problem |
| Low utilization because no matching demand exists | Skill mix or scheduling issue |
| High assignments but low touch time | Work being pushed to the agent but not actually processed |
| High handovers and central network position | Informal triage role or knowledge bottleneck |

Performance models should estimate expected handling time and resolution probability based on ticket characteristics. Agent residuals can then identify persistent differences after case-mix adjustment.

These results should be used for coaching and process design, not simplistic individual league tables.

### 2.5 Relationship with SLA breaches

Start with descriptive measures:

\[
\text{Breach exposure fraction} =
\frac{\text{breached tickets with initial mismatch}}
{\text{all breached tickets}}
\]

\[
\text{Mismatch risk difference} =
P(\text{breach}\mid\text{mismatch}) -
P(\text{breach}\mid\text{match})
\]

Also calculate breach rates by:

- Reassignment count.
- Escalation path.
- Required skill.
- Initial tier.
- Agent utilization at assignment.
- Queue length at assignment.
- After-hours versus staffed periods.
- Time to first productive work.

Use logistic regression, survival analysis, or process-aware machine learning to control for priority, complexity, category, channel, season, and creation time. For example:

\[
P(\text{breach}) =
f(\text{skill mismatch, handovers, workload, queue, priority, complexity})
\]

The model can estimate the adjusted contribution of mismatches and handovers. This is evidence of association; causal claims should be validated through a pilot or controlled policy change.

## 3. Root Cause Analysis for Assignment Inefficiencies

### 3.1 Likely root causes and diagnostic evidence

| Potential root cause | Process-mining evidence |
|---|---|
| Round-robin ignores skill | High mismatch rate even when qualified agents are available |
| Round-robin ignores workload | Tickets assigned to agents with long queues while comparable agents are lightly loaded |
| Incomplete or stale skill profiles | Agents repeatedly succeed in skills not listed, or fail in skills marked as proficient |
| Skill taxonomy is inconsistent | Similar labels such as `DB-SQL` and `Database-SQL` produce avoidable routing differences |
| Incorrect categorization | Category or required skill changes shortly after initial diagnosis |
| Poor requirement detection | Ticket description clearly indicates a specialist skill, but routing uses only category |
| No real-time workload visibility | Overloaded agents continue receiving work after their backlog becomes materially higher |
| Insufficient specialist coverage | Correctly routed tickets wait because all qualified agents are occupied or off shift |
| L1 training or authority gaps | Large agent-level variation in escalation for otherwise comparable incidents |
| Weak escalation package | L2 repeats diagnostics already attempted by L1 |
| Rigid tier boundaries | L1-capable work goes to L2, while cross-skilled L1 agents are idle |
| Incentives favor transfer over ownership | Agents escalate quickly or repeatedly without meaningful troubleshooting |
| Manual ownership conventions | A few agents act as informal routing hubs, producing high network centrality |
| Missing availability data | Tickets are assigned to absent, meeting-bound, or otherwise unavailable agents |

### 3.2 Variant analysis

Define contrasting cohorts.

**Smooth variants:**

- One correct initial assignment, or one justified escalation.
- No unnecessary reassignment.
- Resolved within SLA.
- No reopen within a defined quality window.

**Churn variants:**

- Two or more reassignments.
- Repeated tier changes.
- Long handover waits.
- SLA breach or reopen.

Compare the cohorts by:

- Priority, category, channel, and creation hour.
- Initial required skill.
- Initial assignee skill match.
- Agent workload at assignment.
- Staffing level by skill.
- Category or requirement changes.
- L1 agent and dispatcher.
- Escalation reason.
- Diagnostics completed before transfer.
- Final resolving tier and skill.

For example, if churn variants are concentrated in P2 network tickets created after 17:00, the issue may be firewall coverage rather than generalized dispatcher performance.

### 3.3 Decision mining

Decision mining analyzes choices at process decision points such as:

- Initial assignment.
- L1 resolve versus escalate.
- L2 versus L3 destination.
- Same-tier reassignment.
- Specialist consultation versus ownership transfer.

Use only attributes available at decision time:

- Priority, category, channel, description keywords.
- Known required skill.
- Configuration item and affected service.
- Troubleshooting results.
- SLA time remaining.
- Candidate agents’ skills, queues, schedules, and current WIP.
- Day and shift.

Define a good assignment outcome as, for example:

- Resolved without further reassignment.
- SLA met.
- No reopen.
- Acceptable handling time and quality.

Decision trees can produce understandable rules; gradient-boosting or similar methods may improve prediction but require explainability and governance. The analysis may reveal rules such as:

- “P2 network tickets with firewall-related terms have a high probability of two transfers when first assigned to generic L1.”
- “CRM tickets are usually resolved by L1 when a specific diagnostic result is present.”
- “Assignments made when the selected agent has more than a given amount of predicted remaining work have a materially higher breach rate.”

Decision mining should distinguish causes:

- If the requirement was known but ignored, fix routing.
- If it was not detected, fix triage.
- If the routing was correct but the queue was saturated, fix capacity.
- If similarly assigned agents produce different outcomes, investigate training, tools, or knowledge access.

## 4. Developing Data-Driven Resource Assignment Strategies

The strategies below are complementary. Predictive triage determines what the ticket needs; skill rules identify eligible resources; workload-aware routing chooses among them; capacity management ensures resources exist.

### Strategy 1: Proficiency-based skill routing with specialist protection

**Issue addressed:** Wrong-skill assignments, repeated same-tier transfers, and L2/L3 specialists doing low-complexity work.

**Approach:**

- Define mandatory and optional skills for each ticket.
- Maintain proficiency levels such as awareness, working, advanced, and expert.
- Exclude agents who do not meet mandatory requirements.
- Among eligible agents, select the **least specialized sufficiently qualified** agent unless severity or complexity justifies an expert.
- Reserve scarce experts for tickets requiring their capability.
- Support multi-skill tickets with primary and secondary skill requirements or a collaboration model.

A routing score could use:

\[
\text{Score}(a,t) =
w_1\text{SkillFit} +
w_2\text{AdjustedSuccess} +
w_3\text{Continuity}
-
w_4\text{ScarcityCost}
\]

Historical success should be case-mix adjusted so the algorithm does not simply send all work to a small number of high-volume agents.

**Process-mining insight used:**

- Agent-skill success matrix.
- Initial mismatch patterns.
- Reassignment destinations.
- Specialist low-skill utilization.
- Skills causing the longest queues.

**Required data:**

- Standardized ticket skill requirements.
- Agent skills, proficiency, certification, and effective dates.
- Historical outcomes by agent-skill combination.
- Availability and tier restrictions.
- Security or segregation-of-duty constraints.

**Expected benefits:**

- Higher initial skill-match rate.
- Fewer same-tier reassignments.
- Better specialist utilization.
- Reduced duplicated diagnosis.
- Lower resolution time and breach risk for skill-dependent tickets.

### Strategy 2: Workload- and SLA-aware dynamic assignment

**Issue addressed:** Uneven workload and round-robin assignments that ignore queue length, expected effort, and SLA urgency.

**Approach:**

After skill eligibility is established, choose the agent with the best predicted completion outcome rather than the fewest raw tickets.

At each decision, calculate:

- Current expected remaining workload.
- Scheduled availability.
- Predicted handling time for the ticket-agent pairing.
- SLA time remaining.
- Ticket priority and aging.
- Existing high-priority commitments.
- Continuity benefit if the agent already knows the ticket.

Use predicted workload hours instead of ticket count. A P2 database incident and a P4 password reset should not carry equal weight.

Possible policy objectives include:

- Minimize predicted SLA breaches.
- Assign to earliest predicted completion.
- Balance workload within a safe range.
- Reserve some specialist capacity for P1/P2 work.
- Prevent assignment to agents whose queues exceed defined thresholds.

**Process-mining insight used:**

- Queue-time distributions.
- Workload-to-breach relationship.
- Agent and tier utilization patterns.
- Peak-period demand and capacity.
- Cases assigned to overloaded agents while eligible agents were available.

**Required data:**

- Real-time WIP and assignment state.
- Active work status and predicted remaining effort.
- Agent schedules and absences.
- SLA deadlines and business calendars.
- Conditional service-time estimates.
- Skill eligibility.

**Expected benefits:**

- More even workload.
- Lower assignment-to-start time.
- Fewer overload-driven reassignments.
- Improved P2/P3 SLA compliance.
- Better use of currently underutilized resources.

### Strategy 3: Predictive triage and required-skill classification

**Issue addressed:** Poor initial categorization, incorrect skill identification, and routine L1 routing of tickets that are predictably specialist cases.

**Approach:**

Train a multi-label prediction model to estimate:

- Required skill or skill set.
- Expected complexity.
- Probability of L1 resolution.
- Likely resolving tier.
- Expected effort.
- Risk of reassignment or SLA breach.

Inputs may include:

- Ticket title and description.
- Category, subcategory, and channel.
- Affected service or configuration item.
- Customer or site context.
- Diagnostic responses.
- Similar historical incidents.
- Known error and knowledge-base matches.

Use confidence thresholds:

- High-confidence, low-complexity tickets go to the appropriate L1 skill group.
- High-confidence specialist tickets may route directly to L2/L3 where policy allows.
- Low-confidence cases go to a human triage queue with ranked recommendations.
- Security and other controlled categories retain hard policy rules.

**Process-mining insight used:**

- Final resolved skill versus initial category.
- Smooth versus churn variants.
- Description patterns associated with routing corrections.
- Historical paths that reliably resolve at L1 or require specialist handling.

**Required data:**

- Ticket text and structured classification fields.
- Curated historical skill and resolution labels.
- Final resolving skill and tier.
- Reassignment reasons.
- Quality outcomes and reopen data.

**Expected benefits:**

- Fewer initial classification errors.
- Reduced “ritual” L1-to-L2 transfers.
- Faster routing to the correct skill pool.
- Better workload forecasts by skill.
- Improved first-assignment resolution.

The model must be monitored for drift and should not learn poor historical routing as the desired behavior.

### Strategy 4: Data-driven L1 enablement and refined escalation

**Issue addressed:** Excessive escalations and specialist time spent repeating basic diagnostics.

**Approach:**

Use successful L1 variants to identify ticket types that L1 can resolve reliably. Then implement:

- Context-specific troubleshooting scripts.
- Knowledge article recommendations.
- Targeted training by L1 agent and skill gap.
- Required diagnostic evidence before escalation.
- Time-boxed troubleshooting based on priority and SLA slack.
- “Consult without transfer” or swarming with L2 for borderline cases.
- Skill-aware L1 sub-pools, such as access, desktop, or common application support.

Escalation criteria should avoid both extremes:

- Escalating before basic checks are completed.
- Holding a specialist incident too long at L1.

**Process-mining insight used:**

- L1 FCR by ticket type and agent.
- Cases one L1 group resolves but another escalates.
- Repeated L2 diagnostics.
- Escalations that immediately resolve using known standard actions.
- Escalations that breach because they were delayed too long.

**Required data:**

- L1 diagnostic steps and outcomes.
- Knowledge-article usage.
- Escalation reason and diagnostic package.
- Agent training and authorization.
- Historical L1-resolved variants.

**Expected benefits:**

- Increased FCR.
- Reduced unnecessary L2/L3 demand.
- Better escalation quality.
- Lower repeated touch time.
- Faster specialist resolution when escalation is necessary.

### Strategy 5: Forecast-driven capacity and dynamic skill pools

**Issue addressed:** Genuine shortages of particular skills, shift coverage gaps, and rigid tier boundaries.

**Approach:**

Forecast arrival volume and handling demand by:

- Skill.
- Priority.
- Time of day and day of week.
- Season and release calendar.
- Customer or service.

Use the forecast to:

- Change schedules or on-call coverage.
- Create virtual skill pools across tiers.
- Temporarily reallocate cross-skilled agents.
- Reserve critical-skill capacity during predictable peaks.
- Prioritize cross-training where demand-capacity gaps are largest.
- Reduce single-person dependencies.

A dynamic trigger could move a cross-skilled agent into a pool when:

- Predicted queue wait exceeds a threshold;
- Skill utilization exceeds a safe range; or
- The number of P2 tickets at risk exceeds a threshold.

**Process-mining insight used:**

- Skill-specific arrival and service patterns.
- Time-dependent queues.
- Skill concentration and coverage gaps.
- Underused cross-skilled agents.
- Repeated breach periods.

**Required data:**

- Historical arrivals and service demand.
- Agent schedules and absence patterns.
- Skill/proficiency matrix.
- Release, maintenance, and business calendars.
- Real-time queue and SLA-risk data.

**Expected benefits:**

- Lower peak-period queues.
- More resilient skill coverage.
- Reduced dependency on a few experts.
- Better schedule-to-demand alignment.
- Improved SLA performance without indiscriminate headcount increases.

## 5. Simulation, Implementation, and Monitoring

### 5.1 Evaluate strategies with business process simulation

Build a discrete-event simulation or process digital twin calibrated from the mined process.

The baseline model should include:

- Time-varying arrivals by priority, category, channel, and skill.
- The discovered process variants and branching probabilities.
- Agent and skill pools.
- Shift schedules, absences, and calendars.
- Service-time distributions by activity, ticket type, skill fit, and tier.
- Queueing discipline and current round-robin policy.
- Escalation, reassignment, reopen, and resolution probabilities.
- SLA clocks and pause rules.
- Concurrency and non-preemptive work behavior where applicable.

Do not use one global average service time. Use conditional empirical distributions or fitted distributions for different ticket and resource combinations.

#### Validate the baseline

Before testing alternatives, verify that the simulation reproduces historical:

- Ticket volumes.
- Queue lengths.
- Resolution-time distributions.
- Escalation and reassignment rates.
- Agent/tier utilization.
- SLA compliance.
- Major process variants.

If the baseline cannot reproduce observed performance, scenario results will not be credible.

#### Simulate alternative policies

Compare:

1. Current round-robin/manual process.
2. Skill-based routing only.
3. Workload/SLA-aware routing only.
4. Predictive triage only.
5. L1 enablement and refined escalation.
6. Capacity changes.
7. Combined future-state policy.

Test normal and adverse conditions:

- Demand increases.
- Specialist absence.
- Major software release.
- P2 arrival spike.
- Reduced staffing.
- Prediction errors or incomplete skill data.

Track:

- SLA compliance by priority.
- Average and P90 resolution time.
- Queue and handover time.
- Reassignments per ticket.
- FCR and reopen rate.
- Skill-match rate.
- Specialist low-skill utilization.
- Agent utilization and workload inequality.
- Cost, overtime, and required staffing.

Run multiple replications using common random seeds and report confidence intervals. The selected strategy should balance SLA, quality, utilization, and cost. Maximizing utilization to nearly 100% is generally undesirable because queues become unstable and there is no reserve for P1/P2 demand.

Historical decision replay can be used as a preliminary test: at each past assignment, apply the proposed rule using only agents and information available at that time. This is useful for screening but does not fully capture future queue changes, so it should not replace simulation.

### 5.2 Phased implementation

A controlled rollout should include:

1. **Data foundation**
   - Normalize skill and category taxonomies.
   - Add structured assignee, reassignment reason, availability, and SLA fields.
   - Establish ownership and review of skill profiles.

2. **Baseline**
   - Freeze definitions and calculate at least several months of baseline KPIs.
   - Segment by priority, category, and skill.

3. **Shadow mode**
   - Generate routing recommendations without executing them.
   - Compare recommendations with human choices and outcomes.
   - Review cases where the recommendation would have differed.

4. **Pilot**
   - Start with selected P2/P3 categories or one service group.
   - Retain human override and record the override reason.
   - Use a matched control group or stepped-wedge rollout where practical.

5. **Scale**
   - Expand only after SLA, quality, and workload outcomes improve.
   - Train dispatchers and agents on the new decision logic.
   - Preserve emergency and security controls.

Every automated decision should log:

- Policy/model version.
- Candidate resources.
- Eligibility results.
- Assignment scores.
- Recommended and actual assignee.
- Override and reason.
- State of queues and SLA slack at decision time.

### 5.3 Process mining dashboards

#### Operational dashboard

Near-real-time views should show:

- Open tickets by priority, skill, tier, and age.
- SLA time remaining and predicted breach risk.
- Queue length and P90 wait by skill.
- Available qualified agents.
- Current expected workload by agent.
- Tickets awaiting work after escalation or reassignment.
- Coverage gaps for critical skills.

#### Routing quality dashboard

Track:

- Initial skill-match rate.
- First-assignment resolution rate.
- Reassignments per ticket.
- Percentage with more than one reassignment.
- Same-tier versus cross-tier transfers.
- Handover latency.
- Ping-pong rate.
- Assignments to unavailable or overloaded agents.
- Category and required-skill correction rate.
- Manual override rate and outcome.

#### Resource and skill dashboard

Track:

- Active utilization and scheduled availability.
- Workload distribution and Gini/CV measures.
- Touch hours and WIP by agent/tier.
- Skill demand-to-capacity ratio.
- Specialist leverage and low-skill work share.
- Number of qualified resources per critical skill.
- Overtime and coverage gaps.
- Risk-adjusted handling and quality metrics.

#### Outcome dashboard

Track:

- P1–P4 SLA compliance.
- Resolution time split into queue, handover, and touch time.
- L1 FCR by ticket type and channel.
- Escalation yield.
- Reopen and repeat-contact rate.
- Customer satisfaction.
- SLA rate for matched versus mismatched assignments.
- SLA rate by reassignment count and workload state.

#### Process-mining views

Maintain:

- Directly-Follows Graph with frequency and waiting-time overlays.
- Variant explorer for smooth and churn paths.
- Conformance violations against routing and escalation rules.
- Handover social network with bottleneck and ping-pong edges.
- Resource-skill performance heatmap.
- Calendar heatmap for queues, utilization, and breaches.
- Case-level drill-down showing the complete assignment history.

#### Algorithm and governance monitoring

For predictive or automated routing, also monitor:

- Required-skill model precision, recall, and top-k accuracy.
- Confidence calibration.
- Recommendation acceptance and override rates.
- Outcomes of accepted versus overridden recommendations.
- Model and category drift.
- Sudden concentration of work on a small number of agents.
- Quality countermeasures such as reopen rate and customer satisfaction.

Results should be evaluated against the baseline or control group using confidence intervals and control charts. This creates a closed improvement cycle:

**mine actual behavior  identify causes  simulate policy  pilot  monitor conformance and outcomes  refine routing, skills, and capacity.**
