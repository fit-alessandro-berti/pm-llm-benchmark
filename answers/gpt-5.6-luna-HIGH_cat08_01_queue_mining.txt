## 1. Queue Identification and Characterization

### 1.1 Preparing and interpreting the event log

First, I would validate and normalize the event log:

- Pair each `START` and `COMPLETE` event for the same case, activity, and occurrence.
- Check for missing completions, duplicate events, inconsistent clocks, and negative durations.
- Standardize time zones and distinguish calendar time from clinic operating time.
- Preserve repeated activities separately, such as a second blood test or repeat physician review.
- Identify the clinical process path for each visit, since not every patient follows the same sequence.

The event log should ideally also contain:

- Actual patient arrival time
- Appointment time
- Queue-entry or “ready for service” time
- Staff, room, and equipment identifiers
- Appointment type and specialty
- Urgency and patient category
- Cancellation, no-show, rework, and escalation events

Without an arrival or queue-entry event, the first observable activity start cannot be used to calculate the patient’s initial waiting time accurately.

### 1.2 Calculating waiting and service times

For two consecutive activities for a visit:

\[
\text{Waiting time}_{i \rightarrow j}
=
\text{Start time of activity }j
-
\text{Completion time of activity }i
\]

This represents the observable gap during which the patient has completed one activity but has not yet started the next. It may include:

- Waiting for staff
- Waiting for a room or device
- Waiting for clinical authorization or handover
- Patient movement between locations
- Administrative or documentation delays
- A deliberately scheduled interval

Therefore, the initial measure should be called an **inter-stage gap**. It becomes a true queue-time measure only after confirming that the gap represents waiting rather than planned preparation, travel, or clinical observation.

For each activity:

\[
\text{Service time}_i
=
\text{Completion time}_i
-
\text{Start time}_i
\]

For a complete visit:

\[
\text{Visit duration}
=
\text{Final completion time}
-
\text{Patient arrival time}
\]

If arrival time is unavailable, the analysis can use the first recorded activity start as a proxy, but this will understate total visit duration.

For sequential activities, the total observed visit duration can be decomposed into:

\[
\text{Visit duration}
=
\text{Total service time}
+
\text{Total inter-stage waiting time}
\]

For parallel activities, the calculation must be based on the process model rather than simply taking the next event in timestamp order. For example, blood testing and ECG may run in parallel, and the relevant delay may be the time until both are complete before the next consultation.

### 1.3 Example using the illustrative visit

For visit V1001:

| Transition | Observable wait |
|---|---:|
| Registration completion to Nurse Assessment start | 6 min 35 sec |
| Nurse Assessment completion to Doctor Consultation start | 20 min 45 sec |
| Doctor Consultation completion to ECG start | 11 min 45 sec |
| ECG completion to Check-out start | 7 min 55 sec |
| **Total inter-stage waiting** | **47 min** |

The service times are:

- Registration: 6 min 30 sec
- Nurse Assessment: 9 min 50 sec
- Doctor Consultation: 24 min 35 sec
- ECG: 7 min 50 sec
- Check-out: 4 min 10 sec

The observed time from registration start to check-out completion is approximately 99 minutes 55 seconds. This illustrative case therefore spends approximately 47 minutes in inter-stage gaps and 53 minutes receiving services. The population analysis must determine whether this pattern is typical.

### 1.4 Queue metrics

I would calculate the following metrics for every important transition, such as:

- Registration  Nurse Assessment
- Nurse Assessment  Doctor Consultation
- Doctor Consultation  ECG
- Doctor Consultation  Blood Test
- Diagnostic Test  Specialist Review
- Final clinical activity  Check-out

#### Distribution of waiting time

For each transition:

- Mean waiting time
- Median waiting time
- Minimum and maximum
- 75th, 90th, and 95th percentiles
- Standard deviation and coefficient of variation
- Percentage of cases waiting longer than a defined threshold
- Percentage of cases with no wait or near-zero wait

The median shows the typical experience, while the 90th and 95th percentiles reveal severe delays that may be hidden by the average.

#### Queue frequency and burden

- Number of cases passing through the transition
- Number and percentage experiencing a wait above the threshold
- Total waiting minutes generated by that queue
- Average waiting minutes per clinic visit
- Percentage of total visit duration attributable to that queue
- Number of cases delayed beyond a clinical or operational service-level target

A queue with a 15-minute average delay affecting 10,000 patients may be more important than one with a 45-minute average delay affecting 100 patients. Thus, I would calculate:

\[
\text{Total delay burden}
=
\text{Number of affected cases}
\times
\text{Average waiting time}
\]

#### Time-dependent metrics

Waiting should also be segmented by:

- Hour of day
- Day of week
- Month or season
- Specialty
- Staff member
- Room or equipment
- Patient type
- Urgency
- Appointment type
- New versus follow-up
- Single-service versus multi-service visits

The clinic should also estimate queue length or work-in-process by time period where possible. Resource utilization can be calculated from overlapping service intervals:

\[
\text{Resource utilization}
=
\frac{\text{Time resource is occupied}}
{\text{Available resource time}}
\]

Very high utilization, particularly combined with long queues, is evidence of a capacity bottleneck.

### 1.5 Identifying the most critical queues

I would prioritize queues using several criteria rather than using the longest average wait alone.

A queue is a high-priority candidate when it has one or more of the following characteristics:

1. **High total delay burden**  
   It consumes a large number of patient-minutes across the clinic.

2. **Long median or high-percentile wait**  
   The delay is experienced by a substantial proportion of patients, not just a few outliers.

3. **High frequency**  
   The transition occurs in a large percentage of visits.

4. **Large effect on total visit duration**  
   The queue contributes significantly to the overall length of stay.

5. **Severe patient-experience impact**  
   For example, patients may tolerate a waiting period before the doctor differently from an unexplained delay after completing all clinical tests.

6. **Impact on vulnerable or urgent patients**  
   Long waits for urgent patients require particular attention, even if their volume is lower.

7. **Evidence of avoidability**  
   A queue associated with preventable handover delays or poor scheduling may be more actionable than one caused by a fixed clinical observation period.

8. **High variability**  
   A queue with a high P90 or P95 may create unpredictable patient experiences and staff escalation work.

A practical prioritization score could combine normalized values for:

- Waiting time
- Case volume
- Percentage exceeding threshold
- Urgency or patient-risk weighting
- Contribution to total visit duration
- Ease of intervention

This produces a ranked queue register for management rather than an isolated list of averages.

## 2. Root Cause Analysis

Queue mining identifies where delay occurs, but process mining is needed to understand why.

### 2.1 Resource bottlenecks

Potential causes include:

- Too few registration clerks during arrival peaks
- A nurse assigned to multiple specialties
- A physician clinic running behind schedule
- Limited ECG equipment or diagnostic rooms
- A room unavailable because cleaning or documentation is incomplete
- A particular staff member being the only person qualified for a task
- Uneven workload across staff members

The event log can be used to compare waiting time by:

- Resource
- Room
- Equipment
- Specialty
- Time of day
- Patient type
- Resource utilization

For example, if the Doctor Consultation  ECG delay is much higher when Room 3 is occupied or Tech X is assigned, the issue may be equipment or staffing capacity rather than physician performance.

Resource-performance analysis should examine both:

- Service time by resource
- Waiting time before that resource is used

A resource with short service times but very high waiting may be capacity constrained. Conversely, a resource with long and highly variable service times may be creating downstream queues.

### 2.2 Handover and dependency problems

A gap may be caused by a breakdown in coordination between:

- Registration and nursing
- Nursing and physicians
- Physicians and diagnostic technicians
- Diagnostic staff and specialist reviewers
- Clinical services and check-out

Examples include:

- The next team does not know that the patient is ready.
- Orders are incomplete or require manual clarification.
- A room is technically available but has not been released in the system.
- A test result is available but the physician is not notified.
- The patient must physically return to a location that has no visibility of their status.

Process mining can identify repeated patterns such as:

- Long gaps after a particular activity
- Additional administrative events before the next clinical activity
- Rework loops
- Repeated order corrections
- Handover delays associated with particular departments or shifts

A resource-interaction or handover analysis can reveal where cases repeatedly move between teams and where those transitions generate delay.

### 2.3 Variability in service times

Average service times alone may conceal substantial variability. A physician consultation may average 20 minutes but range from 5 to 60 minutes. The longer cases can cause queues for all subsequent patients.

I would analyze:

- Median, P90, and P95 service time by activity
- Service-time variation by patient type and specialty
- Service-time variation by resource
- Relationship between service time and downstream waiting
- Frequency of rework or repeat activities

High variability can be caused by:

- New patients requiring more history-taking
- Complex or urgent cases
- Missing information
- Incomplete pre-visit preparation
- Different staff working methods
- Unclear clinical protocols

The analysis should distinguish unavoidable clinical complexity from process-related variation.

### 2.4 Appointment scheduling and arrival patterns

The clinic may have sufficient total daily capacity but still experience queues because demand arrives in bursts.

Using appointment and arrival data, I would compare:

- Scheduled arrivals by 15- or 30-minute interval
- Actual arrivals by interval
- Available service capacity by interval
- Arrival lateness
- No-shows and cancellations
- New and follow-up appointment mix
- Expected service duration by appointment type

A typical problem is scheduling several new patients at the same time even though new visits have longer and more variable service times. Another is scheduling a physician’s appointments without considering the downstream ECG, blood test, or specialist-review capacity.

Process mining can compare the actual process variants associated with appointment types. For example, a “cardiology new patient plus ECG” pathway may require considerably more capacity than a standard follow-up visit.

### 2.5 Differences by patient type and urgency

Queue behavior should be stratified by:

- New versus follow-up
- Urgent versus normal
- Specialty
- Age or accessibility needs, where appropriate and permitted
- Single-purpose versus multi-service visits
- Appointment type

Possible findings include:

- New patients have longer registration and nurse-assessment waits.
- Follow-up patients are delayed because they are mixed with more complex new patients.
- Urgent patients are inserted into a full schedule, creating delays for both urgent and routine patients.
- A particular specialty generates more diagnostic rework.

These comparisons should not simply be used to deprioritize complex patients. They should support clinically appropriate segmentation and scheduling.

### 2.6 Process mining techniques to support diagnosis

Useful analyses include:

1. **Process discovery and directly-follows graphs**  
   Reveal the most common pathways, unexpected activity sequences, loops, and rework.

2. **Performance overlays on the process model**  
   Display average and high-percentile waiting times on each transition.

3. **Variant analysis**  
   Compare common pathways such as:
   - New versus follow-up
   - Cardiology versus orthopedics
   - Diagnostic versus non-diagnostic visits
   - Urgent versus normal visits

4. **Resource analysis**  
   Compare utilization, service time, waiting time, and case mix by staff member, room, or equipment.

5. **Bottleneck and synchronization analysis**  
   Identify activities where multiple preceding steps must be completed before a patient can proceed.

6. **Conformance checking**  
   Determine whether long waits are associated with deviations from the intended pathway.

7. **Temporal and load analysis**  
   Relate waiting time to arrival rate, concurrent workload, time of day, and day of week.

8. **Predictive or prescriptive process mining**  
   Predict which visits are likely to experience long waits and test what-if interventions.

The event log alone may not identify the definitive cause. It should be supplemented with staff rosters, room calendars, equipment availability, appointment schedules, patient arrival times, order timestamps, and short observational studies.

## 3. Data-Driven Optimization Strategies

The following interventions should be selected after confirming the dominant queues and tested through simulation or a controlled pilot.

### Strategy 1: Align staffing and resource capacity with observed demand

**Targets**

- Registration queues during arrival peaks
- Nurse Assessment queues
- Physician queues
- ECG, blood-test, or imaging queues
- Check-out queues

**Root cause addressed**

Capacity is not aligned with the arrival pattern, or a resource is operating close to saturation during specific time windows.

**Data supporting the proposal**

Use the event log to produce:

- Arrival volume by 15- or 30-minute interval
- Waiting time by interval
- Resource utilization by interval
- Service-time distribution by activity
- Queue performance by staff member and location

If the nursing queue is high between 9:00 and 10:30 while nursing utilization is consistently above 90%, a flex nurse or cross-trained staff member could be assigned during that period. If one ECG room is overloaded while another is underused, room and technician allocation may be redesigned.

Possible actions include:

- Staggering staff shifts around arrival peaks
- Cross-training registration and nursing staff
- Creating a shared pool for routine assessments
- Assigning a flex technician during high-volume periods
- Moving staff from low-demand periods to high-demand periods
- Opening a second diagnostic room only during predictable peaks

**Expected impact**

The clinic should estimate the impact using a discrete-event simulation calibrated with actual arrivals and service times. A reasonable pilot target might be:

- 15–30% reduction in the P90 wait for the affected activity
- 10–20% reduction in total visit duration during peak periods

These are targets to validate, not assumptions about the eventual result.

**Cost consideration**

This strategy can often begin with schedule changes or cross-training rather than adding permanent staff. However, utilization should not be increased to the point where small disruptions generate very long queues.

---

### Strategy 2: Redesign appointment templates and smooth arrivals

**Targets**

- Registration-to-nurse waiting
- Nurse-to-doctor waiting
- Doctor-to-diagnostic waiting
- Queues caused by large arrival bursts

**Root cause addressed**

Appointment schedules do not reflect actual service-time distributions, patient complexity, or downstream capacity.

**Data supporting the proposal**

Use historical data to determine:

- Typical and P90 service times for new and follow-up visits
- Arrival lateness and no-show patterns
- Average number of diagnostic services by appointment type
- Arrival volume by specialty and time period
- The time required between physician consultation and testing

Possible scheduling changes include:

- Separate templates for new and follow-up patients
- Longer slots for high-complexity or multi-service visits
- Staggered rather than synchronized arrival times
- Reserved buffer slots for urgent patients
- Protected capacity for ECG, blood tests, or imaging
- Scheduling diagnostic capacity immediately after likely physician completion
- Avoiding excessive clustering of appointments at the start of a session

For example, if cardiology new visits frequently require an ECG and follow-up review, the appointment template should reserve downstream capacity rather than scheduling only the physician slot.

**Expected impact**

If peak arrival congestion is the main cause, a pilot might target:

- 20% reduction in registration P90 waiting time
- 10–15% reduction in peak-period nurse waiting
- Fewer visits with waits above the defined threshold

The exact result should be estimated through replay or simulation before implementation.

**Risks**

Excessive smoothing can reduce flexibility, create idle capacity, or make it harder to accommodate urgent patients. The design should retain buffers and monitor unused capacity, cancellations, and access times.

---

### Strategy 3: Redesign the pathway to remove unnecessary serial waiting

**Targets**

- Nurse-to-doctor waiting
- Doctor-to-diagnostic waiting
- Diagnostic-to-review waiting
- Repeated movement between departments

**Root cause addressed**

Activities are performed sequentially even when some can safely occur in parallel, or patients are sent through a poorly coordinated pathway.

Potential interventions include:

- Electronic pre-registration and completion of forms before arrival
- Pre-visit insurance and demographic verification
- Completing protocol-approved tests before the physician consultation
- Performing ECG and blood collection in parallel where clinically appropriate
- Triggering test scheduling automatically when the physician order is entered
- Assigning a coordinated “one-stop” pathway for patients requiring several services
- Sending results automatically to the reviewing clinician
- Using a single care coordinator for complex visits

The intervention must be clinically validated. Not every test should be moved before the consultation, and some tests depend on the physician’s assessment.

**Data supporting the proposal**

Process variants can identify:

- Visits with a repeated serial sequence
- Large gaps between doctor completion and test start
- Tests that are predictable from appointment type
- Diagnostic services frequently performed on the same visit
- Cases where results are available long before review

**Expected impact**

For the illustrative V1001 visit, reducing the Doctor Consultation-to-ECG gap by half would remove approximately six minutes from the observed visit. At the population level, the impact depends on how often this pathway occurs. A realistic pilot objective might be:

- 20–40% reduction in the targeted inter-stage gap
- 5–15% reduction in total duration for multi-service visits

The benefit should be measured against any additional coordination or room requirements.

---

### Strategy 4: Introduce real-time readiness and handover coordination

**Targets**

- Delays caused by unclear patient status
- Handover gaps between registration, nursing, doctors, diagnostics, and check-out
- Patients waiting despite an available resource

**Root cause addressed**

Teams lack visibility into which patients are ready, which orders are complete, and which rooms or resources are available.

Possible tools include:

- A real-time patient-flow dashboard
- “Patient ready” status after each activity
- Automatic notification when an order is completed
- Room and equipment status indicators
- A pull-based queue in which staff select the next clinically appropriate patient
- Escalation alerts for patients waiting beyond a threshold
- Standardized handover checklists

The dashboard should show more than a queue count. It should show:

- Patient readiness
- Clinical priority
- Required next activity
- Elapsed waiting time
- Assigned resource
- Missing prerequisite

**Data supporting the proposal**

If long waits occur despite low measured resource utilization, the problem may be coordination rather than physical capacity. Repeated long gaps after a completed activity, particularly where no other activity is recorded, are strong candidates for this intervention.

**Expected impact**

A reasonable pilot target could be:

- 25% reduction in long handover-related waits
- Reduction in the number of patients exceeding the wait threshold
- Fewer manual escalation calls and patient-status inquiries

---

### Strategy 5: Reduce service-time variability through standard work and segmentation

**Targets**

- Downstream queues caused by long or unpredictable service times
- Registration and nurse delays for new patients
- Physician queues caused by highly variable consultations

**Root cause addressed**

Service duration varies because of incomplete preparation, different work methods, or mixing very different types of visits in one queue.

Possible actions include:

- Standardized registration and nurse-assessment checklists
- Separate fast-track processing for simple follow-up visits
- Pre-visit completion of forms and medication lists
- Standard preparation protocols for diagnostic tests
- Clear escalation rules for complex cases
- Scheduling high-complexity visits in protected longer slots
- Reviewing unusually long service episodes for process causes

**Data supporting the proposal**

The analysis should establish whether long service times are associated with:

- New patient status
- Missing information
- Particular clinicians or locations
- Specific diagnoses or specialties
- Rework and repeat documentation
- Time of day or accumulated schedule delay

**Expected impact**

Reducing service-time variability can improve both average and high-percentile waiting. A useful target is a 10–20% reduction in P90 service time for the selected activity, provided clinical completeness is maintained.

## 4. Trade-offs and Constraints

### 4.1 Shifting the bottleneck

Improving one stage may create a new bottleneck:

- Faster registration may overload nursing.
- Faster physician flow may overload ECG or laboratory services.
- Parallel processing may create congestion in rooms.
- A fast-track pathway may transfer complexity to the physician.

The process should therefore be evaluated end-to-end rather than by optimizing one queue independently.

### 4.2 Cost and staffing

Additional staff, extended hours, new equipment, or software can reduce waiting but increase cost. Lower-cost alternatives should be considered first:

- Shift redesign
- Cross-training
- Better appointment templates
- Standardized handovers
- More effective use of existing rooms and staff

The appropriate objective is not maximum resource utilization. In a highly utilized queueing system, a small increase in utilization can produce a disproportionate increase in waiting time.

### 4.3 Staff workload and sustainability

Flexible staffing and real-time escalation can increase cognitive load or produce staff fatigue. Staffing plans should be assessed for:

- Overtime
- Break compliance
- Workload balance
- Sick leave and turnover
- Staff satisfaction
- Reliance on particular individuals

### 4.4 Clinical quality and safety

Activities should only be parallelized when clinically safe. Speed should not come at the expense of:

- Diagnostic accuracy
- Appropriate triage
- Documentation quality
- Infection control
- Patient identification
- Informed consent
- Continuity of care

Urgent patients should be prioritized according to clinical rules, not simply by arrival time. The clinic should monitor whether routine patients are systematically displaced or whether urgent patients still receive timely care.

### 4.5 Equity and access

Digital registration, self-service kiosks, and fast-track pathways may disadvantage:

- Older patients
- Patients with disabilities
- Patients with limited digital access
- Patients with language barriers

Digital interventions should therefore be supplemented with assisted alternatives.

### 4.6 Balancing objectives

I would use a multi-objective evaluation framework covering:

- Waiting time
- Total visit duration
- Patient satisfaction
- Clinical quality
- Safety
- Staff workload
- Operating cost
- Equity across patient groups

Before implementation, a discrete-event simulation can compare alternatives using the actual arrival and service-time distributions. A pilot in one specialty or time block is preferable to an immediate clinic-wide change.

## 5. Measuring Success

### 5.1 Patient-flow KPIs

The primary KPIs should be measured overall and by transition:

- Mean, median, P90, and P95 waiting time
- Total inter-stage waiting time per visit
- Percentage of visits exceeding waiting-time thresholds
- Total visit duration
- Median and P90 visit duration
- Percentage of visit time spent waiting
- Queue frequency by activity
- Number of patients experiencing severe waits
- Time from arrival to first clinical contact
- Time from final clinical activity to check-out

The P90 or P95 is particularly important because reducing the average while leaving extreme delays unchanged may not improve patient experience sufficiently.

### 5.2 Capacity and operational KPIs

- Resource utilization by staff, room, and equipment
- Queue length by time interval
- Throughput per hour
- Appointment start-time adherence
- Number of patients completing visits per session
- Overtime hours
- Idle time during scheduled operating hours
- Room and equipment availability
- Cancellation and no-show rates
- Rework and repeat-activity rates

### 5.3 Quality and patient-experience KPIs

- Patient satisfaction score
- Complaints related to waiting
- Abandonment or walk-out rate
- Readmission or repeat-visit indicators where relevant
- Documentation completeness
- Diagnostic turnaround time
- Clinical escalation or adverse-event indicators
- Staff satisfaction and perceived workload

All operational measures should be stratified by:

- Specialty
- Patient type
- Urgency
- Appointment type
- Time of day
- Day of week
- Accessibility or demographic groups where appropriate and legally permitted

### 5.4 Ongoing process monitoring

The same event-log structure can support a continuous monitoring system. A useful ongoing log should contain:

- Case or visit ID
- Activity and lifecycle status
- Start and completion timestamp
- Patient arrival and appointment timestamp
- Resource, room, and equipment
- Specialty and patient category
- Urgency
- Order and readiness status
- Cancellation, rework, and exception events

A dashboard can display:

- Current queue size
- Current and recent waiting times
- P90 waiting by transition
- Patients approaching an escalation threshold
- Resource utilization
- Daily and weekly trend lines
- Comparison with baseline and target

Control charts or statistical process-control methods can distinguish normal variation from genuine deterioration. Alerts should be triggered when, for example:

- P90 nurse waiting exceeds target for several consecutive sessions
- A resource’s utilization remains above a safe operating level
- A new process variant appears with unusually long duration
- A particular patient group experiences a disproportionate increase in waiting

### 5.5 Evaluation design

Success should be measured using more than a simple before-and-after average. The clinic should:

1. Establish a baseline using several weeks or months of historical data.
2. Pilot the intervention in a defined specialty, shift, or day.
3. Compare with a control area where feasible.
4. Adjust for patient mix, urgency, seasonality, and volume.
5. Review both averages and high-percentile outcomes.
6. Monitor quality, cost, and staff workload alongside waiting time.
7. Reassess the process after several weeks to detect bottleneck migration.

A successful intervention is one that produces sustained reductions in patient waiting and visit duration without unacceptable increases in cost, staff burden, clinical risk, or inequity.
