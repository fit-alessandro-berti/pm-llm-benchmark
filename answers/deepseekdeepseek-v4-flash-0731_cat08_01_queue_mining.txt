# Data-Driven Queue Mining and Patient Flow Optimization at a Multi-Specialty Outpatient Clinic

## 1. Queue Identification and Characterization

### 1.1 How to Calculate Waiting Times from Event Log Data

The most important property of this event log is that it contains both **START** and **COMPLETE** timestamps for each activity. This allows us to separate **service time** from **waiting time**.

For a single patient visit:

- **Service time** for an activity = `COMPLETE timestamp  START timestamp`
- **Waiting time between two consecutive activities** = `START of next activity  COMPLETE of previous activity`

For example, if Nurse Assessment completes at `09:25:10` and Doctor Consultation starts at `09:45:55`, the patient waited **20 minutes 45 seconds** for the doctor.

In practice, the calculation would be done by:

1. Separating the event log into START and COMPLETE events.
2. Sorting all events by Case ID and timestamp.
3. Pairing each START with its corresponding COMPLETE for the same activity.
4. For each case, identifying the previous completed activity and the next started activity.
5. Computing the difference between those timestamps.

This defines the **effective patient queue time** between clinical/administrative stages. It includes waiting in the waiting room, waiting in an exam room for a physician, waiting for a phlebotomist, and waiting for a room or machine to become available.

One caveat: the event log snippet does **not** include an “Arrival” or “Scheduled Appointment Time” event. Therefore, the wait **before Registration** cannot be fully measured unless we add an arrival/check-in timestamp or use the scheduled appointment time as a proxy. If the clinic can record actual arrival time, this should be added; otherwise, we can estimate initial wait as `Registration START  Scheduled Appointment Time`.

### 1.2 Key Queue Metrics

For each transition, or “edge,” in the patient journey — such as Registration  Nurse Assessment, Nurse Assessment  Doctor Consultation, Doctor Consultation  ECG Test, ECG Test  Check-out — I would calculate:

- **Average waiting time** — identifies the overall burden.
- **Median waiting time** — robust to outliers and better represents the typical patient.
- **Maximum waiting time** — highlights extreme failures.
- **90th and 95th percentile waiting time** — captures the worst common experiences.
- **Percentage of cases exceeding a threshold** — e.g., % of patients waiting more than 15, 30, or 60 minutes.
- **Queue frequency** — the number of cases that pass through each queue over the six-month period.
- **Total waiting burden** — `sum of all waiting times` for a queue, because a moderate wait that affects thousands of patients may be more important than a long wait affecting only a few.

In addition, the event log can be used to reconstruct **queue lengths over time**:

- For any time \(t\), the number of patients waiting for activity B is the number of cases whose previous activity has completed before \(t\) but whose activity B has not yet started.
- This can be plotted as a queue-length chart by hour and day to identify congestion periods.

### 1.3 Identifying the Most Critical Queues

I would not rely only on average waiting time. A queue with a high average but low volume may be less impactful than a moderately long queue affecting most patients. Instead, I would use a **prioritization framework** that considers:

1. **Wait-time severity** — high mean, median, and especially 90th/95th percentile waits.
2. **Frequency/volume** — how many patients are affected.
3. **Violation rate** — percentage of patients exceeding the clinic’s wait-time target.
4. **Downstream impact** — a queue before the doctor has more impact on total visit duration than a queue before check-out, because it delays everything that follows.
5. **Patient-type and urgency impact** — long waits for urgent or vulnerable patients are more serious than long waits for low-acuity follow-up patients.

I would rank queues using a score such as:

> **Queue Criticality Score** = weighted combination of average wait, 90th percentile wait, violation rate, and patient volume

For example, if the queue between Nurse Assessment and Doctor Consultation has:

- a high median wait,
- a high 90th percentile wait,
- affects 80% of patients,
- and delays downstream activities,

it would likely be the most critical queue.

The goal is to identify not just where the longest wait is, but where the **largest total amount of patient waiting time** can be removed with reasonable effort.

## 2. Root Cause Analysis

After identifying where queues occur, the next step is to understand **why** they occur. Many different process issues can produce the same queue.

### 2.1 Potential Root Causes

#### Resource Bottlenecks

A queue often forms because a resource — a doctor, nurse, clerk, technician, or room — is saturated. If a doctor’s utilization is consistently above 85%, patients will wait even if upstream activities run perfectly. The event log can show:

- Total busy time per resource.
- Number of activities completed per resource per day.
- Gaps between activities where the resource is idle.
- Room/equipment occupancy.

#### Activity Dependencies and Handovers

Many waits are caused by poor coordination between activities. For example, a nurse may complete an assessment, but the doctor may not be notified until much later. This is not necessarily a capacity problem; it is a communication/handover problem. The event log can identify handover patterns by looking at:

- Which resource completes the previous activity.
- Which resource performs the next activity.
- The waiting time between every pair of resources.

If large waits occur only when certain nurses hand over to certain doctors, the issue may be informal communication or scheduling rather than overall capacity.

#### Variability in Service Times

Even if the average doctor consultation is 15 minutes, some consultations take 40 minutes. A single long consultation causes a ripple effect, making every following patient wait longer. The event log can calculate:

- The distribution of service times for each activity.
- The coefficient of variation of service times.
- Whether certain providers or patient types have unusually high variability.

High variability often requires different scheduling or buffer capacity strategies.

#### Appointment Scheduling Policies

Fixed 15-minute appointment slots for every patient type can be a major cause of queues. New patients, urgent patients, and some specialties simply take longer. The event log can reveal:

- Actual consultation duration by specialty, provider, and patient type.
- Number of patients scheduled per hour.
- Start-time punctuality: whether the physician starts on time.
- Whether the previous patient’s overrun delays the next patient.

#### Patient Arrival Patterns

Patients may arrive in a large wave before the clinic opens or before scheduled appointment times. This creates artificial peak demand. The event log can reconstruct arrival patterns from Registration START timestamps. If 60% of registrations occur between 8:30 and 10:00 AM, but staffing is flat, queues are predictable.

#### Differences by Patient Type or Urgency

Urgent patients may be inserted into the process, interrupting normal flow. New patients may require longer registration and nursing assessment than follow-up patients. The event log can stratify all queue metrics by:

- New vs. follow-up patient.
- Urgent vs. normal.
- Specialty or clinical pathway.
- Time of day / day of week.

### 2.2 Process Mining Techniques for Root Cause Analysis

Beyond simple wait-time calculation, several process mining techniques would help pinpoint root causes:

- **Resource Utilization Analysis**: Identify which resources are over-utilized and when they are idle. If a resource has idle time while patients are waiting for them, the problem is flow, not capacity.

- **Dotted Chart Analysis**: Plot each event over time, colored by resource or case. This makes it visually obvious when queues build and when resources are idle.

- **Performance Spectrum**: Show the timeline of waiting and service times for each process instance. This helps identify whether waits are periodic, random, or caused by extraordinary cases.

- **Variant Analysis**: Discover the most common process paths. For example, some patients go `Registration  Nurse Assessment  Doctor Consultation  Check-out`, while others go `Registration  Doctor Consultation  Blood Test  Doctor Consultation  Check-out`. Comparing waiting times across these variants can reveal process design problems.

- **Bottleneck Analysis**: Calculate the total time each activity contributes to the overall visit duration, including waiting. This identifies the “critical path” of the patient journey.

- **Handover/Social Network Mining**: Analyze the frequency and waiting time of handoffs between specific nurses, doctors, clerks, and technicians. This can reveal communication bottlenecks.

- **Queueing Theory Using Event Log Data**: Fit statistical distributions to arrival patterns and service times. Use Little’s Law and queueing models to estimate whether observed waits match expected waits given capacity. If the observed waits are much higher than the theoretical model predicts, the cause is process inefficiency, not just lack of capacity.

## 3. Data-Driven Optimization Strategies

Based on the likely root causes, I would propose the following concrete, data-driven strategies. Each would need to be validated and refined with the actual event log.

### Strategy 1: Demand-Driven Staffing and Cross-Training

**Targets:** Registration, Nurse Assessment, and Check-out queues.

**Root Cause:** Staffing schedules are not aligned with patient arrival peaks. Many clinics open with a wave of patients at 8:30–9:00 AM, but clerks and nurses may start later or take breaks during peak times.

**What the data would show:** Queue-length curves by 15-minute intervals show long waits during specific hours, while resource analysis shows low utilization during other hours.  
For example, the Registration queue may peak at 20 patients at 9:00 AM and be empty at 11:00 AM.

**Strategy:**

- Use the event log to create hourly demand curves for each activity.
- Adjust staff shift start times, lunch breaks, and shift end times to match demand.
- Create a small pool of cross-trained administrative/clinical staff who can move between Registration, Nurse Assessment, and Check-out when queues exceed thresholds.
- Use simple optimization or simulation to find the lowest-cost staffing plan that meets wait-time targets.

**Potential Impact:**

- Reduces peak registration and assessment waits by an estimated **20–40%** without increasing total staff hours.
- Improves patient perception because the first impression is faster.

### Strategy 2: Queue-Aware Appointment Scheduling with Differentiated Slots

**Targets:** Doctor Consultation queues and downstream waiting times.

**Root Cause:** The appointment system uses fixed slot lengths that do not match real service-time distributions. A 15-minute slot may be too short for new patients or urgent consultations, causing overruns and delays.

**What the data would show:**  
Consultation duration distributions vary by provider, specialty, and patient type. For example:

- New Cardiology patients: average 28 minutes, with some taking 45 minutes.
- Follow-up Cardiology patients: average 12 minutes.

Yet both may be booked for 15-minute slots.

**Strategy:**

- Redesign appointment templates using actual duration distributions.
- Book new patients into longer slots and follow-ups into shorter slots.
- Reserve a certain number of daily slots for urgent add-on patients.
- Use predictive no-show rates to overbook low-show periods carefully, without creating new bottlenecks.
- If some doctors are consistently behind, adjust their scheduling intervals or reduce the number of patients per half-day.

**Potential Impact:**

- Reduces average doctor wait time by **25–40%**.
- Improves schedule reliability and reduces the ripple effect of late-running doctors.
- Maintains access because scheduling capacity is matched to actual capacity.

### Strategy 3: Protocol-Based Pre-Consult Diagnostics and Parallel Testing

**Targets:** Diagnostic test queues after Doctor Consultation, and total visit duration.

**Root Cause:** The process is unnecessarily sequential. Many patients see a doctor, then wait for a blood test or ECG, and then wait again only to have the doctor interpret the result. For many predictable conditions, some tests could be done before the consultation, or at least in parallel.

**What the data would show:**  
Variant analysis reveals that a large percentage of patients in certain specialty groups follow the same path:

`Registration  Nurse Assessment  Doctor Consultation  ECG  Doctor Consultation or Review`

For many of these patients, the ECG or blood test was clinically predictable from the beginning. The event log also shows significant waiting time after the initial doctor consultation.

**Strategy:**

- Work with clinicians to define evidence-based protocols for pre-consult testing (e.g., ECG for chest pain, blood work for diabetes follow-ups, X-ray for orthopedic injuries).
- At Registration or Nurse Assessment, activate these pre-consult orders for eligible patients.
- In some cases, tests can be performed while the patient is waiting for the doctor, instead of after the doctor visit.
- Alternatively, if the clinic has multiple diagnostic activities, perform them in parallel rather than sequentially.

**Potential Impact:**

- Reduces total visit duration for eligible patients by **20–30%**.
- Reduces post-consult test waiting time and eliminates the need for a second doctor review in some cases.
- Improves patient experience and reduces unnecessary movement.

This strategy must be clinically safe and limited to conditions where testing is clearly indicated.

### Strategy 4: Real-Time Queue Coordination and Pull-Based Patient Flow

**Targets:** Handover waits, especially between Nurse Assessment and Doctor Consultation, and between Doctor Consultation and Diagnostic Tests.

**Root Cause:** Patient flow is often “push-based.” When one activity finishes, the patient is sent to the next waiting area, but the next resource may not know they are ready. The event log may show that doctors have idle time while patients are waiting, because the notification process is slow.

**Strategy:**

- Build a real-time “waiting-time and queue status” dashboard using the event log.
- Use automated alerts to notify nurses, doctors, and technicians when a patient has completed the previous activity.
- Use a “pull” system: when a doctor becomes available, the system selects the next appropriate patient from the queue based on readiness and patient priority.
- Identify queue thresholds: if a certain queue exceeds its 90th-percentile wait, activate a flow coordinator or reassign available staff.

**Potential Impact:**

- Reduces handoff and communication-related waiting times by **15–25%**.
- Improves utilization of existing exam rooms and physician time.
- Low incremental cost if the event log system is already in place.

## 4. Consideration of Trade-offs and Constraints

Every optimization strategy has potential negative side effects. These must be anticipated and managed.

### Trade-offs of Staffing Strategy

- Shifting staff to peak times may reduce coverage during lower-demand periods, potentially increasing waits for non-peak patients.
- Cross-training requires time and may cause temporary staff dissatisfaction.
- Overstaffing peak periods without reducing non-peak coverage could increase payroll costs.

**Mitigation:** Use simulation to set minimum staffing levels for all periods. Offer flexible scheduling incentives. Use cross-training to create flexibility, not to increase total labor cost.

### Trade-offs of Appointment Scheduling Redesign

- Longer slots for new patients may reduce the total number of patients a doctor can see, potentially increasing waiting time for appointments.
- Reserving urgent slots means fewer available slots for elective patients.
- Dynamic overbooking based on no-show rates can backfire if patients unexpectedly show up, creating longer waits.

**Mitigation:** Do not simply extend all slots; use data to find the optimal mix. Use probability-based overbooking cautiously and monitor actual show-up rates. Re-evaluate monthly.

### Trade-offs of Pre-Consult Diagnostics

- More pre-consult tests could increase laboratory and radiology workload, possibly shifting the bottleneck from the doctor to diagnostic services.
- Unnecessary tests increase cost and potentially harm patients through false positives, incidental findings, or additional testing.
- Clinicians may be reluctant to delegate order decisions to nurses or intake staff.

**Mitigation:** Restrict pre-consult testing to strong clinical protocols validated by local physicians. Monitor test appropriateness and positive yield. Add resources only where the data shows the diagnostic service is the new bottleneck.

### Trade-offs of Real-Time Queue Coordination

- Technology dashboards can create alert fatigue if too many notifications are triggered.
- If the “pull” system prioritizes patients by elapsed wait, it may unintentionally delay urgent patients.
- There is a risk of spending money on IT systems without solving the underlying capacity problem.

**Mitigation:** Involve frontline staff in designing the dashboard. Use tiered alerts with clear escalation rules. Always combine technology with process redesign and capacity planning.

### Balancing Wait-Time Reduction, Costs, and Quality

The clinic should not aim for zero waiting time, because that would require extremely high resource idle capacity and would be cost-inefficient. Instead, the goal should be:

- Set **clinically and experientially acceptable wait-time targets** by patient type and urgency.
- Compare the **cost per minute of waiting time reduced** for each strategy.
- Use simulation or a “digital twin” of the clinic to test strategies before implementation.
- Monitor **clinical quality indicators** to ensure that reducing waiting time does not reduce thoroughness of care.

For example, if adding one extra technician reduces diagnostic wait time by 5 minutes per patient, while changing the scheduling template reduces it by 15 minutes per patient with no additional labor cost, the scheduling intervention should be implemented first.

## 5. Measuring Success

### 5.1 Key Performance Indicators

I would define KPIs at multiple levels:

#### Queue-Level KPIs
- Average, median, and 90th percentile waiting time for each activity transition.
- Percentage of patients waiting longer than target thresholds.
- Maximum waiting time.
- Average and maximum queue length by time of day.

#### Visit-Level KPIs
- Total visit duration from arrival/check-in to check-out, stratified by patient type and urgency.
- Percentage of visits completed within target time (e.g., 60, 90, or 120 minutes depending on complexity).
- Wait time as a percentage of total visit duration.

#### Resource-Level KPIs
- Utilization of doctors, nurses, technicians, exam rooms, and diagnostic equipment.
- Idle time between activities for critical resources.
- Overtime hours.

#### Patient Experience KPIs
- Patient satisfaction survey scores related to waiting.
- Number and type of complaints about waiting times.
- Percentage of patients leaving before seeing a physician, if measurable.

#### Clinical Quality and Cost KPIs
- Unplanned return visit rate within 72 hours to ensure safety.
- Diagnostic test yield and appropriateness rate.
- Cost per patient visit.
- Labor cost per visit and diagnostic cost per visit.

### 5.2 Ongoing Process Monitoring

After implementing the strategies, I would build a **continuous process-mining dashboard** using the same event log structure.

This dashboard would:

- Automatically recalculate queue KPIs daily, weekly, and monthly.
- Display statistical process control charts for key wait-time metrics. If a KPI moves outside the normal control limits, the clinic can investigate quickly.
- Compare current performance to the pre-intervention baseline.
- Re-run variant analysis and bottleneck analysis monthly to detect whether the bottleneck has shifted to another stage.
- Flag unexpected new queues, such as increased waiting for lab tests after pre-consult diagnostic orders are introduced.

In addition, I would recommend a **pilot-and-scale approach**:

1. Implement each strategy in one department or one patient stream.
2. Compare its performance with a control group using the same KPIs.
3. If the pilot shows statistically significant improvement without adverse effects, scale it to other departments.
4. Continue monitoring for at least 90 days to ensure improvements are sustainable.

Finally, the event log should be used as a **continuous learning loop**: discover new process variants, identify emerging bottlenecks, simulate additional improvements, implement, and monitor again. This transforms process mining from a one-time diagnostic project into a sustainable patient-flow management system.
