## 1. Queue Identification and Characterization

### 1.1 Define waiting time from the event log

For each visit (case), reconstruct the ordered sequence of activities using timestamps. Because the log contains both activity start and completion times, distinguish:

- **Service/processing time** for activity \(A\):  
  \[
  \text{Service Time}_A = \text{Complete}_A - \text{Start}_A
  \]

- **Inter-activity waiting (queue) time** between activity \(A\) and the next activity \(B\):  
  \[
  \text{Queue Time}_{A \rightarrow B} = \text{Start}_B - \text{Complete}_A
  \]

For example:

- Registration completes at 09:08:45
- Nurse Assessment starts at 09:15:20

\[
\text{Queue Time}_{Registration \rightarrow Nurse} = 6\text{ min }35\text{ sec}
\]

This is the patient’s observed wait between completing one care step and beginning the next. It may include time in a physical waiting room, delays caused by handoffs, waiting for a room, clinician, diagnostic equipment, results, or administrative preparation.

The analysis should also calculate:

- **Arrival-to-registration wait**, if arrival/check-in time is available.
- **Post-consultation waits**, such as consultation-to-ECG or consultation-to-checkout.
- **Total waiting time per visit**: sum of all valid inter-activity queue times.
- **Total visit duration**:
  \[
  \text{Last Completion Timestamp} - \text{First Start Timestamp}
  \]
- **Value-added ratio**:
  \[
  \frac{\text{Total Service Time}}{\text{Total Visit Duration}}
  \]
  This highlights how much of the visit is actual care versus waiting.

### 1.2 Important data preparation steps

Before calculating queues, validate the event data:

1. Match each activity start to its corresponding completion for each visit.
2. Standardize activity names, specialty labels, resources, locations, and timestamp time zones.
3. Detect missing, duplicate, reversed, or implausibly long timestamps.
4. Treat overnight gaps carefully; a delay from one day to the next may represent a planned follow-up rather than a queue.
5. Separate planned waiting from operational waiting where possible. For example, a planned two-hour gap before a diagnostic test may not be comparable to an unexpected 45-minute same-day delay.
6. Identify parallel activities. Some activities may overlap or be clinically independent. A simple sequential calculation can incorrectly classify overlap as delay unless the care pathway is modeled appropriately.
7. Segment the analysis by clinic, specialty, location, day of week, time of day, urgency, patient type, and route/variant.

### 1.3 Queue metrics to calculate

For each transition, such as Registration  Nurse Assessment or Nurse Assessment  Cardiology Consultation, calculate:

#### Central tendency and distribution
- Mean/average queue time
- Median queue time
- Minimum and maximum queue time
- Standard deviation or interquartile range
- 75th, 90th, and 95th percentile queue times

The median is important because waiting-time distributions are typically skewed. The 90th percentile is especially useful for patient experience: it answers, “How long do the longest-waiting 10% of patients wait?”

#### Frequency and exposure
- Number of visits passing through each transition
- Percentage of all visits exposed to that queue
- Percentage of patients waiting more than a threshold, such as:
  - >15 minutes
  - >30 minutes
  - >60 minutes
- Queue occurrence by time block, day of week, specialty, and site
- Total cumulative queue hours generated by each transition:
  \[
  \sum \text{Queue Time}_{A \rightarrow B}
  \]
  A queue with a moderate average delay but very high volume may be more important than a rare extreme delay.

#### Patient-level impact
- Average total wait per visit
- Percentage of visit duration spent waiting
- Longest individual visit duration
- Number of queues encountered per visit
- Relationship between waiting time and patient experience measures, complaints, no-shows, abandonment, or follow-up adherence, if available

#### Resource-oriented indicators
For resources associated with the next activity:
- Resource utilization and utilization by hour
- Number of patients waiting for each resource
- Average and percentile wait before each staff member, room, modality, or specialty
- Service-time variation by resource
- Handover delays between specific resources, teams, or locations

### 1.4 Identify the most critical queues

The clinic should avoid prioritizing queues only by average delay. A practical prioritization score should combine severity, volume, inequity, and downstream impact.

A queue is likely critical when it has one or more of the following characteristics:

1. **High mean and high percentile waits**  
   For example, Nurse Assessment  Doctor Consultation may average 32 minutes, with a 90th percentile of 70 minutes.

2. **High patient volume**  
   A 12-minute registration delay affecting 4,000 visits per month may create more total lost patient time than a 40-minute delay affecting 30 visits.

3. **High cumulative waiting hours**  
   Rank transitions by:
   \[
   \text{Number of affected visits} \times \text{Average wait}
   \]

4. **Large proportion of patients exceeding the service standard**  
   Example: 45% of patients wait more than 30 minutes for a physician.

5. **Disproportionate impact on priority groups**  
   Long waits for urgent, elderly, complex, new, mobility-limited, or high-risk patients should receive additional weight.

6. **Strong association with long total visit duration or complaints**  
   A queue may be especially important if it drives dissatisfaction, missed diagnostic completion, or patients leaving before checkout.

7. **Downstream propagation**  
   A delayed physician consultation can push diagnostics, specialist review, and checkout later into the day, creating a cascade.

A useful visual output is a queue heat map showing transition-level median and 90th-percentile waits by hour of day and day of week. Another is a process map with links colored and sized by cumulative waiting time.

## 2. Root Cause Analysis

Queue mining identifies where patients wait; root cause analysis determines why. The clinic should analyze queues in relation to resource capacity, workflow design, patient mix, and timing.

### 2.1 Likely root causes

#### A. Resource bottlenecks

A queue before an activity often indicates insufficient effective capacity at that stage, but this must be validated. Common examples include:

- Too few registration clerks during morning arrival peaks
- Nurses assigned to rooming, phone triage, vaccines, and patient assessment simultaneously
- Physician schedules with overlapping appointments or insufficient buffers
- Limited exam rooms preventing nurses or physicians from starting work
- Scarce ECG, X-ray, phlebotomy, or technician capacity
- A single checkout clerk handling payments, referrals, scheduling, and records requests

The key distinction is between:

- **True capacity shortage:** demand exceeds available capacity.
- **Capacity timing mismatch:** enough capacity exists overall, but not at the times demand arrives.
- **Workflow-induced idle time:** resources are available but work is delayed by incomplete handoffs, missing orders, room turnover, or administrative dependencies.

#### B. Activity dependencies and handovers

Patients may wait because the next activity cannot begin until prerequisites are completed:

- A provider must enter an order before diagnostics can be scheduled.
- A nurse must complete triage before room assignment.
- Diagnostic results may require review before checkout.
- A physician may need a room, chart preparation, interpreter, or prior authorization.
- Staff may not be notified promptly that a patient is ready.

Handoffs are particularly vulnerable to delays when responsibility is unclear or information is transferred manually.

#### C. High variability in service times

Long or highly variable activity durations can create queues even when average capacity appears sufficient. Examples:

- New patients need more history taking than follow-up patients.
- Complex consultations take longer than scheduled.
- Urgent cases interrupt routine work.
- Certain specialties have unpredictable procedures or decision complexity.
- Some clinicians have systematically longer service times due to case mix, documentation style, or practice patterns.

A physician whose average consultation is close to the appointment slot length may still generate substantial delay if variability is high and no buffer exists.

#### D. Appointment scheduling policies

Common scheduling causes include:

- Too many patients scheduled at the same start time.
- Uniform appointment lengths despite different patient needs.
- New patients given the same duration as follow-up visits.
- Diagnostics scheduled independently from consultation timing.
- A provider’s schedule not reflecting historical service-time distributions.
- Insufficient buffers for urgent add-ons, late arrivals, complex patients, or documentation.
- Morning schedule compression, where minor early delays accumulate through the day.

#### E. Patient arrival patterns

Patients may arrive in clusters because of:

- Standard appointment times such as every 15 minutes on the hour
- Instructions to arrive 30 minutes early
- Transport schedules
- Morning preference
- Patients arriving early to avoid parking or traffic uncertainty
- Late arrivals being inserted into an already constrained schedule

Arrival variability can overwhelm registration and nursing even if daily staffing is adequate.

#### F. Patient and pathway differences

The same queue may affect groups differently:

- New patients may require longer registration, insurance verification, and nurse assessment.
- Follow-up patients may be delayed behind more complex new patients.
- Urgent patients may appropriately bypass queues, creating delay for routine patients.
- Certain specialties may require more diagnostics, room turnover, or physician review.
- Patients requiring interpreters, mobility assistance, or financial counseling may have longer pathways.

These differences should be assessed without treating clinically necessary variation as waste.

### 2.2 Process mining techniques to diagnose causes

#### Resource analysis

Link each activity to its staff member, room, equipment, or team. Analyze:

- Utilization over time
- Number of simultaneous assignments
- Idle periods despite patient queues
- Workload by resource and shift
- Queue time before each resource
- Differences in service time and queue creation across comparable resources

For example, if patients wait 40 minutes for ECG only between 10:00 and 12:00 while the ECG room is consistently occupied, the issue is likely capacity or scheduling. If the room is often idle while patients wait, the cause may be delayed order entry, batching, communication, or routing.

#### Bottleneck analysis

Identify activities where high workload, high utilization, long service times, and large incoming queues coincide. A bottleneck should be assessed dynamically because it may change by hour or day.

Useful comparisons include:

- Arrival rate to a service stage versus completion rate
- Queue length trends over time
- Resource occupancy when queues occur
- Whether downstream stages are blocked or starved

#### Variant analysis

Group visits into common process variants, for example:

- Registration  Nurse  Physician  Checkout
- Registration  Nurse  Physician  ECG  Physician Review  Checkout
- Registration  Nurse  Lab  Physician  Checkout

Compare waiting times, service times, route frequency, rework, and total duration across variants. This can reveal that a minority pathway—such as cardiology visits requiring same-day ECG—creates disproportionate delays.

#### Performance analysis by segment

Analyze queue metrics by:

- Specialty
- Provider
- Site
- Day of week
- Hour of day
- Patient type
- Urgency
- Referral source
- Diagnostic requirement
- New versus follow-up status

For instance, a long doctor wait may be concentrated in one specialty on Monday mornings rather than being a clinic-wide problem.

#### Conformance checking

Compare observed patient flows against intended workflows or scheduling rules. Conformance analysis can identify:

- Unplanned repeated assessments
- Rework or duplicate registration
- Patients skipping expected steps
- Diagnostic testing occurring late in the visit when it should have been pre-ordered
- Unnecessary handoffs or loops
- Delayed or inconsistent completion of required documentation

#### Correlation and predictive modeling

Use statistical or machine-learning models to identify variables associated with long waits. Possible predictors include:

- Appointment hour
- Arrival lateness/earliness
- Provider
- Specialty
- New/follow-up status
- Urgency
- Number of planned diagnostics
- Previous activity duration
- Resource load at arrival
- Queue length at each stage

This does not replace operational judgment, but it can quantify which factors have the strongest relationship with delay.

## 3. Data-Driven Optimization Strategies

The specific interventions should be selected after validating the clinic’s measured bottlenecks. The following are concrete strategies that can be tested through pilot programs, simulation, and phased rollout.

### Strategy 1: Align staffing and room capacity with observed demand peaks

**Target queues:**  
- Arrival/registration  nurse assessment  
- Nurse assessment  physician consultation  
- Consultation  diagnostics  
- Diagnostics  checkout, depending on the constrained resource

**Likely root cause addressed:**  
Capacity timing mismatch: staffing or rooms may be adequate across the full day but inadequate during predictable demand peaks.

**Data support:**  
Use six months of event logs to produce hourly arrival curves, queue lengths, service completion rates, resource utilization, and wait percentiles. For example, the analysis may show that registration wait exceeds 20 minutes from 08:00–09:30 on weekdays, while clerks are underutilized after 14:00. Or it may show that nurse-to-doctor queues peak when most physicians begin clinic sessions simultaneously.

**Interventions:**
- Stagger clerk, nurse, technician, and support staff shifts around actual arrival peaks.
- Add part-time or flex coverage only during identified peak intervals instead of hiring full-time staff.
- Cross-train selected staff to support registration, rooming, checkout, or diagnostics during surges.
- Reserve rooms according to specialty and expected patient volume.
- Use a “float” role to clear bottlenecks, room patients, coordinate diagnostics, or manage handoffs.
- Stagger provider start times rather than scheduling all providers to begin at the same hour.

**Expected impact:**  
If the data show that a morning peak causes most registration delays, reallocating one staff member during the two-hour peak could plausibly reduce average registration-to-nurse wait by 20–40%, depending on baseline capacity and arrival variability. The target should be established through queueing simulation rather than assumed.

**Validation approach:**  
Pilot the revised staffing pattern on selected days, then compare risk-adjusted median and 90th-percentile waits with matched baseline days.

---

### Strategy 2: Redesign appointment templates using patient type, specialty, and expected service-time variability

**Target queues:**  
- Nurse assessment  physician consultation  
- Physician consultation  diagnostics  
- End-of-day delays and cascading queues across the clinic

**Likely root cause addressed:**  
Appointment templates that do not match actual consultation duration, clinical complexity, diagnostic needs, or patient arrival patterns.

**Data support:**  
Analyze actual service times and downstream delays by patient type, specialty, provider, visit reason, urgency, and diagnostic pathway. For example, logs may show that:

- New cardiology patients require a median 35-minute consultation, while follow-ups require 15 minutes.
- Certain specialties have high variability and need buffers.
- Visits requiring ECG create predictable post-consultation queues.
- Delays grow progressively through the day, indicating overbooked or overly tight schedules.

**Interventions:**
- Create distinct appointment types for new, follow-up, complex, procedure-related, urgent, and diagnostic-requiring patients.
- Use historical percentiles, not just average duration, to determine slot lengths.
- Add targeted buffers after high-variability appointments or before known peak periods.
- Limit simultaneous arrivals by smoothing appointment start times.
- Schedule urgent/add-on capacity deliberately rather than absorbing it randomly into the day.
- Pre-book predictable diagnostics in coordinated slots or perform them before the consultation when clinically appropriate.
- Reduce double-booking where the data demonstrate it creates downstream congestion.

**Expected impact:**  
The intended result is not necessarily fewer booked visits, but more reliable flow. A well-designed template can reduce late-session physician queues and improve the 90th-percentile waiting time materially, potentially by 15–30% in affected specialties. Any capacity impact must be assessed alongside no-show rates, provider productivity, and care quality.

**Validation approach:**  
Use discrete-event simulation or a controlled pilot comparing the current schedule template against a revised template. Measure visits completed, total visit time, physician utilization, and wait percentiles.

---

### Strategy 3: Move appropriate work upstream and parallelize diagnostic pathways

**Target queues:**  
- Consultation  ECG/lab/X-ray  
- Diagnostic test  physician review  
- Physician review  checkout

**Likely root cause addressed:**  
Sequential, order-dependent workflow in which patients wait for a provider to complete a consultation before a predictable diagnostic test can be initiated.

**Data support:**  
Variant analysis may show that a common pathway—such as cardiology consultation followed by ECG—has a long post-consultation wait, especially when the ECG service is busy. If the diagnostic is frequently required for a defined patient group, it may be suitable for protocol-driven pre-ordering or pre-visit planning.

**Interventions:**
- Establish clinical protocols for pre-visit screening and standing orders where medically appropriate.
- Identify patients likely to need labs, ECGs, imaging, or questionnaires before the appointment.
- Perform suitable diagnostics before consultation, allowing results to be available during the provider visit.
- Use electronic worklists and automated routing to send an immediate notification to the diagnostic team once a patient is ready.
- Reserve diagnostic capacity for same-day patients whose clinical pathway requires it.
- Enable nurse-led preparation, medication reconciliation, questionnaires, or education while patients wait for the physician.
- Ensure any parallelization is clinically approved and does not create unnecessary testing.

**Expected impact:**  
For patients requiring a standard diagnostic test, moving the test earlier can remove an entire sequential waiting stage and reduce total visit duration. If consultation-to-ECG waiting averages 25 minutes and the test is safely completed before the visit for a selected group, much of that wait could be eliminated. The actual reduction depends on diagnostic capacity and clinical eligibility.

**Validation approach:**  
Pilot in one specialty, compare end-to-end visit duration and diagnostic turnaround, and monitor for unnecessary tests, repeat tests, or clinical quality concerns.

---

### Strategy 4: Implement real-time patient-flow coordination and digital queue visibility

**Target queues:**  
- All handoff-related queues, particularly nurse  physician, physician  diagnostics, and diagnostics  checkout

**Likely root cause addressed:**  
Delayed handoffs, lack of visibility into patient readiness, manual communication, uncertainty over room status, and staff inability to see emerging queues.

**Data support:**  
The event log may show periods where patients wait despite an apparently available downstream resource. This pattern suggests a coordination rather than pure capacity issue. It may also reveal long differences among specific resource handoffs.

**Interventions:**
- Create a real-time operational dashboard showing patients waiting by stage, elapsed wait, urgency, room status, and downstream capacity.
- Establish escalation rules, such as alerting a charge nurse or flow coordinator when a patient exceeds a queue threshold.
- Use electronic notifications when registration, triage, diagnostics, or room turnover is complete.
- Introduce standardized “ready for next step” status updates.
- Assign a daily flow coordinator during peak periods.
- Use patient messaging to inform patients of expected delays and preparation instructions.

**Expected impact:**  
This strategy is particularly effective where delays are caused by communication and handoff failure rather than lack of capacity. It may reduce long-tail waits and improve 90th-percentile performance even if average wait changes modestly. A reasonable pilot goal could be a 10–20% reduction in avoidable handoff delay.

**Validation approach:**  
Compare queue times during periods with and without flow-coordinator coverage. Track alerts, response times, and the proportion of waits exceeding the escalation threshold.

---

### Strategy 5: Simplify registration and checkout through pre-visit and self-service options

**Target queues:**  
- Arrival  registration  
- Checkout  visit completion

**Likely root cause addressed:**  
Administrative workload concentrated at the beginning and end of the visit, often amplified by insurance verification, forms, payment collection, scheduling, referrals, and discharge instructions.

**Data support:**  
If registration and checkout have high volume, peak clustering, and long waits despite relatively short service times, the issue may be burst demand rather than complex individual transactions. If service time is long for certain patient groups, targeted pre-registration may be especially valuable.

**Interventions:**
- Pre-visit digital registration, demographics verification, forms, consent, insurance verification, and co-pay collection.
- Kiosks or mobile check-in for suitable patients, with staff support for those who need assistance.
- Separate simple check-in/check-out from exception handling.
- Complete follow-up scheduling and referral processing electronically where appropriate.
- Use pre-visit outreach to resolve missing insurance authorization or documentation before the appointment day.
- Maintain an accessible non-digital process to avoid excluding patients with limited digital access.

**Expected impact:**  
Reducing service time and variability at registration can lower queue buildup during morning peaks. If a substantial share of patients completes paperwork before arrival, average front-desk time and queue length can decline without additional staffing.

**Validation approach:**  
Measure adoption rates, average registration service time, registration wait percentiles, patient satisfaction, error rates, and equity impacts across patient groups.

## 4. Trade-offs and Constraints

Reducing one queue can move congestion elsewhere. Every intervention should be evaluated at the end-to-end visit level, not only at the targeted activity.

### 4.1 Common trade-offs

#### Shifting rather than eliminating a bottleneck
Adding registration capacity may move more patients rapidly into the nurse queue. Shortening physician waits may create a larger diagnostic queue. Therefore, evaluate the full patient journey and resource network.

#### Cost and staffing implications
Additional staff, extended hours, float roles, software, kiosks, and training have costs. The clinic should compare:

- Cost per patient-minute of wait reduced
- Cost per additional completed visit
- Impact on overtime and turnover
- Avoided costs from missed appointments, complaints, rework, or lost revenue

Peak-only staffing and better schedule design may offer better value than permanent staffing increases.

#### Staff workload and burnout
Cross-training and flow coordination can improve flexibility, but they can also increase role ambiguity, interruptions, and workload. Staff should be involved in design and protected from unsustainable multitasking.

#### Care quality and clinical appropriateness
Parallel diagnostics and pre-ordering can reduce delay but must not lead to unnecessary testing or undermine clinical judgment. Any standing order or protocol must have clinical governance, eligibility criteria, and ongoing review.

#### Equity and access
Digital self-service can improve throughput for some patients while disadvantaging others. Alternatives must remain available for patients with language barriers, disabilities, low digital literacy, or limited access to technology.

#### Provider autonomy and scheduling preferences
More standardized appointment templates may conflict with provider preferences. A collaborative, data-based approach is needed, recognizing legitimate differences in case mix and clinical practice.

#### Privacy and governance
Real-time queue dashboards should display only the minimum necessary patient information and comply with healthcare privacy, security, and access-control requirements.

### 4.2 Balancing conflicting objectives

The clinic should use a multi-objective improvement framework rather than optimizing average waiting time alone. Relevant objectives include:

- Median and 90th-percentile waiting time
- Total visit duration
- Patient satisfaction and complaints
- On-time appointment starts
- Clinical quality and safety indicators
- Staff workload and overtime
- Resource utilization
- Cost per visit
- Access and equity
- Throughput and no-show/cancellation rates

A practical method is to define guardrail metrics for each pilot. For example:

- Reduce 90th-percentile physician wait by at least 20%.
- Do not increase diagnostic wait by more than 5%.
- Do not reduce completed visits per session.
- Do not increase staff overtime or quality incidents.
- Maintain patient satisfaction for vulnerable populations.

Simulation and limited pilots should be used before broad implementation, particularly when changing schedules or staffing.

## 5. Measuring Success and Sustained Monitoring

### 5.1 Core KPIs

#### Patient waiting and flow KPIs
- Average, median, 90th, and 95th-percentile wait for each key transition
- Total waiting time per visit
- Total visit duration
- Percentage of visit duration spent waiting
- Percentage of patients exceeding wait targets, such as >15, >30, or >60 minutes
- On-time start rate for appointments
- Percentage of visits completed within a target duration
- Number of patients leaving before completion, if captured

#### Queue and capacity KPIs
- Queue length by stage and time interval
- Arrival rate and completion rate by activity
- Resource utilization by staff, room, equipment, and specialty
- Room turnover time
- Diagnostic turnaround time
- Frequency and duration of resource idle time while patients are waiting
- Number of queue threshold breaches and time to resolve them

#### Operational and financial KPIs
- Visits completed per clinic session
- Overtime hours
- Temporary staffing or float staffing cost
- Cost per completed visit
- No-show, cancellation, and rescheduling rates
- Rework or repeat administrative tasks

#### Patient and quality KPIs
- Patient satisfaction scores, particularly questions related to waiting and communication
- Complaints mentioning delays
- Patient-reported understanding of delays and next steps
- Clinical safety events or missed care steps
- Unnecessary diagnostic testing rate, if pre-ordering is introduced
- Follow-up completion and care quality measures relevant to each specialty

#### Equity KPIs
Monitor all core waiting measures by:
- Urgency
- New versus follow-up status
- Specialty
- Age group, language need, disability support need, or other appropriate equity variables
- Site and payer category where legally and ethically appropriate

The goal should be to reduce inequitable waits, not simply improve the overall average while leaving high-risk groups behind.

### 5.2 Ongoing process monitoring

The clinic can use the same event-log structure to create a continuous queue-monitoring capability.

#### Daily or real-time operational monitoring
Use dashboards that display:

- Current patients waiting by stage
- Elapsed wait and urgency
- Queue length by service area
- Available rooms and resources
- Activity completion rates
- Patients at risk of exceeding a wait threshold

This supports operational intervention during the day.

#### Weekly and monthly management monitoring
Review:

- Trends in median and 90th-percentile waits
- Performance by specialty, provider group, clinic location, time of day, and patient segment
- Whether bottlenecks have moved
- Whether volume or case mix has changed
- Impact of schedule, staffing, or workflow changes
- Cost, throughput, satisfaction, and quality guardrails

#### Before-and-after evaluation
For each intervention, compare a baseline period with a post-implementation period while accounting for:

- Seasonal changes
- Day-of-week differences
- Staffing changes
- Patient volume
- Specialty mix
- Holiday effects
- Changes in urgency or complexity

Where feasible, use a pilot-versus-control design or phased rollout across clinics. Avoid claiming success based solely on a short-term average improvement.

### 5.3 Continuous improvement cycle

A sustainable model is:

1. **Measure:** Refresh event logs and calculate queue metrics.
2. **Diagnose:** Identify critical queues, segments, and root causes.
3. **Prioritize:** Select interventions based on patient impact, feasibility, cost, and quality risk.
4. **Test:** Pilot using simulation or controlled rollout.
5. **Evaluate:** Measure queue, throughput, cost, quality, and equity outcomes.
6. **Standardize or adjust:** Expand successful changes and revise unsuccessful ones.
7. **Monitor continuously:** Watch for bottleneck migration and process drift.

The central principle is to treat patient flow as an end-to-end system. The best interventions reduce total patient waiting and improve reliability without simply transferring delay from one department, staff group, or patient segment to another.
