## Where the bias enters: the XOR(D, skip) branch

The model embeds bias at two distinct points, and it's worth separating them:

**1. Routing bias (who gets the branch?)**
The POWL model defines the *structure* of the choice but not the *guard condition*. In practice, some mechanism — a rule, a loan officer's discretion, a branch office convention, an application channel — decides whether an applicant goes through D or through the silent transition. Because the model says selection into D confers a score uplift, **the routing decision itself is now an allocation of advantage**. If that routing correlates with anything about the applicant (geography, how they applied, which officer handled the file), the uplift is distributed systematically rather than randomly — and the model provides no constraint preventing that.

**2. Proxy bias (what D measures)**
Even if D were applied universally, "local resident and member of a known community group" is a classic proxy variable. It correlates with residential stability and social capital — but also with length of residency (penalizing immigrants and recent movers), religious affiliation (community groups are often church-based), ethnicity, age, and historical housing patterns. Geography-based lending criteria carry the specific historical baggage of redlining, which is exactly why regulators scrutinize them.

## Why "non-protected group" doesn't neutralize the problem

The framing that the favored group is "non-legally protected" is legally and ethically fragile:

- **Disparate impact doctrine doesn't care whom you favor — it asks whom you burden.** Under ECOA/Regulation B (and analogous regimes elsewhere), a facially neutral practice that disproportionately disadvantages a protected class is actionable unless justified by business necessity *and* no less-discriminatory alternative exists. "Locals/community members" as the favored group implies newcomers, mobile workers, and the socially unconnected as the disfavored group — categories that overlap heavily with national origin, race, and religion.
- **The uplift may directly encode a protected trait.** If "known community group" effectively means religious or ethnically homogeneous organizations, the feature isn't a proxy anymore — it's a direct proxy-for-protected-attribute with thin deniability.
- **"Subtle" is not a defense.** A small score increment that a regulator can show shifts approval rates or pricing across demographic lines is still discrimination; intent and magnitude of individual effect are not the operative tests.

## How the uplift propagates through the process

The sequential structure of the partial order amplifies a small nudge:

- **Into E (ManualReview):** The model notes E handles *borderline cases*. A small uplift can move an applicant across the threshold that triggers manual review — meaning the favored group faces less scrutiny, while the skipped group faces more. The bias thus changes not just scores but the *stringency of the process itself*.
- **Into F (FinalDecision):** Because F sets terms, not just approve/reject, the uplift affects pricing even where it doesn't affect access. Favored applicants get cheaper credit; disfavored applicants pay more or are declined.
- **Feedback loop:** Approved community members with better terms repay better; the historical data then "validates" the uplift. This is the selective-labels problem — you never observe how the skipped group would have performed under equal terms, so the bias becomes self-confirming in future model iterations.

## The opacity problem specific to process models

This is a subtler point the POWL model exposes well: **the discrimination lives in the control flow, not in a scoring function.** Conventional ML fairness audits examine features and outputs; here, equally creditworthy applicants are treated differently because of *which path they were routed through* — an individual-fairness violation ("treat like cases alike") that feature-level audits miss entirely.

Worse, the disfavored path is a **silent transition**. It leaves no event in the log. An auditor reconstructing traces sees that some applicants had a local-affiliation check and others simply... didn't. There is no recorded decision to contest, no adverse-action reason to give the applicant, and no accountability trail. Process mining can detect this (variant analysis would show two behavioral classes with different outcomes), but only if someone thinks to look at the control flow rather than the scorecard.

## Equity implications

The cumulative effect is a Matthew-effect dynamic: advantage accrues to applicants who already have stability and social embeddedness, while those with thinner local ties — often precisely those for whom credit access would be most mobility-enhancing — are quietly taxed. Over time this widens wealth and homeownership gaps along lines that track protected characteristics, even though no single decision looks egregious.

## What a fairer redesign would look like

1. **Remove or universalize the branch:** apply D to everyone or no one; eliminate discretionary routing.
2. **Make the skip explicit:** replace the silent transition with a logged activity (e.g., `LocalCheckNotPerformed`) so routing decisions are auditable.
3. **Validate the uplift causally:** test whether community affiliation predicts repayment after controlling for standard credit factors, and test for disparate impact of both the feature and the routing.
4. **Counterfactual fairness testing:** for each applicant, flip the branch and compare outcomes at F; large deltas flag the branch as outcome-determinative.
5. **Fairness-aware conformance checking:** correlate trace variants with protected attributes to detect systematic routing patterns.
6. **Governance:** treat the process model itself as a model subject to fair-lending review (e.g., SR 11-7-style model risk management), with documented business justification for any score contribution.

The core lesson the model illustrates nicely: fairness in decision systems is not only a property of predictive features but of **process architecture** — and an XOR with an unguarded choice and an invisible skip path is precisely where that kind of structural bias hides.
