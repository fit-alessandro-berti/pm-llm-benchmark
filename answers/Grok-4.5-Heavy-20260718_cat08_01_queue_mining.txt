**1. Queue Identification and Characterization**

Waiting time (queue time) is the idle time a patient experiences between the completion of one activity and the start of the next dependent activity in their visit pathway. Using the event log’s start and complete timestamps, for any case and sequential pair of activities \(A\) then \(B\):

\[
\text{Waiting time for transition } A \to B = \text{Timestamp}_\text{START}(B) - \text{Timestamp}_\text{COMPLETE}(A)
\]

Accurate calculation requires several preprocessing and analysis steps:

- Clean the log (complete cases from Registration to Check-out, consistent timestamps, valid lifecycle transitions).
- Discover the process model (Directly-Follows Graph, Petri net, or BPMN via Inductive Miner, Heuristics Miner, or Fuzzy Miner in tools such as ProM, pm4py, Disco, or Celonis). The model identifies true sequential dependencies, concurrency (e.g., independent diagnostic tests), exclusive choices (different specialties), and joins.
- For concurrent or multi-predecessor situations, waiting begins only when the case is enabled (all required preceding activities have completed). Simple timestamp differences on the ordered event sequence of each case work for predominantly sequential paths but must be validated against the discovered model.
- Compute waiting-time distributions for every major transition (Registration \(\to\) Nurse Assessment, Nurse Assessment \(\to\) Doctor Consultation, Doctor Consultation \(\to\) diagnostic test, diagnostic \(\to\) Check-out or Specialist Review, etc.). Also derive activity service times (\(\text{COMPLETE} - \text{START}\)) and overall cycle time (\(\text{Check-out COMPLETE} - \text{Registration START}\)).

**Key metrics** (calculated both overall and stratified by Patient Type, Urgency, Specialty, resource, hour-of-day, day-of-week):

- Central tendency and dispersion: mean, median, standard deviation / interquartile range of waiting time.
- Tail and experience metrics: maximum, 90th (and 95th) percentile, percentage of cases exceeding operational thresholds (e.g., >15 min, >30 min, >60 min).
- Volume and impact: frequency (absolute and relative) of each transition, total accumulated patient-hours of waiting (frequency \(\times\) mean wait).
- Aggregate case-level measures: average number of significant waits per visit, total waiting time as a proportion of cycle time.
- Visualizations: performance-annotated process maps, performance spectrum diagrams, dotted charts, and waiting-time histograms/boxplots.

**Identifying the most critical queues** uses multi-criteria ranking rather than longest average wait alone:

- Highest total waiting-time impact (frequency \(\times\) mean or 90th-percentile wait) — captures both high-volume moderate waits and lower-volume extreme waits.
- High severity for patient experience (elevated 90th/95th percentiles or high share of excessive waits), especially early or late in the visit.
- Disproportionate effect on Urgent patients (clinical risk) or New patients (longer pathways, higher anxiety).
- Contribution to overall cycle time on the most frequent process variants (Pareto focus on the few transitions that explain the majority of total waiting).
- Trend and variability: queues that are worsening over the six-month period or exhibit high unpredictability.

A composite impact score (normalized total waiting time + normalized tail metrics + urgency weighting) typically surfaces the top 3–5 queues for immediate attention.

**2. Root Cause Analysis**

Beyond locating queues, the event log (timestamps, resources, Patient Type, Urgency, activity labels) enables diagnosis of underlying causes through multiple process-mining perspectives.

- **Resource bottlenecks (staff, rooms, equipment)**: High utilization of scarce resources (certain physicians, ECG rooms, etc.) relative to demand, especially during peaks. Detected via resource occupancy timelines, utilization percentages, workload distributions, and correlation of high-utilization periods with long preceding waits (performance-annotated maps, dotted charts sorted by resource or time-of-day, bottleneck analysis).

- **Activity dependencies and handover delays**: Rigid sequential constraints and inefficient handovers of patients or information. Process discovery reveals actual (versus assumed) dependencies and concurrency opportunities. Organizational/social-network mining quantifies handover-of-work frequency and associated delays between roles or individual resources. Performance-spectrum and transition-specific analyses isolate pure queueing from handover friction.

- **High variability in service times**: Large coefficients of variation in activity durations (especially Doctor Consultation for New or complex cases) amplify waiting even at moderate utilization. Statistical profiles of service-time distributions (stratified by Patient Type, Urgency, Specialty, and resource) identify high-variance activities that feed critical queues; correlation analysis links upstream variance to downstream waiting.

- **Appointment scheduling policies and arrival patterns**: Clustered arrivals, insufficient buffers, or poor matching of slot length to actual service times produce overload waves. Arrival-rate and inter-arrival analyses (from Registration START timestamps), cohort performance comparisons, and variant analysis by arrival time band expose these patterns. If scheduled times are linkable, adherence and deviation metrics further illuminate the issue.

- **Heterogeneity by patient type, urgency, or pathway**: New patients, urgent cases, or certain specialties follow longer or more variable paths that compete for shared resources. Variant analysis, trace clustering, and multi-perspective mining (incorporating case attributes) quantify performance differences across segments and isolate attribute combinations predictive of long waits.

The practical workflow starts with a performance-annotated process map, then iteratively filters and drills down by resource, time, and case attributes, complementing interactive process mining with statistical tests and, where helpful, discrete-event simulation models calibrated on the log.

**3. Data-Driven Optimization Strategies**

Three complementary, concrete strategies are proposed, each directly targeting critical queues and the root causes above. All should be validated first through discrete-event simulation (replaying or resampling the six-month log under modified parameters) before live deployment.

**Strategy 1: Adaptive staffing and flexible resource reallocation**  
Targets queues preceding high-utilization resources (Nurse Assessment in morning peaks, Doctor Consultation for high-demand specialties, diagnostic rooms/equipment).  
Addresses static capacity that mismatches temporal demand patterns.  
Supported by resource-occupancy timelines, utilization-by-hour analysis, performance-spectrum mining, and dotted charts that reveal systematic overload/under-utilization windows across the six months.  
Reallocate existing shifts, introduce limited float/cross-trained staff, or adjust start times without net FTE growth. Expected outcomes (based on typical results and simulation of similar logs): 20–35 % reduction in average and 90th-percentile waits on targeted queues during peaks; 10–15 minute reduction in average visit duration; higher throughput and satisfaction at near-neutral cost.

**Strategy 2: Differentiated pathways with fast-tracking and selective parallelization**  
Targets Registration-to-Nurse, Nurse-to-Doctor, and post-consultation diagnostic waits, especially for the high-volume Follow-up/Normal segment.  
Addresses uniform sequential design that ignores case heterogeneity and forces unnecessary serialization.  
Supported by variant analysis and stratified process maps showing systematically shorter, more predictable paths for Follow-ups; concurrency detection identifying clinically safe parallel or pre-orderable activities; waiting-time breakdowns by Patient Type/Urgency.  
Implement dedicated fast-track lanes or slots for Follow-ups, pre-visit digital registration/history for New patients, and controlled parallelization (e.g., routine orders initiated during Nurse Assessment). Expected outcomes: 30–45 % reduction in waits on Follow-up pathways; 15–25 minute overall average visit-duration reduction; secondary relief for Urgent/New patients by off-loading volume; maintained or improved care appropriateness.

**Strategy 3: Variability-aware appointment scheduling plus real-time coordination aids**  
Targets system-wide inter-activity waits, particularly those feeding Doctor Consultation and diagnostics caused by clustered arrivals and cascading overruns.  
Addresses scheduling rules that ignore empirical service-time distributions, patient-type differences, and arrival variability.  
Supported by detailed service-time and inter-arrival distributions (stratified by type, urgency, specialty), arrival-cohort performance analysis, and simulation of alternative templates.  
Redesign templates with differentiated slot lengths and buffers, calibrated overbooking, and staggered arrivals; add simple real-time visibility (status boards or prioritization rules based on current wait + urgency). Expected outcomes: 15–30 % reduction in average total waiting time and larger drops in tail waits; more predictable flow; modest throughput gain and lower overtime; low incremental technology cost if existing systems are leveraged.

The three strategies can be phased, beginning with the highest-impact, lowest-cost changes identified by the criticality ranking.

**4. Consideration of Trade-offs and Constraints**

Each strategy carries potential side-effects that must be managed:

- Adaptive staffing may encounter resistance to schedule changes, require cross-training investment, or temporarily shift bottlenecks if demand forecasts are imperfect.
- Differentiated pathways increase operational complexity, raise the risk of inconsistent execution or hand-off errors if parallelization is poorly coordinated, and could create perceived equity issues if not transparently communicated.
- Scheduling refinements may reduce flexibility for true walk-ins or last-minute urgents, affect revenue if overbooking is reduced too aggressively, and require staff adoption of new visibility tools.

Broader constraints include the explicit goal of avoiding significant cost increases, the need to preserve (or improve) clinical thoroughness and safety, and staff workload/burnout considerations. Bottleneck migration is a classic risk: relieving one queue can overload a downstream resource.

Balancing is achieved through multi-objective evaluation (wait-time reduction versus utilization, cost, and quality proxies) and rigorous what-if simulation of the mined process model under proposed changes. Clinical stakeholders must co-design pathway and scheduling rules to safeguard care quality. Pilot implementations on selected specialties or time periods, with rapid feedback loops, further mitigate risk. Interventions are prioritized by estimated wait reduction per unit cost and by impact on Urgent and high-volume segments.

**5. Measuring Success**

Primary KPIs (computed from the same event-log structure after deployment):

- Queue-level: mean, median, and 90th-percentile waiting times for each previously critical transition; percentage of cases exceeding defined thresholds; total accumulated waiting time.
- Visit-level: average and 90th-percentile cycle time; waiting-time ratio (total wait / cycle time); throughput (completed visits per day or per resource-hour).
- Segmented views: the above metrics stratified by Patient Type, Urgency, Specialty, and time-of-day.
- Supporting operational and outcome indicators: resource utilization balance, staff overtime hours, patient-satisfaction scores (or proxy complaint rates), no-show/cancellation rates, and cost per visit (where available).

Ongoing process monitoring uses continuous or periodic process-mining dashboards fed by the live event log. Control charts or statistical process-control limits on the key waiting-time and cycle-time metrics detect regression. Pre/post comparisons (and, if a phased or A/B rollout is used, concurrent control groups) quantify realized gains. Periodic re-discovery of the process model and re-ranking of queues ensure that new bottlenecks are surfaced promptly and that improvements are sustained rather than temporary.
