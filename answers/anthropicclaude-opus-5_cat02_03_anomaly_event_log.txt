## Baseline: What "Normal" Looks Like

Case 1001 provides the reference (happy-path) flow:

1. Create Purchase Requisition
2. Approve Purchase Requisition
3. Issue Purchase Order
4. Receive Goods
5. Vendor Invoice Received
6. Match Invoice to PO (3-way match: PO  goods receipt  invoice)
7. Release Payment
8. Close Case

The key control principles embedded here are: **approve before you commit money (PO)**, **receive and invoice before you match**, and **match before you pay**. Every anomaly below is a violation of one of these three controls.

---

## Case 1002 — Approval after the fact + payment before matching

| Observed order | Problem |
|---|---|
| Create PR (08:30)  **Issue PO (08:40)**  Vendor Invoice (Feb 3 07:00)  **Release Payment (07:15)**  **Approve PR (08:00)**  Receive Goods (Feb 4)  Match Invoice to PO (Feb 5)  Close |

**Anomalies:**

1. **PO issued before requisition approval** (and only 10 minutes after creation). The organization legally committed to the vendor before anyone with budget authority signed off. The 10-minute gap also suggests no meaningful review occurred at all.
2. **Payment released before goods receipt and before invoice matching.** This is the most serious control failure in the log: money left the organization with no evidence that anything was ever delivered and no verification that the invoiced amount, quantity, and price matched the PO.
3. **Approval performed *after* payment.** This is retro-active or "rubber-stamp" approval — the approver had no real choice, since the funds were already gone. It creates the *appearance* of a control in the audit trail without the substance of one.
4. **Matching performed after payment** (Feb 5 vs. payment Feb 3) — the 3-way match becomes a purely cosmetic, after-the-fact reconciliation. If a discrepancy were found, recovery now requires clawing money back from the vendor rather than simply withholding it.

**Why problematic:** classic duplicate/overpayment and fraud exposure (maverick buying, fictitious vendor, unauthorized spend), budget overruns, and an audit finding on segregation of duties / approval hierarchy (e.g., SOX-style control deficiency).

## Case 1003 — Blind payment (paid before invoice and before delivery)

| Observed order | Problem |
|---|---|
| Create PR  Approve PR  Issue PO  **Release Payment (Feb 8)**  **Vendor Invoice Received (Feb 9)**  **Receive Goods (Feb 10)**  Match Invoice to PO (Feb 11)  Close |

**Anomalies:**

1. **Payment released before any invoice existed.** There was no payable document, no amount validated by the vendor, and nothing to match against. Payment was effectively made off the PO alone.
2. **Payment released before goods were received** — a full prepayment with no delivery assurance. If the vendor had failed to deliver, the organization would have an unsecured claim.
3. **Match Invoice to PO occurs three days after payment**, again reducing the 3-way match to a post-mortem rather than a preventive control.

**Why problematic:** highest risk of paying the wrong amount, paying twice (once now, once when the invoice is later processed normally), or paying for goods never received. Unless there is a documented, approved prepayment/advance-payment agreement (which the log does not show), this is an unauthorized disbursement.

## Case 1004 — Goods received before the PO existed

| Observed order | Problem |
|---|---|
| Create PR  Approve PR  **Receive Goods (Feb 6 09:00)**  **Issue PO (Feb 6 09:15)**  Vendor Invoice  Match  Release Payment  Close |

**Anomalies:**

1. **Goods receipt recorded before the purchase order was issued.** Either the vendor shipped without a formal order (unauthorized/verbal ordering) or, more likely, the PO was created retroactively to cover a delivery that had already happened.
2. **The 15-minute gap between receipt and PO issuance is a tell-tale "retro-PO" pattern** — the PO was raised purely to make the invoice payable, not to control the purchase.

**Why problematic:** the PO loses its function as a pre-commitment control. Prices, terms, and quantities can be back-fitted to whatever the vendor delivered and invoiced, so the subsequent "Match Invoice to PO" will always succeed — the match is guaranteed to pass and therefore detects nothing. This is a common route for over-billing and for circumventing procurement/sourcing policy. Note that everything downstream (invoice  match  payment) is in correct order, so this case is *less* severe than 1002 and 1003, but it still defeats a preventive control.

## Cross-Cutting Observations

- **Two of four cases (50%) release payment before the 3-way match.** This is not an isolated slip; it suggests the payment step is not systemically blocked by the match in the ERP configuration.
- **Suspiciously short intervals** (PRPO in 10 min in 1002; invoicepayment in 15 min in 1002; receiptPO in 15 min in 1004) indicate automated or bypassed steps rather than genuine human review — a useful red flag for process mining throughput-time analysis.
- **Every case reaches "Close Case,"** meaning the process completes "successfully" despite the violations. The log therefore looks healthy on outcome-based KPIs; only conformance checking on ordering reveals the problems.

## Recommended Remediations

1. Hard system block: **no PO release without an approved PR** (no retroactive approvals accepted).
2. Hard system block: **no payment run without a completed 3-way match**; prepayments only via a separate, explicitly approved advance-payment path.
3. Prevent **goods receipt postings against non-existent POs**; route unexpected deliveries to an exception queue.
4. Add automated conformance monitoring/alerting for the specific patterns above, plus a minimum-dwell-time check on approval steps to detect rubber-stamping.
5. Retrospectively audit cases 1002 and 1003 for duplicate or unrecovered payments.
