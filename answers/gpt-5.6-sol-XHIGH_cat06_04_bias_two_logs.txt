### Finding

The **Group B log exhibits preferential treatment**, and the combined process is therefore potentially biased **against Group A**.

### How the bias manifests

| Comparison | Group A | Group B |
|---|---|---|
| Residency | All `LocalResident = FALSE` | All `LocalResident = TRUE` |
| Community membership | None | U001 and U003 belong to the Highland Civic Darts Club |
| Score adjustment | No positive adjustments | Club members receive `+10 (Community Boost)` |
| Effect | Final score remains unchanged | Adjusted score is carried into review and final decision |

Examples:

- **P001 and U001 both begin with 720.**
  - P001 receives no adjustment and remains at 720.
  - U001 receives a club-based boost and becomes 730.
  - Both are approved, but U001 receives a larger decision margin despite the same preliminary score.

- **U003 begins at 695**, receives a +10 club boost, and is approved at an adjusted score of 705.
  - By comparison, P002 is rejected with an unadjusted score of 710.
  - This is inconsistent with a purely score-based, monotonic decision rule and suggests that membership or another correlated factor may influence the outcome. Other unrecorded factors could also explain it, so causation is not certain.

- **U002 is a local resident but not a club member** and receives no boost. Thus, the data directly show that **community membership**, rather than residency alone, triggers the adjustment.

### Role of `LocalResident` and `CommunityGroup`

`LocalResident` is perfectly aligned with group status in this sample: all protected applicants are nonlocal, while all unprotected applicants are local. Community-club membership also appears only in Group B. Consequently, these attributes can act as **proxies for protected-group membership**, even if the policy does not explicitly reference the protected status.

If club membership is unrelated to legitimate decision criteria, the +10 adjustment constitutes preferential treatment. Applicants in Group B can receive a scoring benefit that no Group A applicant appears able to access, allowing otherwise borderline Group B cases to cross a decision boundary.

### Important limitation

Both groups have the same observed approval rate—2 of 3—so this small sample does **not** demonstrate an aggregate approval-rate disparity. It does, however, reveal a **procedural or treatment disparity**: similarly scored applicants are not evaluated under the same scoring rules. Over a larger population, this community-based boost could systematically increase approvals for Group B and disadvantage Group A.
