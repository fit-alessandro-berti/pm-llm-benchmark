## Temporal Profile Anomalies

1. **Receive  Approve (`R`  `P`)**
   - Average duration is about 25 hours (`90,000` seconds).
   - Standard deviation is only 1 hour, which is unusually small for a process likely affected by claim complexity, staffing, weekends, and claim type.
   - This may indicate an automated or scheduled approval pattern, timestamp rounding, batch processing, or a data-quality issue.

2. **Approve  Notify (`P`  `N`)**
   - Average delay is 7 days, with a 2-day standard deviation.
   - Notification should ordinarily occur shortly after approval, so a week-long delay is a likely bottleneck.
   - The large variability also suggests inconsistent handling, such as manual queues, regional differences, or delayed customer communications.

3. **Assign  Close (`A`  `C`)**
   - Average duration is only 2 hours.
   - A claim being closed soon after assignment can be valid for withdrawals, duplicates, or obvious invalid claims, but it is suspicious if evaluation, approval, or notification events are absent.
   - It may indicate premature closure, missing event records, or claims being closed through an alternate workflow.

4. **Evaluate  Notify (`E`  `N`)**
   - The average is only 5 minutes, with a standard deviation of 1 minute.
   - This could be valid if notification is automated, but it is unusual if approval is required before notification.
   - It may indicate that evaluation is automatically generating customer communications, approval is skipped, or event timestamps are recorded in the wrong order.

5. **Evaluate  Close (`E`  `C`)**
   - Average duration is 1 hour, but the standard deviation is 50 minutes.
   - The relative variability is high: some cases may be closed almost immediately while others remain open for multiple hours.
   - This could indicate a mix of automated closures, manual review, rejected claims, or incomplete event logging.

6. **Notify  Close (`N`  `C`)**
   - Average duration is 30 minutes with only 5 minutes of variation.
   - This highly regular timing may indicate an automated closure job that runs on a schedule rather than genuine completion after customer notification.
   - It may also result from timestamps being generated by a workflow engine rather than actual user actions.

## Possible Hypotheses

### `R`  `P`: Highly consistent approval timing

- Claims may be approved by a nightly or hourly batch job.
- Approval timestamps may be defaulted to a standard service-level target rather than recording the actual approval time.
- Only a narrow subset of low-complexity claims may be represented in the temporal profile.
- Claims may be automatically approved after a fixed waiting period.
- Timestamp precision may be low, for example rounded to the nearest hour or day.
- Claims submitted outside working hours may be processed at the next standard processing window.

### `P`  `N`: Long notification delay

- Approved claims may enter a manual customer-communication queue.
- Notification generation may depend on another system, such as document creation, payment processing, or compliance validation.
- Notifications may be sent in periodic batches, such as weekly mail or scheduled email runs.
- There may be insufficient staff responsible for customer communication.
- Certain claim types, regions, or high-value claims may require extra review before notification.
- Failed notification attempts may delay the final recorded notification event.

### `A`  `C`: Fast closure after assignment

- Claims may be closed because they are duplicates, withdrawals, incomplete submissions, or outside policy coverage.
- The process may support a fast-track rejection or cancellation path that does not require evaluation and approval.
- Evaluation, approval, or notification events may be missing from the event log.
- Event ordering may be incorrect due to delayed data entry or system synchronization.
- An adjuster may be closing claims without following the intended workflow.
- Some closure events may represent administrative closure rather than a finalized claim decision.

### `E`  `N`: Very short evaluation-to-notification interval

- Notification may be automatically triggered once an evaluation event is entered.
- Evaluation could actually represent an automated scoring step rather than a complete human assessment.
- Approval may be performed implicitly or may not be logged.
- The `E` and `N` events may be generated by the same system transaction.
- The events could be timestamped when recorded in the system rather than when the actual business action occurred.
- A subset of claims may follow an expedited workflow.

### `N`  `C`: Highly regular closure timing

- Claims may be automatically closed after a fixed post-notification interval.
- A scheduled job may close notified claims at regular intervals.
- Closure timestamps may be system-generated rather than manually entered.
- The process may not allow customers sufficient time to respond before closure.
- The event data may contain synthetic, default, or copied timestamps.

## Verification Queries

The following queries use PostgreSQL syntax and assume that, for a given claim, the relevant event is the first occurrence of each activity unless otherwise noted.

### 1. Find `R`  `P` durations outside the expected range

Using a ZETA threshold of 3 standard deviations:

- Expected mean: 90,000 seconds
- Standard deviation: 3,600 seconds
- Expected range: 79,200 to 100,800 seconds

```sql
WITH receive_events AS (
    SELECT claim_id, MIN(timestamp) AS receive_time
    FROM claim_events
    WHERE activity = 'R'
    GROUP BY claim_id
),
approve_events AS (
    SELECT claim_id, MIN(timestamp) AS approve_time
    FROM claim_events
    WHERE activity = 'P'
    GROUP BY claim_id
)
SELECT
    c.claim_id,
    c.claim_type,
    c.claim_amount,
    r.receive_time,
    p.approve_time,
    EXTRACT(EPOCH FROM (p.approve_time - r.receive_time)) AS duration_seconds,
    EXTRACT(EPOCH FROM (p.approve_time - r.receive_time)) / 3600.0 AS duration_hours
FROM claims c
JOIN receive_events r ON r.claim_id = c.claim_id
JOIN approve_events p ON p.claim_id = c.claim_id
WHERE EXTRACT(EPOCH FROM (p.approve_time - r.receive_time)) NOT BETWEEN 79200 AND 100800
ORDER BY duration_seconds;
```

To inspect whether durations cluster around exact hourly or daily values:

```sql
WITH receive_events AS (
    SELECT claim_id, MIN(timestamp) AS receive_time
    FROM claim_events
    WHERE activity = 'R'
    GROUP BY claim_id
),
approve_events AS (
    SELECT claim_id, MIN(timestamp) AS approve_time
    FROM claim_events
    WHERE activity = 'P'
    GROUP BY claim_id
)
SELECT
    ROUND(
        EXTRACT(EPOCH FROM (p.approve_time - r.receive_time)) / 3600.0,
        2
    ) AS duration_hours,
    COUNT(*) AS claim_count
FROM receive_events r
JOIN approve_events p ON p.claim_id = r.claim_id
GROUP BY 1
ORDER BY claim_count DESC, duration_hours;
```

A strong concentration around a fixed duration, such as exactly 24, 25, or 26 hours, would support a batch-processing or timestamp-default hypothesis.

### 3. Compare `P`  `N` delay by claim type

```sql
WITH approve_events AS (
    SELECT claim_id, MIN(timestamp) AS approve_time
    FROM claim_events
    WHERE activity = 'P'
    GROUP BY claim_id
),
notify_events AS (
    SELECT claim_id, MIN(timestamp) AS notify_time
    FROM claim_events
    WHERE activity = 'N'
    GROUP BY claim_id
)
SELECT
    c.claim_type,
    COUNT(*) AS claims_with_approval_and_notification,
    ROUND(
        AVG(EXTRACT(EPOCH FROM (n.notify_time - p.approve_time)) / 86400.0),
        2
    ) AS avg_delay_days,
    ROUND(
        STDDEV_SAMP(EXTRACT(EPOCH FROM (n.notify_time - p.approve_time)) / 86400.0),
        2
    ) AS stdev_delay_days,
    ROUND(
        MAX(EXTRACT(EPOCH FROM (n.notify_time - p.approve_time)) / 86400.0),
        2
    ) AS max_delay_days
FROM claims c
JOIN approve_events p ON p.claim_id = c.claim_id
JOIN notify_events n ON n.claim_id = c.claim_id
GROUP BY c.claim_type
ORDER BY avg_delay_days DESC;
```

This can reveal whether a particular claim type, such as home insurance claims, is responsible for much of the notification delay.

### 4. Identify claims closed soon after assignment

The expected `A`  `C` mean is 2 hours, with a standard deviation of 1 hour. The query below finds claims closed within one hour of assignment, which may be candidates for premature closure.

```sql
WITH assign_events AS (
    SELECT claim_id, MIN(timestamp) AS assign_time
    FROM claim_events
    WHERE activity = 'A'
    GROUP BY claim_id
),
close_events AS (
    SELECT claim_id, MIN(timestamp) AS close_time
    FROM claim_events
    WHERE activity = 'C'
    GROUP BY claim_id
)
SELECT
    c.claim_id,
    c.customer_id,
    c.claim_type,
    c.claim_amount,
    a.assign_time,
    cl.close_time,
    EXTRACT(EPOCH FROM (cl.close_time - a.assign_time)) / 60.0 AS minutes_to_close
FROM claims c
JOIN assign_events a ON a.claim_id = c.claim_id
JOIN close_events cl ON cl.claim_id = c.claim_id
WHERE cl.close_time >= a.assign_time
  AND EXTRACT(EPOCH FROM (cl.close_time - a.assign_time)) < 3600
ORDER BY minutes_to_close;
```

### 5. Find quickly closed claims that skipped evaluation, approval, or notification

```sql
WITH assign_events AS (
    SELECT claim_id, MIN(timestamp) AS assign_time
    FROM claim_events
    WHERE activity = 'A'
    GROUP BY claim_id
),
close_events AS (
    SELECT claim_id, MIN(timestamp) AS close_time
    FROM claim_events
    WHERE activity = 'C'
    GROUP BY claim_id
)
SELECT
    c.claim_id,
    c.claim_type,
    c.claim_amount,
    a.assign_time,
    cl.close_time,
    EXTRACT(EPOCH FROM (cl.close_time - a.assign_time)) / 60.0 AS minutes_to_close,
    EXISTS (
        SELECT 1
        FROM claim_events e
        WHERE e.claim_id = c.claim_id
          AND e.activity = 'E'
          AND e.timestamp BETWEEN a.assign_time AND cl.close_time
    ) AS has_evaluation,
    EXISTS (
        SELECT 1
        FROM claim_events e
        WHERE e.claim_id = c.claim_id
          AND e.activity = 'P'
          AND e.timestamp BETWEEN a.assign_time AND cl.close_time
    ) AS has_approval,
    EXISTS (
        SELECT 1
        FROM claim_events e
        WHERE e.claim_id = c.claim_id
          AND e.activity = 'N'
          AND e.timestamp BETWEEN a.assign_time AND cl.close_time
    ) AS has_notification
FROM claims c
JOIN assign_events a ON a.claim_id = c.claim_id
JOIN close_events cl ON cl.claim_id = c.claim_id
WHERE cl.close_time >= a.assign_time
  AND EXTRACT(EPOCH FROM (cl.close_time - a.assign_time)) <= 7200
  AND (
      NOT EXISTS (
          SELECT 1
          FROM claim_events e
          WHERE e.claim_id = c.claim_id
            AND e.activity = 'E'
            AND e.timestamp BETWEEN a.assign_time AND cl.close_time
      )
      OR NOT EXISTS (
          SELECT 1
          FROM claim_events e
          WHERE e.claim_id = c.claim_id
            AND e.activity = 'P'
            AND e.timestamp BETWEEN a.assign_time AND cl.close_time
      )
      OR NOT EXISTS (
          SELECT 1
          FROM claim_events e
          WHERE e.claim_id = c.claim_id
            AND e.activity = 'N'
            AND e.timestamp BETWEEN a.assign_time AND cl.close_time
      )
  )
ORDER BY minutes_to_close;
```

This distinguishes valid fast-track closure cases from cases where mandatory process events may be missing.

### 7. Correlate timing anomalies with the assigned resource

If the `A` event resource represents the assigned adjuster or assignment owner:

```sql
WITH assign_events AS (
    SELECT DISTINCT ON (claim_id)
        claim_id,
        timestamp AS assign_time,
        resource AS assigned_resource
    FROM claim_events
    WHERE activity = 'A'
    ORDER BY claim_id, timestamp
),
approve_events AS (
    SELECT claim_id, MIN(timestamp) AS approve_time
    FROM claim_events
    WHERE activity = 'P'
    GROUP BY claim_id
),
notify_events AS (
    SELECT claim_id, MIN(timestamp) AS notify_time
    FROM claim_events
    WHERE activity = 'N'
    GROUP BY claim_id
)
SELECT
    a.assigned_resource,
    COUNT(*) AS claim_count,
    ROUND(
        AVG(EXTRACT(EPOCH FROM (n.notify_time - p.approve_time)) / 86400.0),
        2
    ) AS avg_approval_to_notification_days,
    ROUND(
        STDDEV_SAMP(EXTRACT(EPOCH FROM (n.notify_time - p.approve_time)) / 86400.0),
        2
    ) AS stdev_approval_to_notification_days,
    COUNT(*) FILTER (
        WHERE EXTRACT(EPOCH FROM (n.notify_time - p.approve_time)) > 1123200
    ) AS delayed_notification_count
FROM assign_events a
JOIN approve_events p ON p.claim_id = a.claim_id
JOIN notify_events n ON n.claim_id = a.claim_id
WHERE n.notify_time >= p.approve_time
GROUP BY a.assigned_resource
HAVING COUNT(*) >= 5
ORDER BY avg_approval_to_notification_days DESC;
```

If `resource` contains an adjuster identifier compatible with `adjusters.adjuster_id`, it can be joined to the adjusters table:

```sql
WITH assign_events AS (
    SELECT DISTINCT ON (claim_id)
        claim_id,
        resource AS assigned_resource
    FROM claim_events
    WHERE activity = 'A'
    ORDER BY claim_id, timestamp
),
approve_events AS (
    SELECT claim_id, MIN(timestamp) AS approve_time
    FROM claim_events
    WHERE activity = 'P'
    GROUP BY claim_id
),
notify_events AS (
    SELECT claim_id, MIN(timestamp) AS notify_time
    FROM claim_events
    WHERE activity = 'N'
    GROUP BY claim_id
)
SELECT
    ad.name,
    ad.region,
    ad.specialization,
    COUNT(*) AS claim_count,
    ROUND(
        AVG(EXTRACT(EPOCH FROM (n.notify_time - p.approve_time)) / 86400.0),
        2
    ) AS avg_notification_delay_days,
    COUNT(*) FILTER (
        WHERE EXTRACT(EPOCH FROM (n.notify_time - p.approve_time)) > 1123200
    ) AS extreme_delay_count
FROM assign_events a
JOIN adjusters ad
    ON ad.adjuster_id::text = a.assigned_resource
JOIN approve_events p
    ON p.claim_id = a.claim_id
JOIN notify_events n
    ON n.claim_id = a.claim_id
WHERE n.notify_time >= p.approve_time
GROUP BY ad.name, ad.region, ad.specialization
ORDER BY avg_notification_delay_days DESC;
```

### 8. Detect out-of-order events and negative durations

Negative durations can indicate synchronization issues, late data entry, incorrect timestamps, or incorrect event sequencing.

```sql
WITH activity_times AS (
    SELECT
        claim_id,
        MIN(timestamp) FILTER (WHERE activity = 'R') AS receive_time,
        MIN(timestamp) FILTER (WHERE activity = 'A') AS assign_time,
        MIN(timestamp) FILTER (WHERE activity = 'E') AS evaluate_time,
        MIN(timestamp) FILTER (WHERE activity = 'P') AS approve_time,
        MIN(timestamp) FILTER (WHERE activity = 'N') AS notify_time,
        MIN(timestamp) FILTER (WHERE activity = 'C') AS close_time
    FROM claim_events
    GROUP BY claim_id
)
SELECT
    claim_id,
    receive_time,
    assign_time,
    evaluate_time,
    approve_time,
    notify_time,
    close_time
FROM activity_times
WHERE (assign_time < receive_time)
   OR (evaluate_time < assign_time)
   OR (approve_time < evaluate_time)
   OR (notify_time < approve_time)
   OR (close_time < notify_time)
ORDER BY claim_id;
```

### 9. Inspect all event sequences for anomalous claims

Once suspicious claim IDs are identified, inspect their complete history:

```sql
SELECT
    ce.claim_id,
    ce.event_id,
    ce.activity,
    ce.timestamp,
    ce.resource,
    ce.additional_info
FROM claim_events ce
WHERE ce.claim_id IN (
    SELECT ce2.claim_id
    FROM claim_events ce2
    WHERE ce2.activity = 'C'
    GROUP BY ce2.claim_id
    HAVING COUNT(*) > 0
)
ORDER BY ce.claim_id, ce.timestamp, ce.event_id;
```

For a specific claim:

```sql
SELECT
    event_id,
    activity,
    timestamp,
    resource,
    additional_info
FROM claim_events
WHERE claim_id = :claim_id
ORDER BY timestamp, event_id;
```

This final inspection is important because aggregate temporal-profile anomalies may arise from valid alternative paths, duplicate events, missing activities, backdated timestamps, or genuine operational delays.
