## 1. Identifying Instance-Spanning Constraints and Their Impact

### 1.1 Prepare the event log for cross-instance analysis

Traditional process mining often treats each order as an independent case. Here, that is insufficient because resources, batches, priorities, and compliance limits connect multiple orders. The analysis should therefore combine:

- **Case-level event data:** order sequence, activity start/complete timestamps, end-to-end lead time.
- **Resource-level data:** station/resource ID, station type, shift/calendar, capacity, utilization, concurrent assignments.
- **Order attributes:** express/standard, cold-packing requirement, destination region, hazardous-material flag.
- **Batch-level data:** batch ID, batch creation time, batch release/label-generation time, batch size and region.
- **Facility-state data reconstructed from the log:** number of active hazardous orders in Packing or Quality Check at every timestamp; number of occupied cold stations; queues by order class.

Useful views include:

1. **Control-flow process map:** order paths and rework/variation.
2. **Performance map:** median and percentile waiting/service times at each activity.
3. **Resource-centric timeline:** station occupancy and queues.
4. **Object-centric or multi-entity process model:** links among orders, stations, batches, regions, and hazardous-capacity state.
5. **Time-series state reconstruction:** event replay to calculate the facility state at each event time.

A key methodological step is to distinguish:

\[
\text{Activity elapsed time} = \text{queue/waiting time} + \text{service time} + \text{interruption time}
\]

The start/complete events provide service duration directly. The interval between completion of the predecessor activity and start of the next activity is the initial measure of waiting. That waiting can then be attributed using resource, batch, priority, and regulatory-state evidence.

---

### 1.2 Shared Cold-Packing stations

#### Formal identification

For every order requiring cold packing:

1. Determine when it became **ready for Packing**:
   - typically Item Picking completion, subject to any additional business rules.
2. Determine the actual **Packing start time**.
3. Reconstruct occupancy of cold stations from Packing start/complete intervals for orders requiring cold packing.
4. Identify whether all five cold-packing stations were occupied during the order’s waiting interval.
5. Identify the queue composition: number of waiting cold orders, express vs. standard, hazardous vs. non-hazardous.

This creates a direct cross-case relationship: one order waits because other orders occupy the shared finite-capacity station pool.

#### Primary metrics

- **Cold-packing queue time**
  \[
  T_{cold\_queue} = t_{\text{Packing Start}} - t_{\text{Ready for Packing}}
  \]

- **Cold-station utilization**
  \[
  U_{cold} = \frac{\text{occupied cold-station time}}{5 \times \text{available station time}}
  \]

- **Probability that all cold stations are occupied** at a moment a cold order becomes ready.

- **Average and percentile queue length** for cold-packing orders:
  - mean queue length;
  - P50, P90, P95 queue length;
  - peak queue length by shift/day/season.

- **Cold-packing service time** versus waiting time:
  - separates inefficient packing execution from capacity contention.

- **Delay attribution rate:** percentage of cold-order waiting time during which all cold stations were occupied.

- **Priority displacement effects:** standard cold orders delayed by express cold orders.

#### Impact interpretation

If cold stations have sustained utilization above roughly 85–90%, rising P90 waiting time, and frequent all-stations-busy periods, the problem is likely structural capacity/scheduling contention rather than isolated staff-performance issues. If utilization is modest but queues remain high, poor dispatching, shift imbalances, or mismatches between station availability and labor availability may be the cause.

---

### 1.3 Shipping batching by destination region

#### Formal identification

For each order, determine:

- when it became **ready for Shipping Label Generation**—usually after Quality Check completion;
- its destination region;
- its shipping batch identifier, if logged;
- the batch’s label-generation/release timestamp;
- other orders included in the same batch and their ready times.

If batch IDs are incomplete, infer batches using region, label-generation timestamps, shipment windows, and shared shipping resource/batch indicator.

#### Primary metrics

- **Batch waiting time**
  \[
  T_{batch\_wait} = t_{\text{Batch Release/Label Generation}} - t_{\text{Ready for Label}}
  \]

- **Batch formation time**
  \[
  T_{formation} = t_{\text{Batch Release}} - t_{\text{First Eligible Order in Batch}}
  \]

- **Late-arrival dependence:** amount of time a completed order waits because a batch is incomplete.

- **Batch size distribution:** mean, median, variance, minimum and maximum by region.

- **Batch fill rate:** actual batch size compared with target or route capacity.

- **Batch release frequency:** batches per hour/day by region.

- **Percentage of orders held beyond a target threshold** after Quality Check solely for batch completion.

- **Trade-off metrics:** transport efficiency versus fulfillment delay:
  - order lead time;
  - route utilization;
  - cost per shipment;
  - missed dispatch cutoffs.

#### Impact interpretation

Long wait after Quality Check combined with short label-generation service time indicates that batching, not label generation itself, is driving delay. Region-specific analysis is important: low-volume regions may experience long batch waits, while high-volume regions may form frequent batches but still experience congestion later in the process.

---

### 1.4 Priority handling for express orders

#### Formal identification

Priority handling must be analyzed as a scheduling policy that changes the experience of other orders. Reconstruct station-level sequences and determine when an express order:

- entered a queue ahead of standard orders;
- began processing before standard orders that were already waiting;
- preempted an in-progress standard order, if preemption is truly recorded;
- consumed a scarce resource during high-demand periods.

A practical analysis compares standard-order waiting times in periods with and without express arrivals at the relevant resource.

#### Primary metrics

For express orders:

- **Express lead time and SLA attainment rate**
- **Express queue time at Picking, Packing, and QC**
- **Share of express orders meeting promised dispatch deadline**
- **Time-to-start after becoming ready at each activity**

For standard orders:

- **Priority-induced delay**
  \[
  T_{\text{priority induced}} =
  T_{\text{observed standard wait}} -
  T_{\text{expected wait without express interference}}
  \]

The expected baseline can be estimated using comparable periods, regression models, queueing models, or simulation.

- **Overtake count:** number of express orders started before a standard order that was ready earlier.
- **Displacement count:** number of standard orders whose start was deferred because an express order was selected.
- **Interrupted work time:** if standard work can be paused, the duration and frequency of interruptions.
- **Fairness/service degradation:** P90/P95 standard order lead time, especially for cold-packing standard orders.

#### Impact interpretation

Priority may improve express SLA performance while creating unacceptable tail delays for standard orders. The goal is not merely to confirm that express is faster; it is to quantify whether express acceleration is proportionate to the delay imposed on standard orders and whether the current policy causes avoidable preemption.

---

### 1.5 Hazardous-material concurrency limit

#### Formal identification

For every hazardous order, identify the intervals during which it is actively in:

- Packing;
- Quality Check.

At each timestamp, calculate:

\[
N_{haz}(t) =
N_{haz,\ Packing}(t) + N_{haz,\ QC}(t)
\]

The regulatory constraint requires:

\[
N_{haz}(t) \leq 10
\]

Use event replay to produce a continuous time series of hazardous concurrency. Then determine whether hazardous orders wait because the facility is at the cap even when the relevant local resource is otherwise available.

#### Primary metrics

- **Hazardous concurrency level over time**
- **Percentage of operating time at the cap of 10**
- **Number and duration of cap-binding periods**
- **Hazardous-material compliance violations:** any interval where \(N_{haz}(t)>10\)
- **Hazard-related waiting time**
  \[
  T_{haz\_wait} =
  \text{time ready for Packing/QC but blocked because } N_{haz}(t)=10
  \]

- **Hazardous order throughput:** completed hazardous orders per hour/shift/day.
- **Hazardous order lead time and P90/P95 lead time**
- **Non-hazardous indirect impact:** whether reserved capacity or hazardous scheduling creates idle resources or displacement effects.
- **Bottleneck location:** determine whether Packing, QC, or their combined regulatory pool creates the actual constraint.

#### Impact interpretation

The regulatory cap is a facility-wide, cross-instance capacity constraint. A local Packing station may be available, but a hazardous order may still be prohibited from starting because the global hazardous count is already 10. This distinction is essential: adding a packing station would not help if the compliance cap is the binding constraint.

---

### 1.6 Differentiating within-instance from between-instance waiting

A rigorous attribution framework is needed because an order can be delayed for multiple reasons.

#### Within-instance causes

These are delays or durations primarily associated with the order itself, such as:

- longer Picking/Packing/QC service duration;
- rework, quality failure, missing inventory, exception handling;
- order complexity, item count, special packaging requirements;
- system errors or documentation issues.

Indicators include:

- abnormally long activity duration while the resource is assigned;
- repeated activities or rework loops;
- case-specific exception events;
- delay occurring despite resource availability and no binding batch/compliance condition.

#### Between-instance causes

These result from dependencies on other orders:

- all cold-packing stations occupied by other orders;
- standard order overtaken by express work;
- batch waiting for other region-compatible orders;
- hazardous processing blocked by facility-wide concurrency count;
- queue growth caused by arrivals from other orders.

#### Attribution logic

For each waiting interval, reconstruct the system state and assign a cause hierarchy or multi-cause label:

| Waiting condition | Likely attribution |
|---|---|
| Cold order ready; all cold stations occupied | Cold-resource contention |
| Standard order ready; an express order starts ahead of it | Priority-induced delay |
| Order is QC-complete; waiting until regional batch release | Batch formation delay |
| Hazardous order ready; hazardous count equals 10 | Regulatory-cap delay |
| Resource available and no cross-case condition blocks start | Case-specific/operational delay |
| Multiple conditions simultaneously true | Shared or primary/secondary attribution |

For overlapping causes, use either:

- **primary cause attribution** based on the first blocking condition or strongest constraint; and
- **multi-label attribution** for root-cause analysis.

Statistical validation can supplement rule-based attribution. For example, model Packing queue time as a function of cold-station occupancy, express arrival rate, hazardous count, time of day, order complexity, and staffing level. This quantifies the marginal effect of each cross-instance factor.

## 2. Analyzing Constraint Interactions

The constraints should not be optimized in isolation because they can amplify or offset one another.

### 2.1 Express priority and cold-packing contention

An express order requiring cold packing may jump ahead of standard cold orders. This can improve express SLA performance but may:

- increase standard cold-order queue time;
- create repeated overtaking of the same standard orders;
- delay standard orders into later shipping batches;
- increase congestion at Quality Check if express orders flow through packing faster.

If preemption is used, partially completed standard packing work may also create additional setup, handling, or error risk.

The analysis should measure the effect by examining periods in which express cold orders arrive while the cold-station pool is fully utilized. These are the moments where priority policy has the greatest downstream impact.

---

### 2.2 Batching and hazardous-material limits

If several hazardous orders are destined for the same region, batching may encourage them to progress together. However, the hazardous concurrency limit may prevent them from entering Packing or QC simultaneously. Possible consequences include:

- delayed batch formation because hazardous orders are staggered;
- completed non-hazardous orders waiting for the hazardous orders needed to complete a regional batch;
- a batch-release rule inadvertently incentivizing accumulation of hazardous work;
- bursty releases from the hazardous queue, causing downstream batch instability.

Conversely, rigid batching can worsen hazardous congestion if it creates pressure to release many hazardous orders into Packing/QC at once.

---

### 2.3 Cold-packing and hazardous-material constraints

An order may require both cold packing and hazardous handling. Such an order consumes two scarce forms of capacity:

1. a cold-packing station; and
2. one slot in the facility-wide hazardous concurrency limit.

If either is unavailable, the order cannot proceed. This creates a synchronization problem. Reserving a cold station while waiting for a hazardous slot would waste specialized capacity; releasing the hazardous slot too early may create safety/compliance problems. Scheduling must coordinate both constraints.

---

### 2.4 Priority and hazardous compliance

Express hazardous orders are especially challenging. An express policy cannot override a legal concurrency cap. If the hazardous limit is already reached, the order cannot begin Packing or QC regardless of delivery urgency.

Without a clear policy, staff may make ad hoc choices, potentially causing:

- compliance risk;
- inconsistent express performance;
- starvation of standard hazardous orders;
- inefficient use of scarce hazardous capacity.

The scheduling rule should explicitly define how express hazardous work is prioritized within the legal cap, rather than treating it as an exception outside the model.

---

### 2.5 Batching and priority policy

An express order may be operationally complete but delayed by a batch waiting rule. If express orders are promised faster delivery, holding them for a standard regional batch may negate the benefit of upstream priority.

Possible solutions include express-specific batch thresholds, time-based release rules, or a controlled exception for shipping an express order independently when the SLA risk exceeds the transport-efficiency benefit.

---

### 2.6 Why interaction analysis matters

A local improvement can create a global deterioration:

- Adding cold-station capacity may shift congestion to QC or the hazardous cap.
- Releasing batches more frequently may reduce order lead time but increase transport cost and route inefficiency.
- Aggressive express prioritization may improve express SLA but worsen standard P95 lead time and increase WIP.
- Maximizing hazardous-cap utilization may increase throughput but create downstream bursts and quality-control congestion.

Therefore, optimization must use end-to-end KPIs and scenario simulation, not only activity-level utilization.

## 3. Constraint-Aware Optimization Strategies

### Strategy 1: Dynamic, constraint-aware dispatching for Packing and Quality Check

#### Constraints addressed

- Shared cold-packing capacity
- Express priority
- Hazardous-material concurrency limit
- Downstream QC congestion

#### Specific changes

Replace simple first-in-first-out or ad hoc priority rules with a dispatching policy that evaluates each ready order against:

- express/standard status;
- remaining SLA time;
- cold-packing requirement;
- hazardous status;
- current hazardous concurrency;
- availability of cold and standard stations;
- expected packing and QC duration;
- current and predicted downstream QC queue;
- regional batch cutoff or expected batch-release time.

A practical rule could be:

1. Never violate the hazardous-material cap.
2. Prioritize orders at greatest SLA risk, not all express orders equally.
3. For cold stations, reserve part of capacity or time windows for express demand based on forecast, rather than constantly preempting standard work.
4. Select hazardous orders only when both a feasible Packing/QC path and hazardous capacity are available.
5. Prevent standard-order starvation through an aging rule: the longer a standard order waits, the more its priority increases.

For example, rather than immediately interrupting a standard order whenever an express order arrives, permit non-preemptive completion of a nearly finished standard order unless the express SLA would be endangered.

#### Data and analysis used

- Historical arrival patterns by hour/day/season.
- Express demand forecast.
- Processing-time distributions by order type and station.
- Predicted delay to next activity.
- Historical relationship between WIP and cycle time.
- Process-mining-derived bottleneck and queue-state patterns.

#### Expected outcomes

- Lower express SLA misses without excessive standard-order tail delays.
- Reduced unnecessary interruptions and setup loss.
- Better cold-station utilization.
- Greater compliance reliability because hazardous decisions are embedded in dispatch logic.
- Reduced downstream blockage by avoiding releases into QC that cannot be absorbed.

---

### Strategy 2: Adaptive regional batching with maximum-wait and SLA-aware release rules

#### Constraints addressed

- Shipping batch dependencies
- Express priority
- Indirect effects from cold-packing and hazardous delays

#### Specific changes

Move from a purely size-based or fixed-time batch policy to an adaptive policy. A regional batch should be released when any of several triggers is met:

- target batch size achieved;
- maximum waiting time reached for the oldest eligible order;
- an express order approaches its dispatch/SLA cutoff;
- predicted arrivals are insufficient to fill the batch within an acceptable additional wait;
- a transportation cutoff is approaching;
- the marginal transport savings from waiting are lower than the cost of delayed delivery.

Possible differentiated rules:

- **Express:** smaller batch threshold and short maximum wait; allow independent release if necessary.
- **High-volume regions:** use more frequent, moderately sized batches.
- **Low-volume regions:** establish a strict maximum waiting time and use scheduled consolidation windows.
- **Hazardous orders:** avoid using batch rules that encourage hazardous WIP accumulation; release based on safe operational flow and dispatch feasibility.

A related redesign is to perform label generation earlier, while retaining route consolidation later. This decouples administrative completion from physical dispatch batching where operationally feasible.

#### Data and analysis used

- Regional order arrival-rate distributions.
- Historical batch size versus waiting time.
- Delivery/SLA performance by batch policy.
- Transport cost and route utilization data.
- Forecast of likely arrivals before the next dispatch cutoff.
- Delay propagation analysis from packing/QC to batch formation.

#### Expected outcomes

- Significant reduction in post-QC waiting.
- Faster release of express and aging standard orders.
- Better balance between delivery performance and transport efficiency.
- Reduced sensitivity of downstream shipping to upstream fluctuations.
- More stable end-to-end flow during peak periods.

---

### Strategy 3: Forecast-based cold-packing capacity and staffing management

#### Constraints addressed

- Shared cold-packing station scarcity
- Express cold-order surges
- Interaction with hazardous orders and downstream QC

#### Specific changes

Use historical demand and seasonal forecasts to predict cold-packing workload by shift. Then adjust capacity through a combination of:

- staffing cold stations according to predicted arrival peaks;
- cross-training standard-pack staff for cold-packing procedures, where legally and operationally permissible;
- extending cold-station operating windows during predictable peaks;
- scheduling breaks and maintenance outside peak cold-order periods;
- deploying temporary/mobile cold-capable capacity if feasible;
- introducing appointment-like release controls from Picking so cold orders do not arrive in uncontrolled bursts;
- evaluating the business case for adding one or more cold stations if demand regularly exceeds practical capacity.

Capacity investment should be assessed carefully. If the hazardous cap is frequently binding for cold-plus-hazardous orders, adding cold stations alone may not improve throughput. The analysis must identify which constraint is active when delays occur.

#### Data and analysis used

- Demand forecast by product mix, season, region, and hour.
- Cold-station occupancy and queue distributions.
- Counterfactual analysis: expected delay reduction from an additional station or staffing hour.
- Simulation of alternative station counts and shift patterns.
- Cost comparison of additional capacity versus late-delivery costs.

#### Expected outcomes

- Reduced cold-packing queue time and P90/P95 waiting.
- Fewer instances where express cold orders displace standard orders.
- Better peak-season resilience.
- More predictable flow into QC and shipping.
- Evidence-based capacity investment rather than intuition-driven expansion.

---

### Strategy 4: Hazardous-work release control and coordinated Packing–QC scheduling

#### Constraints addressed

- Hazardous-material concurrency limit
- QC congestion
- Interaction with batching and express priority

#### Specific changes

Implement a facility-wide hazardous-work controller that manages admission into Packing and QC. The controller should:

- maintain real-time hazardous concurrency count;
- reserve a small portion of hazardous capacity for urgent express work where appropriate;
- prevent starting hazardous Packing if QC is expected to be unavailable shortly afterward;
- sequence hazardous work to avoid large bursts;
- prioritize hazardous orders based on SLA risk, age, expected duration, and batch/dispatch cutoff;
- create a controlled “pull” mechanism: start the next hazardous order only when a slot is available and downstream capacity is viable.

The important change is to manage Packing and QC as a joint regulated flow rather than separate local queues.

#### Data and analysis used

- Hazardous concurrency time series.
- Hazardous packing and QC service-time distributions.
- Downstream QC availability and queue predictions.
- Express hazardous demand rate.
- Historical cap-binding periods and delays.
- Region/batch deadlines for hazardous orders.

#### Expected outcomes

- Continued compliance with the legal limit.
- Lower hazardous WIP and more stable throughput.
- Reduced waiting caused by a full cap.
- Less downstream blocking and fewer hazardous surges.
- Better predictability for batch completion and dispatch planning.

---

### Strategy 5: Decouple selected activities and reduce avoidable dependency

#### Constraints addressed

- Shipping batch waiting
- Priority interactions
- Resource contention caused by synchronized releases

#### Specific changes

Assess whether parts of the process can be decoupled without compromising quality or compliance:

- Generate shipping labels and shipment documents once an order passes QC, while retaining physical route consolidation for later.
- Stage completed orders by region and dispatch cutoff, using transparent “ready-to-ship” status.
- Pre-sort or pre-stage cold and hazardous orders after Picking to reduce setup time when a resource becomes available.
- Separate routing decisions from label creation, if systems allow.
- Use a virtual batch: record intended route batch early, but permit release exceptions for SLA-risk orders.

#### Data and analysis used

- Time spent between QC completion and label generation.
- Error/rework rates associated with earlier label creation.
- Batch delay by order class.
- Operational feasibility and IT-system constraints.
- Cost of exception shipments versus late-delivery cost.

#### Expected outcomes

- Reduced end-to-end lead time without necessarily eliminating route optimization.
- Less administrative delay hidden inside batch waiting.
- Better visibility of orders that are operationally complete but blocked by shipping policy.
- Easier intervention for express or aging orders.

---

## 4. Simulation and Validation

Before implementation, build a discrete-event simulation or digital-twin-style model calibrated from the event log. The simulation should represent orders as individual entities while maintaining shared facility state, so it can model the instance-spanning constraints explicitly.

### 4.1 Model structure

Each simulated order should include:

- order type: express or standard;
- destination region;
- cold-packing requirement;
- hazardous-material flag;
- arrival time;
- estimated work content/order complexity;
- service-time distributions for Picking, Packing, QC, and label generation;
- due date or service-level target.

Model resources should include:

- pickers;
- standard packing stations;
- five cold-packing stations, with possible alternatives;
- QC personnel;
- system/label-generation capacity;
- batching logic by region;
- staffing schedules, breaks, downtime, and shift calendars.

### 4.2 Explicit constraint logic

The simulation must enforce:

1. **Cold-packing capacity**
   - cold-required orders can only use cold-capable stations;
   - no more than the configured number of cold stations can be occupied.

2. **Batching**
   - orders can be held by region until batch release criteria are met;
   - policies can vary by order type and region;
   - dispatch cutoffs and batch-size thresholds should be represented.

3. **Priority**
   - express order ranking, overtaking rules, and any preemption logic;
   - setup/resumption costs if preemption occurs;
   - anti-starvation rules for standard orders.

4. **Hazardous-material regulation**
   - at most 10 hazardous orders across Packing and QC simultaneously;
   - count must update correctly as orders enter and exit either activity;
   - violations should be impossible under compliant scenarios and reported in stress tests.

5. **Interactions**
   - an order requiring both cold packing and hazardous handling must secure both feasible conditions;
   - downstream QC capacity should influence hazardous release decisions where applicable.

### 4.3 Calibration from process mining

Use the three-month log to estimate:

- arrival processes by hour, weekday, and peak season;
- processing-time distributions by order category;
- resource availability and shift patterns;
- actual routing probabilities and rework rates;
- batch-size and batch-formation patterns;
- priority behavior observed in practice;
- station downtime or unavailable periods;
- correlation between order features and processing time.

Do not assume exponential distributions by default. Empirical or fitted distributions should be validated against observed medians, percentiles, and peak behavior.

### 4.4 Scenarios to test

Compare a baseline model reproducing the current process with alternatives such as:

- current FIFO versus dynamic dispatching;
- non-preemptive versus limited-preemption express rules;
- five versus six/seven cold stations;
- alternative staffing schedules;
- fixed-size batching versus adaptive threshold/max-wait batching;
- reserved hazardous capacity for express work versus pure SLA-risk prioritization;
- earlier label generation or virtual batching;
- different hazardous release-control policies.

### 4.5 KPIs for scenario evaluation

Evaluate not only average performance but also service reliability and fairness:

- overall end-to-end lead time: mean, median, P90, P95;
- express SLA attainment;
- standard SLA attainment;
- standard-order P95 lead time and starvation incidence;
- cold-packing queue time and queue length;
- station utilization and idle time;
- hazardous throughput and hazardous waiting time;
- percentage of time hazardous limit is binding;
- regulatory violations, expected to remain zero;
- batch waiting time and average batch size;
- dispatch cutoff misses;
- orders completed per hour/day;
- WIP by stage and order class;
- transport cost/route efficiency where available;
- cost of added capacity or staffing.

A proposed policy should be accepted only if it improves the intended KPI without creating unacceptable deterioration elsewhere, such as a major increase in standard lead time or transport cost.

## 5. Monitoring Post-Implementation

Post-implementation monitoring should combine operational dashboards, process-mining conformance checking, and periodic policy recalibration.

### 5.1 Core end-to-end dashboards

Track by day, shift, region, order type, and order characteristic:

- end-to-end fulfillment lead time;
- median, P90, and P95 cycle time;
- on-time dispatch rate;
- express SLA attainment;
- standard SLA attainment;
- WIP by activity;
- throughput per hour/shift;
- aging orders approaching SLA breach;
- rework and exception rates.

Averages alone are not enough. Tail measures—especially P90/P95 waiting and lead time—show whether queues and starvation remain.

---

### 5.2 Shared cold-packing dashboard

Monitor:

- cold-station utilization by station and shift;
- all-stations-busy percentage;
- cold-order queue length over time;
- cold-packing waiting time, including P90/P95;
- number of express and standard cold orders waiting;
- express overtakes of standard cold orders;
- standard cold-order aging;
- throughput per cold station;
- demand forecast versus actual cold-order arrivals.

A successful intervention should show reduced queue length and waiting, fewer emergency priority displacements, and stable or improved station utilization—not merely lower utilization caused by lower demand.

---

### 5.3 Batching dashboard

Monitor by region:

- ready-to-ship orders awaiting a batch;
- batch formation time;
- batch waiting time per order;
- batch size and fill rate;
- batch release frequency;
- orders released due to maximum-wait or SLA trigger;
- express orders held for batching;
- missed dispatch cutoffs;
- transport utilization/cost indicators.

A successful batching redesign should reduce waiting after QC and cutoff misses while maintaining acceptable transportation efficiency.

---

### 5.4 Priority-policy dashboard

Monitor:

- express SLA attainment and lead time;
- standard lead time, particularly P90/P95;
- overtakes and displacement events;
- interruption frequency and duration, if preemption remains;
- number of standard orders exceeding an aging threshold;
- resource-specific impact of express arrivals;
- fairness indicators across order classes.

This helps prevent a policy from silently improving express metrics by shifting excessive delay to standard orders.

---

### 5.5 Hazardous-material compliance and flow dashboard

This must be real-time or near-real-time because it has compliance implications.

Monitor:

- current hazardous orders in Packing plus QC;
- maximum observed hazardous concurrency;
- percentage of time at 8, 9, and 10 hazardous orders;
- count and duration of cap-binding periods;
- hazardous queue length and hazardous waiting time;
- hazardous throughput;
- attempted or actual limit breaches;
- hazardous orders delayed near SLA cutoff;
- Packing-to-QC transition time for hazardous orders.

The target is not necessarily to maximize time at the cap. Persistent operation at 10 may indicate efficient use, but it may also indicate fragility and excessive queues. The dashboard should therefore pair cap utilization with hazardous waiting, QC congestion, and SLA performance.

---

### 5.6 Process-mining conformance and continuous improvement

Use conformance checking to verify that the redesigned policies are followed:

- cold-required orders use valid stations;
- hazardous orders do not exceed the concurrency rule;
- express handling follows approved escalation logic;
- batch release follows the configured rules;
- unapproved manual workarounds are detected.

Set automated alerts for:

- cold queue above threshold;
- sustained all-cold-stations-busy periods;
- batch waiting exceeding maximum permitted time;
- standard orders aging beyond threshold;
- hazardous concurrency approaching 10;
- any hazardous compliance breach;
- deteriorating P95 lead time;
- regional batch performance anomalies.

Finally, recalibrate forecasts, dispatch thresholds, and batch policies periodically—especially before peak seasons. The event log should become a continuous feedback source: identify whether delays have moved to another stage, validate whether the intended cross-instance constraint is less binding, and adjust policy using measured evidence rather than one-time assumptions.
