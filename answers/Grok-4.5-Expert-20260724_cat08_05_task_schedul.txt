**1. Analyzing Historical Scheduling Performance and Dynamics**

Process mining begins by treating the MES event log as a rich, timestamped event stream. Each case (Job ID) is reconstructed as an end-to-end process instance by ordering events chronologically and linking successive activities via the Case ID. Resource and operator attributes allow multi-perspective mining: control-flow discovery, performance annotation, and organizational analysis.

**Process discovery and flow reconstruction.**  
Apply inductive miner or heuristics miner (with noise thresholds tuned to the high-mix environment) to discover the actual process map, including infrequent variants caused by rework or hot-job insertions. Multi-perspective discovery (control-flow + resource) yields a resource-annotated model showing which machines execute which tasks and the observed sequences on each machine. Case-level replay produces the true job routing for every order, revealing deviations from any idealized bill-of-process.

**Key metrics extracted via performance mining and log enrichment:**

- **Job flow times, lead times, and makespan.**  
  For each case compute (completion timestamp − release timestamp). Aggregate into distributions (mean, median, 90th/95th percentiles, coefficient of variation). Makespan for a planning horizon is obtained by summing or taking the maximum completion times of all jobs released in that window. Process mining tools annotate the discovered model with these statistics and produce dotted charts that visualize flow-time variability across the year.

- **Task waiting (queue) times.**  
  For every “Queue Entry” → “Task Start” pair on a given resource, calculate the waiting duration. Group by work center / machine ID to produce queue-time heat maps and cumulative distribution functions. Identify machines whose waiting-time distributions are heavy-tailed.

- **Resource utilization.**  
  From the log intervals “Setup Start–End”, “Task Start–End”, and machine-level idle periods (gaps between consecutive tasks on the same resource), compute three fractions for each machine and operator: productive processing time, setup time, and idle/starvation time. Utilization = (processing + setup) / available calendar time. Operator utilization can be further broken down by skill or shift.

- **Sequence-dependent setup times.**  
  For each machine, extract the ordered sequence of jobs processed on it. For every consecutive pair (Job A, Job B) where a Setup event exists, record the actual setup duration and the pair of job attributes that drive change-over (material, geometry family, tooling, fixture, etc.). Build a setup-time matrix (or a regression / decision-tree model) that predicts setup duration from the attribute delta of the preceding and succeeding jobs. Aggregate statistics (mean, variance, frequency of long setups) quantify the cost of poor sequencing.

- **Schedule adherence and tardiness.**  
  For each completed job compare actual completion timestamp with the Order Due Date attribute. Compute tardiness = max(0, completion − due date), percentage of tardy jobs, mean and total tardiness, and service level (fraction of jobs completed on or before due date). Conformance checking against a “due-date-aware” reference model highlights systematic deviations.

- **Impact of disruptions.**  
  Isolate intervals tagged “Breakdown Start–End” or priority-change events. Measure the increase in waiting time and flow time of jobs that were in queue or in process at the moment of disruption, and the propagation to downstream resources (ripple effect). Compare KPIs in disruption-free windows versus disruption windows to quantify sensitivity.

All of the above are obtained by standard process-mining operations: filtering, enrichment (adding derived attributes such as remaining operations or cumulative setup), performance annotation, and multi-dimensional dashboards.

**2. Diagnosing Scheduling Pathologies**

Process-mining diagnostics directly surface the pathologies that the simple FCFS/EDD mix produces.

- **Bottleneck identification and impact.**  
  Bottleneck analysis ranks machines by average queue length, waiting time, and utilization. Critical machines (often specialized CNC or heat-treatment units) show utilization > 90 % and waiting-time means several times larger than processing times. Throughput contribution analysis (or “bottleneck strength”) quantifies how much overall system makespan or tardiness is attributable to each bottleneck.

- **Poor prioritization.**  
  Variant analysis partitions cases into on-time versus tardy cohorts. Tardy jobs exhibit systematically higher waiting times at the same resources, even when their original priority was Medium or High. Dotted charts colored by priority reveal that urgent jobs are frequently overtaken by lower-priority jobs that arrived earlier (classic FCFS dominance).

- **Suboptimal sequencing and excess setup.**  
  The mined setup-time matrix shows that long setups occur far more often than necessary; jobs with compatible attributes are rarely sequenced consecutively. On bottleneck machines the cumulative setup time can reach 25–35 % of calendar time—evidence that local dispatching ignores sequence dependence.

- **Downstream starvation.**  
  Resource-contention period analysis and social-network / handover graphs show intervals in which a downstream flexible machine sits idle while an upstream bottleneck is occupied by a job that does not feed that machine. WIP heat maps reveal inventory piles immediately upstream of bottlenecks and near-empty queues downstream.

- **WIP bullwhip / variability amplification.**  
  Time-series of WIP levels (number of active cases) display large oscillations whose amplitude increases from upstream to downstream stages—classic variability propagation caused by uncoordinated local rules and batch-like release behavior after disruptions.

These diagnoses are obtained by combining performance mining, comparative process mining (on-time vs. late, high- vs. low-priority, disrupted vs. stable periods), and resource-perspective analysis.

**3. Root Cause Analysis of Scheduling Ineffectiveness**

Process mining helps separate scheduling-logic failures from pure capacity or stochastic problems.

- **Limitations of static dispatching.**  
  Local FCFS/EDD rules never see global state (downstream queue lengths, remaining work, sequence-dependent setups). Conformance and performance analysis show that the same rule set produces good results only in low-load, low-disruption windows and collapses under realistic conditions—evidence that the logic itself is inadequate rather than merely under-capacity.

- **Lack of real-time visibility.**  
  The event log itself demonstrates that the MES already captures the necessary state; the scheduling system simply does not consume it. Queue-length and machine-status distributions at decision epochs are highly variable, yet dispatching decisions ignore them.

- **Inaccurate duration and setup estimates.**  
  Planned versus actual duration analysis reveals systematic bias and high variance; setup times are often treated as constant or ignored. Predictive models fitted on historical data significantly outperform the static estimates used (or not used) by the current system.

- **Ineffective sequence-dependent handling and poor coordination.**  
  Setup matrices and inter-resource handover statistics show that no global sequencing logic exists; each work center optimizes myopically.

- **Reactive rather than proactive disruption handling.**  
  After a breakdown or hot-job insertion, the log shows long recovery tails; no look-ahead re-sequencing or protective capacity is applied.

By comparing periods of high versus low load, and by examining the residual variance after accounting for observed resource capacity, process mining isolates the fraction of tardiness and WIP attributable to scheduling decisions versus irreducible capacity shortfalls or process noise. When residual performance gaps remain large after capacity is controlled for, the dominant root cause is the scheduling logic itself.

**4. Developing Advanced Data-Driven Scheduling Strategies**

Three complementary strategies, each directly informed by the mined insights:

**Strategy 1 – Multi-attribute Dynamic Dispatching with Sequence-Dependent Setup Awareness**  
At each machine, when a resource becomes free, rank the jobs currently in its queue by a composite priority index:

\[
P_j = w_1\cdot\frac{1}{\text{slack}_j} + w_2\cdot\text{priority}_j + w_3\cdot\frac{1}{\text{remaining processing time}_j} + w_4\cdot\frac{1}{\widehat{s}_{i,j}} + w_5\cdot\text{downstream urgency}_j
\]

where \(\widehat{s}_{i,j}\) is the predicted setup time from the currently loaded job \(i\) to candidate \(j\) (looked up or predicted from the mined setup matrix), and downstream urgency is derived from the current queue lengths and remaining work on subsequent resources (mined from historical flow). Weights \(w\) are calibrated offline on historical data so that the rule minimizes a weighted combination of tardiness and total setup time.  

This directly attacks poor prioritization, excess setups, and lack of coordination. Expected impact: 30–50 % reduction in mean tardiness, 15–25 % reduction in cumulative setup time, modest WIP reduction through faster flow of near-due jobs.

**Strategy 2 – Predictive, Distribution-Aware Finite-Capacity Scheduling**  
Fit empirical or parametric distributions of processing times (conditioned on machine, operator, job family) and setup times from the log. Use these distributions inside a rolling-horizon scheduler (e.g., predictive discrete-event simulation or stochastic integer programming with sample-average approximation) that generates a short-term schedule for the next 8–24 h. The schedule is re-optimized whenever a significant event (breakdown, hot job, large deviation) occurs. Predictive maintenance signals (if derivable from downtime patterns) further adjust machine availability calendars.  

Addresses inaccurate estimates, reactive disruption handling, and lack of look-ahead. Expected impact: more reliable lead-time quotations, lower variance of flow times, proactive avoidance of secondary bottlenecks, 20–40 % improvement in service level.

**Strategy 3 – Setup-Optimized Sequencing and Intelligent Batching at Bottlenecks**  
On identified bottleneck machines, replace pure dispatching by a short-horizon sequencing heuristic (e.g., nearest-neighbor or genetic algorithm on the setup graph) that minimizes total predicted setup time while respecting due-date windows. Compatible jobs are preferentially grouped into “setup families” whose size is limited by the mined WIP and due-date pressure. Upstream release control can be adjusted to feed the bottleneck with a sequence that already favors low-setup transitions.  

Directly targets the excess-setup pathology and bottleneck inefficiency. Expected impact: 20–35 % reduction in setup time on critical resources, higher effective capacity, lower WIP upstream of the bottleneck, and secondary reductions in tardiness.

All three strategies consume the same process-mining artifacts (setup matrices, duration distributions, bottleneck rankings, priority-versus-tardiness relationships) and can be combined (dynamic dispatching as the real-time layer, predictive scheduling as the planning layer, setup optimization as a specialized subroutine on bottlenecks).

**5. Simulation, Evaluation, and Continuous Improvement**

**Simulation framework.**  
Construct a discrete-event simulation model whose parameters are taken directly from the process-mining output:

- routing probabilities and sequences from the discovered process map,
- processing- and setup-time distributions (including sequence dependence),
- arrival processes, priority distributions, and due-date tightness,
- breakdown inter-arrival and repair-time distributions,
- operator availability calendars.

The baseline policy (current FCFS/EDD mix) and each of the three proposed strategies are implemented as interchangeable dispatching/sequencing modules. Controlled experiments are run under multiple scenarios: nominal load, high load (120–140 % of average), frequent disruptions, and mixed priority mixes. Primary outputs are the same KPIs already measured on the historical log (tardiness distribution, mean and variance of flow time, WIP trajectory, utilization, total setup time). Statistical comparison (paired t-tests or bootstrap confidence intervals across replications) quantifies improvement and robustness before any live pilot.

**Continuous monitoring and adaptation.**  
Once a strategy is deployed, the MES continues to generate the same event stream. A process-mining cockpit recomputes the KPI dashboards and the diagnostic indicators (bottleneck strength, setup-time fraction, on-time service level, WIP variability) on a daily or shift basis. Drift detection (e.g., change-point analysis on flow-time or tardiness time series, or concept-drift methods on the process model) flags statistically significant degradations. When drift is detected, the simulation model is re-parameterized with the newest data, the affected strategy components (weights, sequencing heuristics, distribution models) are re-optimized offline, and the updated logic is re-injected. This closes the loop: process mining supplies both the initial insight and the ongoing feedback that keeps the scheduling system aligned with the evolving shop-floor reality.

The overall approach therefore converts the historical MES log into a quantitative understanding of current performance and pathologies, isolates root causes, designs adaptive data-driven policies, validates them rigorously in simulation, and sustains improvement through continuous process mining.
