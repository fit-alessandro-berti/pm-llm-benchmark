$$\pi_j = w_1 \cdot \text{EDD-Urgency}_j + w_2 \cdot \text{Critical-Ratio}_j + w_3 \cdot \text{Priority-Weight}_j + w_4 \cdot \text{Downstream-Load}_j - w_5 \cdot \hat{S}(j_{prev}, j) + w_6 \cdot \text{Queue-Age}_j$$

Where:
*   **EDD-Urgency / Critical Ratio:** Standard due-date urgency and remaining processing time ratios, derived from mined flow time distributions.
*   **Priority Weight:** Customer tier or contract penalty (from `Order Priority`).
*   **Downstream Load:** The predicted queue time at the *next* critical bottleneck machine (e.g., if `Milling` is the bottleneck, avoid sending a job there if its queue is exploding). This requires real-time queue state integration.
*   **Estimated Setup Cost $\hat{S}(j_{prev}, j)$:** The expected setup time based on the mined transition matrix $S(i,j)$. The rule penalizes jobs that require expensive changeovers, *unless* urgency or priority overwhelmingly demands them.
*   **Queue Age:** Prevents starvation of jobs that have waited excessively.

**Process Mining Informed Weighting:** We use regression analysis (or reinforcement learning on historical traces) to learn weights $w_k$ that minimize historical tardiness. For example, if the logs show that at `CUT-01`, reducing setup time had twice the impact on flow time compared to EDD, $w_5$ is increased for that machine.

**Addressing Pathologies:** This directly combats poor prioritization (urgency/priority factors), bottleneck starvation (downstream load factor), suboptimal sequencing (setup cost factor), and starvation (age factor).

If the probability of tardiness for high-priority jobs exceeds a threshold (e.g., >20%), the system automatically triggers rescheduling—either by rerouting jobs to alternative machines (if flexible) or by invoking Strategy 1's dispatching rules with updated priorities.

**Addressing Pathologies:** This tackles unpredictable lead times (probabilistic estimates), inadequate disruption response (automated resimulation), and inaccurate planning (using actual distributions, not deterministic averages).

**Expected Impact:** Dramatically improved delivery promise accuracy; proactive identification of "at-risk" jobs 12-24 hours in advance; reduced reaction time to disruptions from hours to minutes.

### Strategy 3: Bottleneck Setup Optimization via Intelligent Batching and TSP-Based Sequencing
**Core Logic:** At identified bottleneck machines (e.g., `MILL-03`), we do not dispatch one-by-one. Instead, we maintain a rolling "optimization window" (e.g., all jobs currently queued or expected within the next 2 hours).

1.  **Family Clustering:** Using job attributes and the mined setup matrix, jobs are clustered by family (similar tooling/material).
2.  **Batch and Sequence Optimization:** Within the window, we solve a constrained optimization problem—similar to a **Traveling Salesman Problem with Time Windows (TSP-TW)**—to find the job sequence that minimizes:
    $$\sum_{k} \hat{S}(J_k, J_{k+1}) + \lambda \cdot \sum_{k} \max(0, C_k - D_k)$$
    This minimizes total setup time while penalizing due-date violations.
3.  **Dynamic Re-optimization:** Every time a new job arrives or a disruption changes availability, the optimization is re-solved.

**Process Mining Data Usage:** The objective function coefficients $\hat{S}$ come directly from the mined transition matrix. The constraint sets (due dates, job availability) come from real-time event streams (`Queue Entry`, `Job Released`). The identification of which machines should run this algorithm comes directly from the bottleneck analysis in Section 2.

**Addressing Pathologies:** Directly attacks excessive setup times at bottlenecks, increases effective bottleneck capacity (more time available for processing), and reduces flow time variance, thereby lowering WIP.

**Expected Impact:** 15-25% increase in effective bottleneck throughput; significant reduction in flow time for jobs passing through the bottleneck; lower WIP accumulation upstream.

## 5. Simulation, Evaluation, and Continuous Improvement

### 5.1 Discrete-Event Simulation (DES) for Rigorous Strategy Evaluation
Before live deployment, we construct a high-fidelity **Discrete-Event Simulation** (e.g., using SimPy or a specialized manufacturing DES package) parameterized entirely from process mining outputs.

**Parameterization from Mined Data:**
*   **Task Time Distributions:** Empirical distributions fitted to `Task Duration (Actual)` per machine/activity.
*   **Routing Logic:** Probabilistic routing tables extracted from the discovered process model.
*   **Setup Times:** The family-level transition matrix $\hat{S}(i,j)$ mined from historical sequences, applied dynamically based on the simulated job sequence.
*   **Resource Availability & Breakdowns:** Empirical inter-arrival and repair distributions derived from `Breakdown Start`/`End` logs.
*   **Arrival and Release Patterns:** Historical job release patterns from the `Job Released` events, including urgency injections (`Priority Change`).

**Test Scenarios:**
We run comparative simulations for the **Baseline** (current FCFS/EDD mix) against **Strategy 1**, **Strategy 2**, and **Strategy 3** (individually and in hybrid combinations).

Specific stress-test scenarios include:
1.  **Baseline Load:** Historical average arrival rate.
2.  **Peak Load / High WIP:** 150% of average job release rate to test robustness under congestion.
3.  **Disruption-Heavy Environment:** Elevated breakdown frequency (e.g., 2x historical rate) and frequent hot-job injections (`Priority Change`) to test adaptive response.
4.  **Bottleneck Degradation:** Simulating a 20% capacity reduction at the primary bottleneck (`MILL-03`) to see how strategies reroute or sequence jobs.
5.  **New Product Introduction:** Injecting jobs with unknown routings but similar family characteristics to test the robustness of the family-clustering in Strategy 3.

**Evaluation Metrics in Simulation:** Average and P95 flow time; tardiness rate and magnitude; average and peak WIP levels at each station; resource utilization profiles; total setup time per shift; and service-level achievement by priority class.

### 5.2 Framework for Continuous Monitoring and Adaptation
Once the optimal strategy (likely a hybrid: Strategy 1 for general dispatching, Strategy 3 at bottlenecks, Strategy 2 for predictive monitoring) is deployed, we establish a **closed-loop digital twin** with continuous process mining.

**Real-Time KPI Tracking:**
We maintain an automated dashboard computing the metrics from Section 1 on rolling windows (hourly, daily). Key Performance Indicators include:
*   System-wide tardiness rate and average flow time.
*   Queue length and waiting time distributions per machine.
*   Actual vs. predicted setup time ratios (to detect tool wear or material changes).
*   Bottleneck shift indicators (if `MILL-03` utilization drops and `CUT-01` rises, the bottleneck has migrated).

**Drift Detection and Automatic Adaptation:**
Using **Statistical Process Control (SPC)** on the mined metrics:
*   **Scheduling Logic Drift:** If the correlation between the DMFD score ($\pi_j$) and actual flow time weakens, or if tardiness increases despite constant load, the weight vector $\mathbf{w}$ is automatically flagged for retraining using recent log data.
*   **Process Drift:** If task duration distributions shift (detected via Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests on new `Task Duration (Actual)` data), the predictive models in Strategy 2 are updated.
*   **Structural Drift:** If a new job family appears frequently, causing unknown high-setup transitions, Strategy 3's clustering model is updated, and a new family code is added to the transition matrix.

**Feedback Loop:** Every scheduling decision (which job was picked, what sequence was used) is logged as an event in the MES. This creates a "meta-log" of scheduling actions. Periodically, we mine this meta-log against outcomes (tardiness, flow time) to perform **causal process mining**—determining exactly which scheduling actions under which conditions (load, disruption presence) led to success or failure. This feeds back into the weight optimization and predictive model training, ensuring the system evolves with the factory.

For Precision Parts Inc., the transition from simplistic local rules to this integrated framework—rooted in rigorous process mining of actual flow dynamics, informed by sequence-dependent setup analytics, and executed through adaptive, predictive, and bottleneck-optimized strategies—offers the only viable path to controlling WIP, stabilizing lead times, and meeting due dates in a high-mix, disruption-heavy job shop. The continuous simulation and monitoring loop ensures that the system does not become static again, but rather learns and adapts as the manufacturing environment evolves.
