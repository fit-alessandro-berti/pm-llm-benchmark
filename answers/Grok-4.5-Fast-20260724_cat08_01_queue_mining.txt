**1. Queue Identification and Characterization**

Waiting time (queue time) between consecutive activities is defined as the elapsed time from the *completion* timestamp of activity \(i\) to the *start* timestamp of activity \(i+1\) for the same case (visit ID), provided the activities are sequential in the observed process path and no other intervening activity is recorded. This captures pure idle/waiting time experienced by the patient (or the case) rather than activity service time. Service time itself is simply complete minus start for a given activity. Cases with missing timestamps, overlapping activities on the same resource, or parallel branches require careful handling (e.g., via process discovery to establish the control-flow skeleton first, then pairwise successive transitions).

Key metrics to compute per transition (e.g., Registration → Nurse Assessment, Nurse Assessment → Doctor Consultation [specialty], Doctor Consultation → ECG, etc.) include:
- Average, median, and maximum waiting time.
- 90th and 95th percentile waiting times (critical for understanding tail experiences that drive dissatisfaction).
- Queue frequency: absolute number and percentage of cases that traverse the transition.
- Number/percentage of cases exceeding clinically or operationally defined thresholds (e.g., >15 min, >30 min).
- Distribution statistics (standard deviation, coefficient of variation) and temporal patterns (by hour of day, day of week, month).
- Conditioned variants: waiting times sliced by patient type (New vs. Follow-up), urgency, specialty, or resource.

Critical queues are identified by a multi-criteria ranking that prioritizes impact rather than raw length alone. Primary criteria: (a) high volume × high average/percentile wait (total patient-hours wasted), (b) disproportionately long tails affecting vulnerable groups (New patients, Urgent cases), (c) position early in the pathway (cascading effect on downstream throughput), and (d) contribution to overall end-to-end visit duration. A simple composite score (normalized average wait × frequency × cascade weight) plus visual inspection of dotted charts and performance dashboards quickly surfaces the top 2–4 transitions requiring immediate attention. Resource-perspective filters (e.g., waits before a particular doctor or room) further refine prioritization.

**2. Root Cause Analysis**

Significant queues rarely stem from a single factor. Common root causes in this multi-specialty outpatient setting include:
- Resource bottlenecks: limited concurrent capacity of high-demand resources (specialist physicians, specific diagnostic rooms/equipment, skilled nurses). High utilization (>85–90 %) combined with variability produces long queues.
- Activity dependencies and handovers: strict sequential flow (e.g., nurse assessment must finish before doctor can start) plus manual or delayed hand-off of information.
- High variability in service times: some consultations or tests have long-tailed durations due to case complexity, documentation burden, or interruptions.
- Appointment scheduling policies: fixed slot lengths that ignore case mix, insufficient buffer for New/Urgent patients, or overbooking without real-time capacity awareness.
- Patient arrival patterns: batch arrivals at opening, no-shows, or early/late arrivals that desynchronize the schedule.
- Heterogeneity by patient attributes: New patients and certain specialties systematically require longer service or more steps, amplifying queues when not segmented.

Process mining techniques applied to the event log go well beyond simple queue calculation:
- Bottleneck analysis and performance annotation of the discovered process model (directly-follows graph or Petri net) highlight high-wait arcs and high-utilization resources.
- Resource analysis / social network mining and dotted charts reveal workload imbalance, idle time of staff, and concurrent case load per resource.
- Variant analysis and decision-point mining expose path differences (e.g., New-patient paths with extra diagnostic steps versus streamlined Follow-up paths) and the contextual factors (attributes) that correlate with long waits.
- Service-time distribution mining and simulation-ready models quantify variability and enable what-if testing of capacity changes.
- Alignment-based conformance checking identifies deviations (skipped steps, unexpected loops) that inflate waiting.

Together these techniques move from “where is the queue?” to “why does this queue form under which conditions?”.

**3. Data-Driven Optimization Strategies**

Three concrete strategies grounded in the above analysis:

**Strategy A – Dynamic, case-mix-aware appointment scheduling and slot differentiation.**  
Target queues: Registration → Nurse Assessment and Nurse Assessment → Doctor Consultation (especially for New and specialty cases).  
Root cause addressed: mismatch between scheduled capacity and actual demand mix/variability, plus batch arrivals.  
Data support: historical service-time distributions and arrival patterns by patient type/urgency/specialty extracted from the event log; simulation of alternative slot templates shows reduced peak congestion.  
Expected impact: 20–35 % reduction in average morning waiting time for the two earliest queues and 10–15 % shorter overall visit duration, with modest improvement in on-time starts.

**Strategy B – Cross-trained floating nurse pool + real-time workload balancing for assessment and pre-consult preparation.**  
Target queues: Nurse Assessment and the subsequent hand-over to Doctor Consultation.  
Root cause addressed: localized nurse capacity shortages and sequential hand-over delays.  
Data support: resource utilization profiles and concurrent case counts per nurse/room show under-utilized capacity in some areas while others queue; dotted-chart analysis confirms idle pockets.  
Expected impact: 25–40 % reduction in Nurse Assessment waiting time and smoother doctor start times, achieved largely by reallocation rather than net new headcount.

**Strategy C – Selective parallelization and pre-emptive diagnostics for high-probability pathways (e.g., standing orders or rapid ECG/lab for selected New/Cardio patients once nurse assessment flags the need).**  
Target queues: Doctor Consultation → Diagnostic Test and overall end-to-end duration.  
Root cause addressed: strict sequential dependencies and late discovery of test requirements.  
Data support: frequent-itemset and variant analysis reveal high co-occurrence of certain specialties with specific tests; process models quantify the time saved by moving selected tests earlier.  
Expected impact: 15–30 min reduction in total visit time for the affected cohort (often 20–40 % of New patients) and secondary relief of doctor waiting rooms.

**4. Consideration of Trade-offs and Constraints**

- Strategy A may increase administrative overhead for more sophisticated booking rules and could slightly raise no-show risk if slots become less flexible; it must be monitored for equity across patient groups.
- Strategy B risks higher cognitive load on floating nurses and potential dilution of specialty-specific expertise; careful rostering and decision-support tools are required to avoid care-quality erosion.
- Strategy C can raise diagnostic volume (and cost) if over-triggered and may create secondary queues at test resources if capacity is not co-adjusted; false-positive standing orders must be tightly governed by clinical protocols.

Balancing objectives is achieved by multi-objective evaluation: primary KPIs (wait times, visit duration) are optimized subject to hard constraints on cost (no large permanent headcount increase), quality (no increase in clinical incidents or incomplete assessments), and staff workload (utilization caps). Discrete-event simulation driven by the mined process model and service-time distributions allows quantitative trade-off curves before implementation. Pilot testing on one specialty or one day-of-week further de-risks rollout.

**5. Measuring Success**

Core post-implementation KPIs (computed identically from the continuing event log):
- Average, median, and 90th-percentile waiting time per critical transition and overall.
- Mean and 90th-percentile end-to-end visit duration (first registration start to final check-out complete).
- Percentage of visits completing within target thresholds (e.g., ≤90 min total for Follow-up, ≤120 min for New).
- Resource utilization and idle-time distributions (to detect bottleneck migration).
- Secondary: patient satisfaction scores linked to visit IDs if available, staff overtime, and any quality/safety proxies (e.g., incomplete documentation rates).

Ongoing process monitoring uses the same event-log schema streamed or batched into a process-mining platform. Dashboards refresh daily/weekly with the above metrics, control charts, and automated alerts when a KPI drifts beyond statistical limits or when a new dominant variant appears. Periodic re-discovery of the process model and re-calculation of bottleneck rankings ensure that improvements are sustained and that any unintended shifts (new queues) are detected early. A formal before–after comparison (including statistical significance testing) at 30/90/180 days, supplemented by qualitative staff and patient feedback, closes the improvement loop.
