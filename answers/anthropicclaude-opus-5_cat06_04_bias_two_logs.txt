- Non-boosted cases: 710  rejected (twice); 720  approved. Threshold sits somewhere in **(710, 720]**.

The log does not contain the attributes needed to distinguish these. That is itself a finding: the process is **not auditable**. A boost of a stated magnitude that produces effects larger than its stated magnitude is a governance failure regardless of which explanation is true.

## 9. Caveats

Three cases per group, fifteen events per log. Nothing here supports a statistical claim; confidence intervals on a 2/3 approval rate span nearly the whole unit interval. What the sample *does* establish is the existence of a **documented causal mechanism** — a rule that adds points on the basis of an attribute structurally unavailable to the protected group. That mechanism is a design fact, not a sampling artefact, and it does not require a large n to identify. The correct next step is to quantify its prevalence and effect size on a full population.

## 10. Recommendations

**Diagnostic**
1. Extract the actual Rules Engine thresholds and confirm whether a second, lower cut-off applies to community members.
2. Re-run the analysis conditional on score band (e.g. 690–700, 700–710, 710–720) rather than in aggregate; that is where the disparity appears.
3. Compute correlation between `LocalResident`/`CommunityGroup` and the protected attribute across the full log to confirm the proxy relationship at scale.

**Remedial**
4. Demand a documented business justification for the boost: is membership of a darts club predictive of repayment behaviour, net of the existing score features? If not, it fails a legitimate-aim test and should be removed outright.
5. If some locality effect *is* genuinely predictive, replace the categorical club-membership flag with the underlying predictive variable, and make an equivalent pathway available to non-residents.
6. Blind the Underwriter to the provenance of any adjustment — present a single score, no "(Adjusted)" label, no "Community Boost" annotation.

**Governance**
7. Standardise `ScoreAdjustment` encoding across all cases; never mix `N/A` and `0`.
8. Add continuous monitoring on score-matched approval rates, rank-inversion counts, and counterfactual flips — not just demographic parity, which this process would have passed.

**The single most transferable lesson:** equal approval rates are not evidence of fairness. Here they were achieved while a 695-point applicant was approved and a 710-point applicant was rejected. Aggregate parity can coexist with — and conceal — systematic individual-level unfairness.
