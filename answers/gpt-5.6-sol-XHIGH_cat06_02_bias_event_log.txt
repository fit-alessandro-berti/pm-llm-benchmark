## Where bias appears

### 1. Explicit community-affiliation bonus
Membership in the **Highland Civic Darts Club** produces a `+10 (Community)` score adjustment:

- **C001:** 710  720  Approved
- **C004:** 690  700  Approved

Applicants with no affiliation receive no comparable adjustment. This directly privileges membership in a specific organization, despite no evidence that membership predicts creditworthiness.

The bonus can be outcome-determinative. C001 had a lower preliminary score than C003—710 versus 715—but the affiliation bonus moved C001 above C003, after which C001 was approved and C003 rejected.

### 2. Apparent geographic preference
All three local residents were approved, while one of two non-local applicants was rejected:

- Local residents: **3/3 approved**
- Non-local residents: **1/2 approved**

The strongest comparison is:

- **C004:** local, club member, base score 690, adjusted score 700, approved
- **C003:** non-local, no affiliation, score 715, rejected

C004 was approved despite having a substantially lower underlying and adjusted score. This suggests that locality, affiliation, or an undocumented rule associated with those attributes may create a lower effective approval threshold.

The log does not allow locality and club membership to be fully separated because every club member is also local. Nor does it prove that locality caused the decisions. Nevertheless, the pattern warrants investigation.

### 3. Opaque or inconsistent final-decision rules
If credit score were the principal criterion, approving C004 at 700 while rejecting C003 at 715 is difficult to explain. Possible explanations include:

- a preferential threshold for local residents or club members;
- an undocumented exception in the rules engine;
- an unrecorded manual-review factor; or
- missing decision variables in the log.

Because the final decisions are attributed to the **Rules Engine**, the apparent disparity may be embedded in automated rules rather than arising solely from individual reviewers.

### 4. Potential manual-review bias
Reviewers can see `LocalResident` and `CommunityGroup`. Even if they do not formally alter the score, access to these attributes can influence discretionary judgments. Different reviewers handled each case, and the log does not provide review recommendations or reason codes, so reviewer consistency cannot be assessed.

## Fairness and equity implications

These practices undermine the principle that similarly creditworthy applicants should receive similar outcomes:

- Newcomers, non-local residents, socially isolated individuals, and people without access to favored organizations receive no bonus.
- Community membership and geography can proxy for socioeconomic status, ethnicity, age, nationality, or social networks, creating indirect discrimination.
- A small bonus can have a large effect near a decision threshold.
- C004 suggests the advantage may extend beyond the stated 10 points.
- High-scoring non-local applicants can still succeed, as C005 did at 740, but non-local applicants near the cutoff may face a higher effective standard.

If local residence is a legitimate eligibility condition—for example, for a locality-funded program—it should be transparent and assessed separately from creditworthiness. Membership in a specific club is much harder to justify as a credit-risk criterion.

## Overall assessment

The clearest bias is the **explicit +10 preference for a named community group**. The decision pattern also suggests a possible **geographic or combined locality-affiliation preference**, especially because C004 was approved below C003’s rejected score. The sample is too small to establish statistical discrimination, but it provides strong evidence for an audit of the adjustment, decision thresholds, manual-review practices, and rules-engine logic. Counterfactual testing—changing only locality or affiliation while holding all credit factors constant—would help determine whether these attributes materially drive outcomes.
