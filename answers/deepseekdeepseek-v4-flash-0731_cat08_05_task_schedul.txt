## Data-Driven Process Mining and Advanced Scheduling Framework for Precision Parts Inc.

The core proposal is to transform the MES event log from a passive record into an active decision-support asset. The plan has five components: reconstructing actual shop-floor behavior; diagnosing structural pathologies; identifying root causes; designing advanced scheduling strategies; and continuously evaluating and adapting those strategies through simulation and monitoring.

## 1. Analyzing Historical Scheduling Performance and Dynamics

### 1.1 Event-Log Engineering for Process Mining

The MES event log must first be transformed into a standard event log for process mining. Each row is converted into event tuples of the form:

- **Case ID**: `JOB-7001`
- **Activity**: e.g., `Setup Start`, `Task End`, `Queue Entry (Milling)`
- **Resource**: e.g., `CUT-01`, `MILL-03`, `OP-105`
- **Timestamp**: e.g., `2025-04-21 09:38:55`
- **Attributes**: priority, due date, planned/actual duration, setup flag, previous job, etc.

Two complementary views are created:

1. **Job-centric log**: traces each job through its full sequence of events, from release to completion.
2. **Resource-centric log**: traces each machine/operator through their workload and idle periods, allowing reconstruction of the sequence of jobs processed on each resource.

This dual view is essential because scheduling performance arises from the interaction between job flows and resource availability.

### 1.2 Reconstructing Actual Flows and Sequences

**Process discovery** is used to reconstruct the *actual* routing of jobs. Since Precision Parts is a high-mix job shop, the discovered process will be a complex directly-follows graph (DFG). The DFG should be annotated with:

- frequency of transitions between operations;
- average/median waiting time between operations;
- probabilities of rework, inspection failures, or route deviations;
- bottlenecks indicated by high waiting times on queue-entry-to-setup transitions.

Because the number of unique routings may be huge, **trace clustering** groups jobs with similar sequences. This identifies the most common process “variants,” and importantly, allows comparing on-time versus late job variants.

For each machine, a resource-focused sequence log is reconstructed by sorting all `Task Start` / `Task End` events by timestamp. This yields the exact historical sequence of jobs on each machine, enabling setup analysis.

### 1.3 Quantifying Scheduling KPIs

#### Job Flow Times, Lead Times, and Makespan

From the job-centric log:

- **Job flow time**: completion timestamp  release timestamp.
- **Order lead time**: completion timestamp  order arrival timestamp.
- **Job makespan**: time from first task start to last task end, ignoring pre-release waiting. This is useful for measuring pure shop-floor throughput time.
- **Operation cycle time**: time from queue entry to task completion.

Distributions rather than simple averages should be used: percentiles \(P_{10}, P_{50}, P_{90}\), and variance, to capture unpredictability.

#### Task Waiting Times

For each operation, compute:

\[
\text{Waiting time} = \text{Task Start} - \text{Queue Entry}
\]

Aggregate waiting times by machine, by job type, by priority class, and by time of day. This quantifies queue congestion and identifies where jobs spend their time.

#### Resource Utilization

For each resource, the timeline between events is classified into mutually exclusive states:

- **Setup time**
- **Processing time** (productive work)
- **Breakdown/repair time**
- **Idle time** (no job assigned)
- **Starved time** (idle while upstream WIP exists but no work is released or transported to it)
- **Blocked time** (completed work cannot move downstream)

This classification is derived by joining the resource-centric log with queue-entry events and breakdown events.

#### Sequence-Dependent Setup Times

The setup event pair `Setup Start` / `Setup End` gives the actual setup duration for each transition. A **sequence-dependent setup matrix** is mined for each machine and, where possible, for each operator.

For machine \(m\), define \(S_m(a \to b)\) as the setup time when the previous job was of type \(a\) and the current job is of type \(b\). The log is grouped by machine and by `(previous_job, current_job)` pairs. For each pair, the distribution of setup durations is fitted. A Bayesian regression model can be used for pairs with few observations, borrowing strength from similar job families.

Importantly, the setup time can depend not only on job type but also on operator, shift, and machine condition. These contextual dependencies are also mined using regression models.

#### Schedule Adherence and Tardiness

The log records **planned** and **actual** task durations. Tardiness is computed at the job level:

\[
\text{Tardiness}_j = \max(0, \text{actual completion}_j - \text{due date}_j)
\]

Additional metrics include:

- percentage of jobs late;
- total tardiness;
- maximum tardiness;
- average earliness for early jobs;
- conditional average lateness;
- due-date adherence per route variant.

For schedule adherence, each task’s actual start is compared to the planned/estimated start, revealing systematic overruns, optimistic estimates, or long unexplained gaps.

#### Impact of Disruptions

Disruption events such as `Breakdown Start / End` and `Priority Change` are superimposed on the job and resource timelines. The analysis then measures the immediate and ripple effects:

- jobs already waiting at the machine when breakdown occurs;
- downstream jobs starved as a result;
- increase in waiting time for jobs released during the disruption window;
- changes in flow time for jobs that were reprioritized due to urgent orders.

Process mining can also reveal **how the shop actually responded** to disruptions: Was the schedule right-shifted? Were jobs rerouted to alternative machines? Did the disruption trigger a cascade of priority changes?

## 2. Diagnosing Scheduling Pathologies

### 2.1 Bottleneck Identification and Impact

Process mining identifies bottleneck resources by combining:

- high utilization (setup + processing);
- high average waiting times at that machine;
- large queue lengths;
- long “queue entry to task start” durations.

A particularly useful technique is **bottleneck walk analysis**: simulate/observe where queues build and dissolve over time. If a resource is persistently the last to drain and has the highest utilization, it is the bottleneck.

Its impact can be quantified by estimating the marginal effect on throughput: an additional hour of bottleneck time increases lead times of affected jobs by far more than an hour due to queue amplification.

### 2.2 Evidence of Poor Task Prioritization

Variant analysis comparing **on-time jobs vs late jobs** often reveals that while both have similar routings, late jobs spend extra time in long waiting states at non-bottleneck machines. The log can be mined for **priority inversions**:

- a job with earlier due date sits in queue while a job with later due date is dispatched;
- a high-priority order waits because the machine is processing a low-priority job;
- a job with a small remaining processing time is delayed to process a large job, even though the smaller job could have cleared the queue faster.

A **waiting-time profile** per job can be clustered. If one cluster contains almost all late jobs and has a characteristic “long wait after first operation,” this is direct evidence that local dispatching rules are not aligning actual execution with global due-date objectives.

### 2.3 Suboptimal Sequencing and Excessive Setup Times

Process mining quantifies setup waste by comparing the actual sequence of jobs on each machine with a benchmark sequence that minimizes setup time for the same set of jobs.

For a given machine and a given day’s set of jobs, a nearest-neighbor heuristic traveling-salesman problem (TSP) over the mined setup matrix gives a **minimum possible setup time**. The actual setup time can then be compared with this lower bound.

If actual setups are consistently 30–50% above the benchmark, the scheduler is likely ignoring sequence dependence.

### 2.4 Downstream Starvation

Starvation is visible in resource timelines as long idle periods at a non-bottleneck machine while the upstream buffer is empty—or, more paradoxically, while upstream WIP is high but no appropriate jobs have been released.

Process mining can combine resource logs and job queues to identify starvation windows. For example, when machine B is idle, how many jobs in upstream queues are ready for B? If there are jobs but none were dispatched, this is a scheduling/coordination failure. If no upstream jobs were ready, the cause may be upstream bottleneck or poor release timing.

### 2.5 Bullwhip Effect in WIP

Process mining generates time series of input rates and output rates for each work center. The variance ratio between inputs and outputs is computed:

\[
\text{Bullwhip ratio} = \frac{\text{variance of output rate}}{\text{variance of input rate}}
\]

If the output variability is greater than input variability, the scheduling policy is amplifying workload oscillations. Dotted charts and WIP-over-time plots reveal waves of WIP building up and collapsing at each station.

## 3. Root Cause Analysis of Scheduling Ineffectiveness

Process mining surface-level symptoms must be traced to root causes.

### 3.1 Myopic Static Dispatching Rules

Local rules such as FCFS and EDD ignore global state. A work center may process a job because it is due soon locally, but that job may then enter a heavily congested downstream bottleneck, while another job with lower local urgency could have kept the downstream resource fed. Process mining demonstrates this through **global trace analysis**: delays are not at the local decision point but appear after the dispatch decision has already been made.

### 3.2 Lack of Real-Time Visibility

Without a real-time integrated view of machine availability, queue lengths, and job progress, each operator makes locally rational decisions that are globally poor. The event log often shows:

- expedite requests and priority changes being applied frequently;
- operators choosing jobs based on intuition or visual queue length;
- jobs waiting even though a capable alternate machine was idle.

### 3.3 Inaccurate Task Duration and Setup Estimates

The log snippet shows planned durations (e.g., 60 minutes) versus actual durations (66.4 minutes). If planners rely on static standard times, schedules will be biased. Process mining reveals that actual durations depend on:

- job complexity and material type;
- specific machine and tooling condition;
- operator skill and shift;
- batch size;
- setup state from the previous job.

These factors are missed by static planning.

### 3.4 Ineffective Handling of Sequence-Dependent Setups

If scheduling does not explicitly consider setup time, jobs are sequenced in arrival or due-date order regardless of setup cost. The result is excessive changeover time, which is effectively lost capacity at the very resources that are already bottlenecks.

### 3.5 Poor Coordination Between Work Centers

Each work center may see high local utilization but the overall line performs poorly. Process mining exposes this as an **optimization of local metrics at the expense of global flow**. Jobs are released too early, producing high WIP, long waits, and complex priority juggling.

### 3.6 Inadequate Disruption Response

In a reactive environment, breakdowns and hot jobs cause constant “firefighting.” Rather than rescheduling globally, the shop often prioritizes the latest urgent job, creating new delays elsewhere. Process mining shows a cascading pattern of expediting after each disruption.

### 3.7 Distinguishing Scheduling Logic Problems from Capacity and Variability

Process mining, combined with simulation, separates these causes:

- **Capacity limitations**: Queues form mainly at resources with utilization near or above effective capacity; freeing capacity upstream does not help because the bottleneck remains.
- **Scheduling logic problems**: Idle resources exist while jobs wait elsewhere; jobs are delayed despite available capacity; alternate machine usage is inconsistent.
- **Process variability**: Even with good scheduling, high variance in task durations and breakdowns creates uncertainty. This is diagnosed by comparing planned versus actual durations and estimating the coefficient of variation.

Regression modeling of waiting times can also help:

\[
\text{Waiting time} = f(\text{resource load}, \text{arrival burst}, \text{job priority}, \text{due date}, \text{setup compatibility}, \text{operator}, \ldots)
\]

If waiting time is explained mainly by load, it is a capacity problem. If it is explained by dispatching decisions and priority inversions even under moderate load, it is a scheduling problem.

## 4. Developing Advanced Data-Driven Scheduling Strategies

Based on the insights from process mining, I propose three sophisticated strategies. They are designed to work together, but each can be implemented incrementally.

---

### Strategy 1: Global-Aware Dynamic Dispatching (GADD)

#### Core Logic

The current problem is local, static dispatching. GADD replaces simple rules with a dynamic composite score computed for every waiting job whenever a machine becomes free.

For a candidate job \(j\) waiting at machine \(r\), the score is:

\[
\text{Score}_j = w_1 \cdot \text{PriorityWeight}_j \cdot \text{Urgency}_j
+ w_2 \cdot \text{DownstreamUrgency}_j
+ w_3 \cdot \text{SetupAffinity}_j
+ w_4 \cdot \text{Hotness}_j
\]

Where:

- **Urgency**: based on the critical ratio:

\[
\text{CR}_j = \frac{\text{Time to due date}}{\text{Remaining processing time including setups}}
\]

Jobs with low CR are more urgent.

- **PriorityWeight**: driven by order priority (e.g., “High (Urgent)” gets a large multiplier).
- **DownstreamUrgency**: estimates whether dispatching this job now will feed a downstream bottleneck. This is calculated from current downstream queue length and the expected time to process those jobs. If the downstream resource is at risk of starvation, jobs destined for it receive a boost.
- **SetupAffinity**: based on the mined sequence-dependent setup matrix. If changing to the family of job \(j\) requires little setup, score increases; if it requires a long changeover, score decreases.
- **Hotness**: an adaptive term for special disruptions, such as recently broken-down machines or urgent expediting requests.

#### Process Mining Inputs

- **Setup matrix** from historical logs: \(S_m(a \to b)\).
- **Remaining processing time**: predicted from historical task duration models.
- **Downstream congestion**: current queue lengths and expected waiting times mined from queue analysis.
- **Weights**: calibrated using simulation optimization, comparing alternative weight sets against historical performance.

#### Pathologies Addressed

- **Poor prioritization**: due-date urgency and priority are explicit.
- **Setup inefficiency**: setup affinity prevents excessive changeovers.
- **Starvation**: downstream urgency feeds bottleneck resources and prevents non-bottleneck starvation.
- **WIP bullwhip**: global awareness reduces local over-dispatching.

#### Expected Impact

- Significant reduction in tardiness and percentage of late jobs.
- Lower WIP because jobs are only dispatched when they are useful downstream.
- Improved bottleneck utilization and overall throughput.
- Reduced setup time share on critical machines.

---

### Strategy 2: Predictive Stochastic Scheduling with Rolling-Horizon Re-Optimization

#### Core Logic

Static deterministic schedules fail because actual durations, breakdowns, and urgent orders are uncertain. Predictive scheduling explicitly models uncertainty and generates a robust schedule that is re-optimized periodically or upon disruption.

The strategy consists of:

1. **Stochastic duration models** derived from process mining.
2. A **rolling-horizon optimizer** that evaluates candidate schedules using discrete-event simulation.
3. **Event-driven rescheduling** when a breakdown occurs, a hot job arrives, or significant schedule drift is detected.

For every job operation, the process-mining layer provides a probability distribution of task time and setup time, not just a mean. For example, a job may have a 50% chance of taking between 45 and 55 minutes, a 20% chance of taking over 70 minutes, and so on.

The optimizer searches over sequences and resource assignments. Each candidate schedule is evaluated over many Monte Carlo samples of the uncertain durations and disruption events. The objective is:

\[
\min \left[ \alpha \cdot \mathbb{E}(\text{weighted tardiness}) + \beta \cdot \text{expected WIP} + \gamma \cdot \text{expected setup cost} \right]
\]

Because this is computationally heavy, approximate scheduling using **surrogate models** from simulation data can speed it up. The chosen schedule is a **robust schedule**, balancing performance and stability.

#### Predictive Maintenance Integration

From the resource event log, maintenance/breakdown data is mined to estimate each machine’s hazard function:

\[
h_m(t) = P(\text{failure at time } t \mid \text{no failure before } t)
\]

These hazard estimates are used in the Monte Carlo evaluation. The scheduler can also propose preventive maintenance windows at times where a machine’s failure probability is high, reducing unexpected disruptions.

#### Process Mining Inputs

- **Duration distributions** conditioned on job family, machine, operator, shift, and sequence.
- **Routing probabilities** for rework or alternate machine usage.
- **Breakdown frequency and repair time distributions**.
- **Priority change probabilities**, estimated from historical urgent-order frequency.

#### Pathologies Addressed

- **Unpredictable lead times**: schedules include uncertainty, so customer promises are realistic.
- **Disruption impact**: rescheduling happens immediately and globally, not reactively at one work center.
- **Inaccurate estimates**: models use actual empirical distributions, not static standards.

#### Expected Impact

- Lower variance in lead times.
- Better on-time delivery, including under high load and disruption scenarios.
- More credible completion time estimates for customers.
- Reduction in expediting and firefighting.

---

### Strategy 3: Sequence-Dependent Setup Reduction with Family Batching and Clustering

#### Core Logic

The third strategy focuses specifically on minimizing changeovers at bottleneck and setup-intensive machines. The key idea is to sequence jobs so that transitions with high setup costs are avoided whenever possible.

Process mining first constructs a **setup dissimilarity matrix** for each machine. Hierarchical clustering is then applied to identify **setup families**—groups of job types that can be processed consecutively with little changeover.

At run time, the sequencing logic works as follows:

1. For each machine, when multiple jobs are waiting:
   - Assign each job to a setup family.
   - From the current family, first consider jobs that require **zero or minimal setup**.
   - Within the same family, select by due date/critical ratio to protect on-time performance.
2. If no job in the current family is available, choose the family with the smallest setup distance from the current state.
3. At bottleneck resources, introduce **time-bounded batching**: accumulate jobs for up to \(T_{\max}\) minutes so that similar jobs can be processed together. The time bound prevents urgent jobs from waiting too long.

#### Advanced Variant: Setup-Aware Genetic Sequencing

For a bottleneck with a larger queue, the scheduling problem is solved as a sequence-dependent scheduling model:

\[
\min \sum_{\text{jobs}} \left( w_j \cdot \text{Tardiness}_j \right) + \lambda \sum_{\text{transitions}} \text{SetupTime}_{a \to b}
\]

A genetic algorithm or large-neighborhood search produces a sequence of jobs that balances setup savings against due-date pressure. This is far superior to simple batching because it considers both setup and delivery simultaneously.

#### Process Mining Inputs

- **Setup matrix** mined for each machine and operator.
- **Job attribute clusters**: material, tooling, shape, tolerances, etc., inferred from logs and case attributes.
- **Bottleneck status**: determines which machines need aggressive setup optimization.
- **Due-date pressure**: derived from critical ratio distributions.

#### Pathologies Addressed

- **Suboptimal sequencing and high setup time** at bottleneck resources.
- **Lost capacity** on critical machines.
- **High WIP**: lower setup time means more available processing time, which reduces queue accumulation.

#### Expected Impact

- Reduced total setup time by 15–40% on the worst machines.
- Increased bottleneck effective capacity.
- Lower lead times for all jobs passing through the setup-intensive resource.
- Better utilization of operators, who spend more time on productive tasks and less on changeovers.

## 5. Simulation, Evaluation, and Continuous Improvement

### 5.1 Building a Discrete-Event Simulation Model from Process Mining

Before deploying any new scheduling strategy, a digital twin of the shop floor is built and calibrated using the mined event log data.

The simulation model includes:

- **Job arrivals**: sampled from the historical distribution of release events.
- **Routings**: sampled from the discovered process variants and transition probabilities.
- **Task durations**: sampled from fitted distributions (e.g., lognormal, gamma) conditioned on job type, machine, and operator.
- **Setup times**: sampled from the sequence-dependent setup time model.
- **Machine breakdowns**: generated using time-to-failure and repair-time distributions mined from resource logs.
- **Priority changes**: sampled from historical frequency of urgent-order arrivals.

The simulation is validated by running the existing scheduling rules (e.g., FCFS/EDD) and comparing simulated KPIs with actual historical KPIs. If the model reproduces historical flow times, WIP levels, and utilization within acceptable tolerance, it is considered a reliable experimentation platform.

### 5.2 Testing Scenarios

Using the simulation, the proposed strategies are tested against the baseline and each other under:

- **Normal load**: current order arrival rate and mix.
- **High load**: demand increased by 20–40%.
- **High urgent-order rate**: double the frequency of hot jobs.
- **Frequent breakdowns**: increased failure rates for bottleneck machines.
- **Setup reduction scenarios**: simulate the effect of a 20% reduction in setup times on each strategy.
- **Bottleneck outage**: e.g., the main CNC machine fails for two full shifts; compare how quickly each strategy recovers.

For each scenario, key KPIs are measured:

- On-Time Delivery % and total tardiness;
- Average and percentile WIP;
- Average cycle time / lead time;
- Resource utilization and setup ratio;
- Number of due-date misses.

The simulation uses **common random numbers** across strategies to reduce variance and enable paired statistical comparisons.

### 5.3 Selecting and Tuning the Best Strategy

After simulation experiments, the most promising strategy—or combination of strategies—is selected. If GADD is chosen, its weights are tuned through simulation-based optimization. If predictive scheduling is chosen, the rolling-horizon frequency and buffer factors are calibrated.

### 5.4 Continuous Process-Mining Monitoring and Adaptation

Once a strategy is deployed, the cycle does not end. A continuous process-mining pipeline monitors live MES data and automatically updates models.

Key elements:

1. **Real-time KPI dashboard**: daily tracking of flow times, queue delays, WIP, utilization, tardiness, and setup times.
2. **Statistical process control (SPC)**: EWMA or CUSUM charts detect when KPIs drift outside expected ranges.
3. **Drift detection in process behavior**: process mining compares newly discovered process variants to the baseline. If new routings or new delay patterns emerge, the scheduling model may be outdated.
4. **Model retraining**: if actual task durations begin to systematically deviate from predicted distributions, duration models are retrained automatically.
5. **Simulation-based A/B testing**: before changing scheduling logic, candidate changes are tested in the simulation using the latest mined data.

This creates a closed-loop scheduling system:

\[
\text{MES Event Log} \rightarrow \text{Process Mining} \rightarrow \text{Diagnosis} \rightarrow \text{Simulation} \rightarrow \text{Optimal Scheduling} \rightarrow \text{Execution} \rightarrow \text{Feedback}
\]

## Conclusion

Precision Parts Inc. cannot overcome its scheduling problems by choosing a better static dispatching rule. It needs a data-driven, closed-loop approach that learns from the MES event log and continuously adapts.

Process mining makes the shop floor visible: it reconstructs actual flows, quantifies waiting times and setup costs, identifies bottlenecks, and exposes where the current scheduling logic fails. These insights directly inform three advanced scheduling strategies:

- **Global-Aware Dynamic Dispatching** for due-date-aware, starvation-aware, setup-aware real-time decisions;
- **Predictive Stochastic Scheduling** for robust planning under uncertainty;
- **Setup Family Batching** for efficiency at bottleneck resources.

Each strategy is tested and refined in a process-mining-calibrated simulation before deployment. After deployment, continuous process mining monitors performance, detects drift, and triggers updates.

This framework transforms Precision Parts from a reactive, locally managed job shop into an adaptive, data-driven operation with improved on-time delivery, lower WIP, predictable lead times, and higher utilization of its most critical resources.
