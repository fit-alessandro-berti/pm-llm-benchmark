## 2. Root-cause analysis

### A. Complexity and repeated document requests

There is a strong relationship between complexity, number of document requests, and total duration:

- Low-complexity cases have **no document requests** and finish in approximately 1.5 hours.
- The medium-complexity case has **one request** and takes nearly 26 hours.
- High-complexity cases have **two or three requests** and take 48–77 hours.

The main delay intervals illustrate this:

- **Case 2002:** Document request to approval = **20 hours**
- **Case 2003:** Second document request to approval = **23 hours**
- **Case 2005:** First document request to approval = **70h 30m**

Case 2005 is especially inefficient because documents are requested on three separate occasions. This suggests incomplete initial requirements, piecemeal review, slow claimant responses, or repeated discovery of missing information.

**Likely root cause:** High-complexity claims require more evidence and verification, but the document requirements may not be identified and communicated in one complete request.

---

### B. Region

At the aggregate level:

- **Region A average:** approximately **24h 55m**
- **Region B average:** approximately **34h 48m**

Region B appears slower, and the longest case, 2005, belongs to Region B. However, the low-complexity comparison does not show a regional disadvantage:

- Region A low-complexity case: **1h 30m**
- Region B low-complexity case: **1h 25m**

For high-complexity claims:

- Region A, Case 2003: **48h 20m**, two requests
- Region B, Case 2005: **77h 05m**, three requests

Thus, Region B may have additional issues for complex claims—such as different documentation rules, customer response times, staffing levels, or local verification requirements—but the data does not show that Region B is generally slower for all claims.

---

### C. Resource effects

#### Adjusters

- **Adjuster_Mike:** handles Cases 2001 and 2003 in Region A.
- **Adjuster_Lisa:** handles Cases 2002, 2004, and 2005 in Region B.

Lisa’s cases have a higher average duration, but this should not automatically be interpreted as poor individual performance:

- Lisa’s low-complexity case, 2004, is the fastest case in the log.
- Her medium- and high-complexity cases require additional documents.
- Mike’s high-complexity case is also very long.

Moreover, **adjuster and region are fully confounded in this sample**: Mike only appears in Region A and Lisa only in Region B. Therefore, it is impossible to distinguish a Lisa effect from a Region B effect using these five cases.

Still, Case 2005 warrants investigation because it contains three separate requests and long gaps between them:

- First to second request: **29h 30m**
- Second to third request: **22h**
- Third request to approval: **19h**

Possible explanations include workload, incomplete review before the first request, lack of a standard checklist, or delays in receiving documents.

#### Managers

Manager_Bill handles both high-complexity cases, while Manager_Ann handles the low- and medium-complexity cases. This is likely a routing rule rather than evidence that Bill causes delays. The high complexity of the cases is a major confounding factor.

#### Finance resources

Payment delays are secondary but visible:

- Case 2003, Finance_Alan: approval to payment = **17 hours**
- Case 2005, Finance_Carl: approval to payment = **4 hours**
- Other cases: 15–45 minutes

Case 2003 therefore has an additional post-approval delay, possibly caused by an overnight queue, payment validation, claim value, or resource availability.

---

## 3. Conclusions and mitigation suggestions

### Most likely root causes

1. **Claim complexity**
   - High-complexity claims require more verification and supporting evidence.

2. **Repeated, fragmented document requests**
   - This is the clearest operational cause of long durations, particularly in Case 2005.

3. **Long waits between document collection and approval**
   - Cases requiring documents wait roughly 19–23 hours after the final request before approval.

4. **Possible Region B or Adjuster_Lisa interaction**
   - Case 2005 is substantially slower than the comparable high-complexity Region A case, but more data is required to separate regional, resource, workload, and case-specific effects.

5. **Post-approval payment delay**
   - Particularly relevant to Case 2003, although it is not the main cause across the log.

### Recommended actions

- **Perform complexity-based triage at submission.** Route high-complexity claims immediately to specialists with appropriate capacity.
- **Use a complete document checklist.** Identify all likely missing documents during the first evaluation and issue one consolidated request.
- **Add intake validation.** Ask customers for complexity-specific evidence during submission rather than after evaluation.
- **Introduce a document portal with automated reminders.** Track whether delays are internal or caused by customer response time.
- **Set milestone SLAs.** Monitor:
  - Evaluation to document request
  - Final document request to approval
  - Approval to payment
- **Trigger escalation for repeated requests.** A second or third request should prompt supervisory review.
- **Review workload by resource and region.** Check whether Lisa, Bill, or Region B teams have larger queues or fewer specialists.
- **Automate payment initiation after approval** where possible.
- **Collect more data before assigning causality.** The sample is too small and attributes are confounded. A larger analysis should control for complexity, claim value, document-response time, workload, region, and working-hours calendars.

Overall, **complexity-driven document rework is the strongest demonstrated root cause**, while resource and regional effects are plausible but not proven by this small log.
