## 2. Root-cause analysis

### A. Complexity is the strongest observed driver

There is a clear relationship between complexity, additional-document requests, and duration.

| Complexity | Cases | Additional-document requests | Typical outcome |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Low | 2001, 2004 | 0 | Completed in about 1.5 hours |
| Medium | 2002 | 1 | Completed in about 26 hours |
| High | 2003, 2005 | 2 and 3 | Completed in about 48–77 hours |

High-complexity claims require repeated document-related work:

- **Case 2003:** 2 requests for additional documents.
- **Case 2005:** 3 requests for additional documents.
- **Case 2002:** 1 request for additional documents.
- **Cases 2001 and 2004:** no document requests.

This is the strongest pattern in the data. Each additional documentation cycle introduces waiting, rework, verification, and likely communication delays with the claimant.

### B. Delay locations by case

#### Case 2002 — Medium complexity, Region B
Key waits:
- Evaluate Claim  Request Additional Documents: **4 h 15 m**
- Request Additional Documents  Approve Claim: **20 h**
- Total: **25 h 55 m**

The largest delay occurs after the documentation request and before approval. Possible explanations include:
- Waiting for the customer to submit documents.
- Slow verification of received documents.
- Documents not being complete the first time.
- An overnight queue before approval.

#### Case 2003 — High complexity, Region A
Key waits:
- First document request  second document request: **6 h**
- Second document request  approval: **23 h**
- Approval  payment: **17 h**
- Total: **48 h 20 m**

The case has two document requests, indicating that the information received after the first request was likely incomplete, insufficient, or required clarification. There is also a substantial delay after approval before payment.

#### Case 2005 — High complexity, Region B
Key waits:
- First document request  second request: **29 h 30 m**
- Second request  third request: **22 h**
- Third request  approval: **19 h**
- Approval  payment: **4 h**
- Total: **77 h 05 m**

This is the clearest example of repeated rework and document-related delay. Most of the duration occurs before approval:

- Evaluate Claim  Approve Claim: approximately **72 h 10 m**.
- The three documentation requests likely indicate that documents were received late, were incomplete, or required several clarification rounds.

## 3. Attribute-level findings

### Complexity

**Finding:** High complexity is strongly associated with long lead time.

- Low complexity: approximately **1.5 hours**.
- Medium complexity: approximately **26 hours**.
- High complexity: approximately **48 to 77 hours**.

**Likely explanation:** Complex claims may involve:
- More supporting documents.
- More policy or coverage checks.
- Higher-value or higher-risk approvals.
- More investigation and validation work.
- More customer interaction and document clarification.
- Escalation to a senior manager, such as `Manager_Bill`.

**Mitigations:**
1. Use a **complex-claim intake checklist** so all likely documents are requested at submission instead of incrementally.
2. Offer a customer portal with:
   - required-document lists,
   - upload validation,
   - reminders for missing documents,
   - real-time status tracking.
3. Create dedicated **high-complexity claim teams** with specialized adjusters and approvers.
4. Apply rules or predictive models to identify likely missing documentation immediately at intake.
5. Define service-level targets for document review and approval after documents arrive.

### Region

**Observed pattern:**
- Region A:
  - Low case 2001: **1 h 30 m**
  - High case 2003: **48 h 20 m**
- Region B:
  - Low case 2004: **1 h 25 m**
  - Medium case 2002: **25 h 55 m**
  - High case 2005: **77 h 05 m**

Region B contains the longest case, 2005, and the medium-complexity delayed case, 2002. However, Region B also processes low-complexity case 2004 very quickly.

**Conclusion:** Region B may have a workload, customer-response, or local operating issue, but the data does **not** show that Region B alone causes delays. Complexity and repeated document requests appear to explain much of the difference.

For example, Region B’s low-complexity claim finishes in only 1 hour 25 minutes, so the region can operate efficiently for straightforward cases.

**Potential regional causes to investigate:**
- Higher claim volumes or staffing shortages in Region B.
- Longer customer response times in that region.
- Region-specific document requirements.
- Slower approval queues.
- Differences in local systems, handoffs, or working hours.

**Mitigations:**
1. Compare workload and staffing capacity across regions.
2. Monitor regional queue times by activity, especially:
   - document verification,
   - approval,
   - payment.
3. Balance work across regions when one region has a backlog.
4. Standardize documentation requirements and operating procedures across regions.
5. Establish regional service-level alerts when a claim remains awaiting documents or approval beyond a threshold.

### Resources

#### Adjusters

`Adjuster_Lisa` handles cases 2002, 2004, and 2005:

| Case | Complexity | Lead time |
|---|---|---:|
| 2004 | Low | 1 h 25 m |
| 2002 | Medium | 25 h 55 m |
| 2005 | High | 77 h 05 m |

`Adjuster_Mike` handles cases 2001 and 2003:

| Case | Complexity | Lead time |
|---|---|---:|
| 2001 | Low | 1 h 30 m |
| 2003 | High | 48 h 20 m |

Both adjusters handle low-complexity cases quickly but have long cases when complexity is high and document requests are required.

**Conclusion:** There is insufficient evidence that either adjuster is inherently slow. The observed delays are more consistent with claim complexity and documentation rework than individual adjuster performance.

That said, `Adjuster_Lisa` is associated with case 2005, which has the longest document-request cycle. This should trigger a review of:
- whether the initial document request was sufficiently complete;
- whether the customer received clear instructions;
- whether follow-up was timely;
- whether Lisa had excessive workload during the period.

#### Managers

`Manager_Bill` approves both high-complexity cases:
- Case 2003: 48 h 20 m total.
- Case 2005: 77 h 05 m total.

This may reflect that Bill handles the more difficult claims, rather than indicating poor performance. The assignment is likely **confounded by complexity**.

However, approval waits should still be monitored:
- Case 2003 had almost **23 hours** between its last document request and approval.
- Case 2005 had **19 hours** between its final document request and approval.

Possible improvement: establish a dedicated approval queue or priority SLA for claims once all required documents are confirmed complete.

#### Finance resources

- `Finance_Alan` processes a low-complexity case quickly, but case 2003 waits **17 hours** between approval and payment.
- `Finance_Carl` pays cases 2002 and 2004 promptly, while case 2005 takes **4 hours** after approval.

The 17-hour payment delay in case 2003 merits investigation, but the data is too limited to conclude that Finance_Alan is a systemic cause. It could be an overnight delay, payment cut-off time, manual payment validation, or a payment queue.

**Mitigations:**
- Automate payments for eligible approved claims.
- Introduce approval-to-payment SLA targets.
- Monitor payment queues and cut-off effects.
- Prioritize claims that have already waited a long time in document-processing stages.

## Overall conclusion

The main root cause of poor performance is **claim complexity leading to repeated documentation requests and long waiting periods before approval**.

### Most likely causal chain
1. Higher complexity claim.
2. Initial documentation is incomplete or unclear.
3. Multiple requests for additional documents are issued.
4. Customer-response and document-review delays accumulate.
5. Approval is delayed by verification and manager queue time.
6. In some cases, payment adds another delay.

### Priority improvement actions
1. **Prevent repeated document requests** through better initial intake and document checklists.
2. **Track document-cycle time** separately: request sent, documents received, documents validated, and rework requested.
3. **Set SLAs** for review after documents are received and for payment after approval.
4. **Investigate Region B** for workload or operational differences, while controlling for complexity.
5. **Evaluate resource performance within the same complexity category**, rather than comparing total durations across fundamentally different claim types.

A key limitation is the small sample size: the log suggests strong hypotheses, but more cases would be needed to prove that a particular region or resource independently causes delay.
