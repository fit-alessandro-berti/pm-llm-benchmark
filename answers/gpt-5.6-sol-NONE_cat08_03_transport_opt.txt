# 1. Process Discovery and Conformance Checking

## 1.1 Build an analysis-ready event log

A single vehicle-day case is useful for analyzing the operating shift, but it is not sufficient for every question. I would create an **object-centric event model** linking:

- Vehicle-day or route execution
- Vehicle
- Driver and shift
- Package and delivery attempt
- Customer stop
- Planned route and stop
- Maintenance work order

This avoids forcing events such as one stop serving several packages into an artificial single case. Where tooling requires conventional event logs, I would derive separate views:

1. **Vehicle-day log:** depot departure through depot return.
2. **Package-attempt log:** allocation, loading, arrival, outcome, and possible redelivery.
3. **Stop log:** travel to stop, arrival, service, and departure.
4. **Vehicle lifecycle log:** usage, warnings, inspections, maintenance, and failures.

### Integration steps

1. **Standardize identifiers**
   - Normalize vehicle, driver, package, route, stop, and work-order IDs.
   - Resolve changed vehicle or driver assignments during a shift.
   - Maintain a crosswalk between planned stops and observed GPS/scanner events.

2. **Normalize timestamps**
   - Convert all systems to one time zone.
   - Correct daylight-saving and clock-drift issues.
   - Preserve source timestamp, ingestion timestamp, and corrected event time.
   - Define deterministic ordering for simultaneous events.

3. **Clean GPS data**
   - Remove impossible jumps and duplicate points.
   - Apply map matching to infer the road segment traveled.
   - Use geofences to infer depot and customer arrival/departure.
   - Compress frequent GPS pings into meaningful intervals such as:
     - Driving
     - Congested travel
     - Idling
     - At customer
     - Unplanned stop

4. **Reconcile scanner and GPS evidence**
   - Scanner events should represent business milestones, while GPS validates physical movement.
   - For example, customer arrival may be the earlier of a valid arrival scan and sustained entry into the customer geofence, subject to confidence rules.
   - Flag contradictions, such as “delivery success” outside the customer geofence.

5. **Integrate planning data**
   - Attach planned stop order, planned arrival/departure times, expected travel and service time, capacity, package attributes, and customer time windows.
   - Preserve both the original plan and any dispatch revisions. Otherwise, conformance analysis may unfairly compare execution with an obsolete plan.

6. **Integrate maintenance information**
   - Link warning events and repairs to vehicle usage before and after the event.
   - Derive accumulated kilometers, engine hours, idle hours, warning history, and days since maintenance.

7. **Derive intervals and attributes**
   - Travel time: departure from one stop to arrival at the next.
   - Service time: customer arrival to departure.
   - Waiting time: arrival before the time window to service start.
   - Idle time: ignition on with little or no movement, excluding legitimate service.
   - Route lateness: actual versus planned arrival.
   - Rework: additional attempt for the same package.
   - Route distance, deadhead distance, overtime, stop density, and load utilization.

8. **Apply quality controls**
   - Report missing-event rates, unmatched stops, GPS coverage, timestamp anomalies, and source conflicts.
   - Assign confidence scores to inferred events.
   - Exclude or separately analyze low-quality cases rather than silently treating them as normal.

### Likely data challenges

- Different systems using inconsistent IDs and clocks.
- GPS noise, urban-canyon effects, tunnels, and missing coverage.
- Driver scans occurring late, early, in bulk, or not at all.
- Multiple packages delivered at one physical stop.
- Failed attempts generating new package cases or routes.
- Route or vehicle changes after initial dispatch.
- Planned stops without execution and unplanned pickups or deliveries.
- Distinguishing congestion from parking search, breaks, customer service, or breakdowns.
- Distinguishing legitimate stops from noncompliant activity.
- Privacy and labor-law concerns involving driver-level monitoring.

Data quality should itself be measured because apparent nonconformance may reflect recording errors rather than operational failure.

---

## 1.2 Discover the actual delivery process

I would first build a simple directly-follows view, then use robust discovery algorithms such as the **Inductive Miner**, which can handle noise and produce a model suitable for conformance checking. Heuristics Miner can complement it for frequency-based behavior. Object-centric discovery is preferable for interactions among routes, stops, packages, and vehicles.

A typical discovered route model might contain:

> Start shift  route assigned  loading  depart depot  travel  arrive customer  service outcome  depart customer  repeat  return depot  end shift

The model should retain alternative paths such as:

- Delivery success
- Customer unavailable
- Address inaccessible
- Parcel refused
- Retry later in the same shift
- Unplanned stop
- Traffic delay
- Driver break
- Vehicle warning or breakdown
- Return-to-depot detour
- Overtime
- Undelivered parcels returned

The model would be enriched with:

- Frequency on each path
- Median, percentile, and variance of durations
- Waiting and service times
- Distance, idle time, estimated fuel, and lateness
- Segmentation by territory, time of day, driver cohort, vehicle type, and weekday

Because unconstrained GPS data creates “spaghetti models,” raw pings should not be treated as activities. Semantic event abstraction and filtering of infrequent behavior are essential. The underlying exceptions remain available for drill-down.

---

## 1.3 Compare actual execution with dispatch plans

The planned route can be represented as a reference process or planned trace. Conformance checking can then operate at three levels.

### Control-flow and sequence conformance

Look for:

- Stops served out of planned sequence
- Skipped planned stops
- Unplanned stops
- Repeat visits and backtracking
- Return to depot during the route
- Deliveries assigned to another vehicle
- Delivery attempted before or after its planned position
- Missing required arrival, outcome, or departure scans

Token replay and alignment-based conformance can quantify missing, inserted, or reordered activities. For route sequences, edit distance or longest-common-subsequence measures can supplement process-model fitness.

### Temporal conformance

Compare:

- Actual and planned depot departure
- Actual and expected travel time between stops
- Actual and planned arrival time
- Compliance with customer time windows
- Actual and planned service time
- Actual return time and shift end
- Accumulated delay propagation throughout the route

A route can follow the planned sequence but still be operationally nonconformant because every leg takes longer than expected.

### Spatial and resource conformance

Assess:

- Actual versus planned distance and corridor
- Geofence compliance
- Capacity and load compliance
- Driver and vehicle assignment changes
- Break and working-hour compliance
- Prohibited-road or access violations
- Detours and excessive deadhead mileage

Useful conformance outputs include:

- Fitness: how well actual traces fit the plan
- Deviation counts and severity
- Lateness-weighted conformance cost
- Distance and fuel impact of deviations
- Precision/generalization of the reference process
- Percentage of deviations that were operationally beneficial

Not every deviation is undesirable. An out-of-sequence stop may improve punctuality under real-time congestion. Deviations should therefore be classified as **harmful, neutral, beneficial, or data-related**.

# 2. Performance Analysis and Bottleneck Identification

## 2.1 Key performance indicators

| KPI | Example calculation from the event log |
|---|---|
| **On-time delivery rate** | Successful deliveries completed within the promised window ÷ eligible deliveries. Report both customer-window and dispatch-plan punctuality. |
| **First-attempt success rate** | Packages delivered successfully on first attempt ÷ packages attempted. |
| **Failed-delivery rate** | Failed attempts ÷ all attempts, segmented by reason. |
| **Average/median stop service time** | `Depart Customer  Arrive Customer`; report median, P90, and variability rather than only the mean. |
| **Travel time per leg** | Next-stop arrival minus previous-stop departure, excluding classified breaks or disruptions as appropriate. |
| **Travel-to-service ratio** | Total travel duration ÷ total customer service duration. |
| **Waiting time** | Service start minus arrival, particularly for early arrival before a time window. |
| **Route cycle time** | Depot return minus depot departure. Shift duration separately includes pre-trip and post-trip work. |
| **Delay against plan** | Actual minus planned arrival/departure by stop and cumulatively by route. |
| **Distance efficiency** | Actual map-matched distance ÷ planned or optimized distance. |
| **Fuel per kilometer/package** | Fuel consumed ÷ distance or delivered packages. If direct fuel data is unavailable, estimate from vehicle model, speed, acceleration, load, and idling, labeling it as estimated. |
| **Idle rate** | Ignition-on stationary time ÷ engine-on time, excluding validated customer service and breaks. |
| **Vehicle capacity utilization** | Initial or average payload/volume ÷ vehicle capacity. Also measure stop and route utilization. |
| **Vehicle productive utilization** | Time driving or serving customers ÷ available vehicle time. |
| **Packages per route-hour/km** | Successfully delivered packages ÷ route duration or distance. |
| **Traffic-delay frequency/duration** | Congestion episodes and minutes above free-flow or historical baseline travel time. |
| **Breakdown rate** | In-shift breakdowns per 1,000 km, engine hours, or route-days. |
| **Maintenance downtime** | Unavailable maintenance hours ÷ scheduled availability hours. |
| **Overtime rate** | Routes or hours beyond scheduled shift limits. |
| **Redelivery cost** | Additional travel, service, handling, and route time attributable to repeat attempts. |

Fuel information is not explicitly listed in the source data. Ideally, fuel-card or telematics fuel data should be added. Until then, fuel KPIs must be treated as model-based estimates.

---

## 2.2 Bottleneck analysis

I would use the following techniques:

### Performance-annotated process maps

Overlay median, P90, frequency, waiting time, cost, and lateness on activities and transitions. This can reveal:

- Long depot loading or dispatch-to-departure delay
- Slow travel corridors
- Excessive customer dwell time
- Long post-failure handling
- Repeated depot returns
- Late end-of-route stops

### Queue and dwell-time analysis

Distinguish:

- Driving delay
- Parking-search time
- Waiting for time-window opening
- Door-to-door service time
- Administrative/scanner time
- Breakdown time

Where possible, identify parking search as low-speed circling near a stop followed by parking and service.

### Variant analysis

Compare frequent variants and outcomes:

- On-time versus late routes
- Low-cost versus high-cost routes
- First-attempt success versus repeat attempts
- Routes with and without breakdowns
- Planned-sequence versus resequenced routes

Measure each variant’s frequency and contribution to total late minutes, fuel, overtime, and failed attempts. A rare variant with a major cost impact may be more important than a frequent minor delay.

### Temporal and geographic analysis

Use heat maps by road segment, geofence, hour, and weekday to identify:

- Recurring congestion hotspots
- Difficult parking areas
- Customers with consistently long dwell times
- Territories with excessive stem distance
- Depot departure waves that create avoidable delay

### Cohort and resource analysis

Compare driver, vehicle, route, and depot cohorts using risk-adjusted measures. Drivers must be compared on similar route difficulty, package mix, congestion, and vehicle condition. Otherwise, hard routes may be mistaken for poor performance.

### Impact quantification

For each bottleneck, estimate:

- Additional minutes per occurrence
- Probability of causing a late delivery
- Additional kilometers and idle time
- Estimated fuel and labor cost
- Overtime contribution
- Packages affected
- Downstream delay propagation

For example:

> A congested corridor adds a median 14 minutes on weekday mornings, affects 18% of routes, and increases the probability of subsequent time-window violations by 22 percentage points.

Regression, survival analysis, gradient-boosted models, and matched-cohort comparisons can quantify associations. Controlled pilots are still needed before claiming causality.

# 3. Root Cause Analysis

## 3.1 Suboptimal route planning

### Possible cause

Static plans may not reflect actual traffic, parking, time-window feasibility, stop density, or service-time variability. Territories may also create excessive crossovers and deadhead travel.

### Validation

- Compare planned distance and sequence with actual and retrospective optimized routes.
- Analyze recurring beneficial resequencing by experienced drivers.
- Identify geographic backtracking and route overlap.
- Compare routes with similar demand but different sequence efficiency.
- Measure whether late routes are already infeasible at dispatch using realistic travel and service distributions.

Evidence would include high planned-to-actual variance, repeated out-of-sequence improvements, or routes whose planned duration exceeds available working time.

---

## 3.2 Inaccurate travel-time estimation

### Possible cause

Dispatch may rely on generic speed assumptions or averages that ignore day, hour, road class, weather, and seasonal patterns.

### Validation

- Compare planned and actual travel time by origin-destination pair and departure interval.
- Plot systematic residuals by hour, corridor, vehicle type, and weather.
- Identify whether errors are biased rather than random.
- Test whether early travel-time underestimation predicts downstream lateness.

A consistent 20% underestimate on certain morning corridors indicates a planning-model problem rather than driver execution.

---

## 3.3 Traffic congestion

### Possible cause

Routes pass through predictable peak congestion or fail to react to incidents.

### Validation

- Infer congestion from low speeds and compare with external traffic data.
- Map delay episodes and their downstream impact.
- Compare the same route segment at different hours.
- Compare routes that used alternative corridors.
- Separate traffic delay from customer service and parking dwell.

Recurring location/time clusters support scheduled avoidance; unpredictable incidents support real-time rerouting.

---

## 3.4 Variable customer service and parking time

### Possible cause

Apartments, security access, signatures, age verification, bulky parcels, poor parking, and multi-package stops may take longer than the planning allowance.

### Validation

- Analyze service-time distributions by customer, building type, parcel characteristics, outcome, and area.
- Use P50/P90 rather than one global average.
- Identify repeated low-speed circulation before arrival.
- Compare planned service allowance with actual dwell time.
- Evaluate whether high-variance stops are placed late in routes, where their impact is amplified.

---

## 3.5 Vehicle breakdowns and maintenance

### Possible cause

Preventive maintenance intervals may not reflect actual duty cycles. Urban stop-start use, idling, and recurring warning codes can accelerate failures.

### Validation

- Link in-shift warning/breakdown events to recent kilometers, engine hours, idle time, vehicle age, and past repairs.
- Use survival or hazard models to estimate failure risk.
- Detect repeat repairs and vehicles with abnormal downtime.
- Compare identical vehicle types under different duty cycles.
- Quantify route abandonment, rescue vehicle use, late packages, and overtime caused by failures.

---

## 3.6 Driver behavior and skill differences

### Possible cause

Differences may exist in route familiarity, scan discipline, parking choices, acceleration/idling, customer interaction, or adherence to recommended sequences.

### Validation

- Compare drivers within matched routes, territories, time bands, and vehicle types.
- Analyze service time, idling, hard acceleration if available, sequence deviations, scan quality, and punctuality.
- Identify practices used by consistently high performers.
- Separate legitimate breaks and traffic from unexplained stationary time.

Driver findings should be used primarily for coaching and process design, not punitive ranking. Data quality, fairness, labor agreements, and privacy must be addressed.

---

## 3.7 Failed delivery attempts

### Possible cause

Customer absence, inaccurate address details, access restrictions, poor time-window forecasting, insufficient notifications, or delivery too early/late.

### Validation

- Analyze failures by reason, time relative to requested window, customer, building type, day, and notification status.
- Trace packages across attempts to measure redelivery loops.
- Compare first-attempt success for notified versus non-notified customers.
- Quantify added distance, time, fuel, and handling cost.
- Identify addresses with recurring failures that require lockers, safe-place instructions, or appointment delivery.

# 4. Data-Driven Optimization Strategies

## Strategy 1: Dynamic traffic-aware routing and controlled resequencing

**Targets:** Congestion, travel-time error, route lateness, excess distance, and overtime.

**Root cause:** Static routing and travel-time estimates that do not reflect current road conditions.

**Process-mining support:**

- Segment-level delay heat maps
- Planned-versus-actual travel-time residuals
- Repeated beneficial driver deviations
- Identification of delay propagation thresholds

**Action:**

- Use historical time-dependent travel-time distributions in planning.
- Update routes with live traffic, new orders, failures, and remaining time windows.
- Trigger resequencing only where predicted benefits exceed disruption costs.
- Provide dispatchers and drivers with the reason and expected gain.
- Freeze imminent stops to avoid constant route changes.

**Expected effects:**

- Higher on-time delivery rate
- Lower travel time and route duration
- Lower actual-to-planned distance
- Lower fuel per package and overtime
- Fewer late stops caused by early-route congestion

A controlled pilot should compare matched territories using static and dynamic routing.

---

## Strategy 2: Redesign territories, stop sequences, and route workload

**Targets:** Backtracking, route overlap, poor capacity use, overloaded routes, and excessive stem mileage.

**Root cause:** Territory boundaries and workload allocations based on nominal stop counts rather than observed effort.

**Process-mining support:**

- Actual route traces and spatial conformance
- Service-time distributions by stop type
- Historical demand and time-window density
- Route variants, route overlap, and deadhead distance
- Identification of chronically infeasible routes

**Action:**

- Recluster stops into compact territories.
- Optimize using expected travel, service-time distributions, parcel volume/weight, parking difficulty, and time windows.
- Balance routes by expected workload and lateness risk, not only packages or stops.
- Use vehicle-routing optimization with capacity and working-hour constraints.
- Maintain limited territory continuity where driver familiarity improves performance.

**Expected effects:**

- Reduced kilometers per package
- Improved vehicle utilization
- Lower travel-to-service ratio
- More stable shift duration
- Fewer late deliveries and less overtime

---

## Strategy 3: Improve time-window management and first-attempt success

**Targets:** Failed deliveries, repeated travel, customer waiting, and wasted capacity.

**Root cause:** Customer absence, broad or inaccurate arrival estimates, missing access instructions, and poor alignment between sequence and requested windows.

**Process-mining support:**

- Failure variants and reason codes
- Customers/areas with recurring failed attempts
- Relationship between failure and arrival relative to time window
- Redelivery loops and their incremental cost
- Service-time and access-delay patterns

**Action:**

- Send predictive arrival notifications and allow customers to confirm or redirect.
- Offer narrower windows once route confidence is high.
- Capture gate codes, safe-place preferences, and building access details.
- Move high-risk deliveries to confirmed slots, lockers, parcel shops, or pickup points.
- Re-optimize the route when a customer reports absence.
- Improve failure reason-code quality to support continuous learning.

**Expected effects:**

- Higher first-attempt success
- Lower failed-delivery and redelivery rates
- Lower distance and fuel per delivered package
- Better punctuality and customer satisfaction
- Reduced route workload on subsequent days

---

## Strategy 4: Usage- and risk-based predictive maintenance

**Targets:** In-shift breakdowns, unplanned stops, vehicle downtime, and maintenance cost.

**Root cause:** Calendar-based maintenance may not account for mileage, stop-start intensity, idle time, warning history, and recurring component failures.

**Process-mining support:**

- Vehicle lifecycle variants
- Link between warning events and subsequent repairs
- Failure hazard by engine hours, mileage, idling, route type, and vehicle age
- Repeated repair loops and downtime analysis

**Action:**

- Develop vehicle risk scores based on duty cycle and warning history.
- Schedule inspections before high-risk vehicles receive critical or long routes.
- Bundle preventive work during low-demand periods.
- Standardize post-repair checks for recurring failure modes.
- Maintain spare-capacity rules based on predicted fleet risk.

**Expected effects:**

- Fewer breakdowns per 1,000 km
- Reduced unscheduled downtime
- Fewer route rescues and late packages
- Lower overtime and potentially lower total maintenance cost
- Higher vehicle availability

---

## Strategy 5: Targeted driver and operational coaching

**Targets:** Excess idling, inconsistent scanning, long dwell times, avoidable sequence deviations, and inefficient driving.

**Root cause:** Gaps in route familiarity, parking strategy, device usage, eco-driving, or customer handling.

**Process-mining support:**

- Risk-adjusted driver cohorts
- Best-performing variants on comparable routes
- Recurrent activity-level deviations
- Idling and dwell-time patterns
- Scanner/GPS inconsistencies

**Action:**

- Coach specific behaviors rather than applying generic training.
- Share best practices from high performers in comparable territories.
- Train on parking approaches, scanner workflow, failed-attempt handling, and eco-driving.
- Improve handheld usability where delays originate from system design.
- Review results jointly with drivers to capture explanations not present in the log.

**Expected effects:**

- Lower stop-time variability
- Reduced idle rate and estimated fuel use
- Better event-data quality
- More consistent punctuality
- Fewer avoidable deviations

# 5. Operational Constraints and Continuous Monitoring

## 5.1 Embed operational constraints in optimization

Optimization should not minimize distance alone. It must produce executable and compliant routes.

### Driver working hours

Include:

- Shift start/end and maximum working time
- Mandatory breaks
- Overtime limits
- Driver qualifications and territory restrictions
- Predicted service/travel uncertainty, not only average duration

Routes should have buffers based on target reliability, such as a P80 or P90 completion estimate.

### Vehicle capacity and suitability

Include:

- Weight, volume, package count, and load sequence
- Vehicle range and charging requirements if electric
- Access restrictions, height/weight limits, and low-emission zones
- Refrigeration, security, or special handling requirements
- Predicted maintenance risk and vehicle availability

Capacity should be tracked through the route as deliveries unload the vehicle and pickups add load.

### Customer time windows

Treat hard and soft windows differently:

- Hard windows must not be violated.
- Soft windows receive explicit penalty costs.
- Early arrival can create waiting time and should not automatically be considered efficient.
- Plans should include expected service-time variability at difficult locations.
- Dynamic rerouting must recheck every remaining time window.

### Business continuity and human constraints

Also account for:

- Depot loading capacity and departure waves
- Driver familiarity and route stability
- Available spare vehicles
- Customer priority and service-level agreements
- Fair workload allocation
- Safe-driving requirements
- Labor agreements, privacy, and explainability

A multi-objective function should balance punctuality, distance, fuel, overtime, workload fairness, first-attempt success, and disruption risk.

---

## 5.2 Continuous process-mining monitoring

A monitoring framework should combine real-time operational control with weekly and monthly improvement analysis.

### Executive dashboard

Track:

- On-time delivery and first-attempt success
- Cost and estimated fuel per package
- Packages per route-hour and kilometer
- Overtime
- Fleet availability and breakdown rate
- Actual versus planned distance
- Customer complaints or service-level breaches

Show baselines, targets, confidence intervals, and trends rather than isolated daily values.

### Route control dashboard

Track:

- Current versus planned stop progression
- Predicted time-window violations
- Accumulated delay
- Remaining capacity and shift time
- Congestion and breakdown alerts
- Recommended interventions

### Process conformance dashboard

Show:

- Frequent process variants
- Sequence deviations
- Skipped and unplanned stops
- Repeat depot visits
- Missing scans
- Route fitness and deviation severity
- Beneficial versus harmful deviations

### Bottleneck and geographic views

Show:

- Travel-delay heat maps by hour
- Parking and dwell-time hotspots
- Customer locations with recurring failures
- Depot dispatch bottlenecks
- Routes with high delay propagation

### Fleet health dashboard

Track:

- Warning-to-failure patterns
- Breakdown rate by vehicle and type
- Upcoming high-risk vehicles
- Scheduled versus unscheduled downtime
- Repeat repairs
- Maintenance compliance and post-maintenance outcomes

### Data-quality dashboard

Track:

- Missing or duplicate events
- GPS coverage
- Unmatched planned/actual stops
- Timestamp conflicts
- Implausible locations and speeds
- Scanner/geofence disagreement

Without this view, operational changes may be confused with changes in recording quality.

---

## 5.3 Measurement and governance cycle

1. Establish a six-month baseline and segment it by season, weekday, territory, and demand.
2. Prioritize changes by expected KPI and financial impact.
3. Pilot each strategy in matched routes or territories.
4. Use difference-in-differences or controlled A/B comparisons where practical.
5. Monitor safety and customer outcomes as guardrails.
6. Review results with dispatchers, drivers, maintenance personnel, and customer service.
7. Update models when process or demand drift appears.
8. Promote successful pilots gradually and retain rollback capability.

Recommended alerts include:

- Predicted time-window breach
- Route duration likely to exceed legal working hours
- Congestion materially affecting remaining stops
- Vehicle failure-risk threshold crossed
- Sudden increase in failed deliveries or service time
- Persistent conformance or data-quality deterioration

The objective is not merely to discover a static process model. Speedy Parcels should create a closed loop in which process mining diagnoses actual behavior, optimization proposes feasible interventions, pilots validate their effects, and dashboards detect whether the improvements remain effective over time.
