**1. Queue Identification and Characterization**

Waiting time (queue time) is defined as the idle time a patient spends between the *completion* of one activity and the *start* of the subsequent activity in their case. From an event log with lifecycle transitions (START/COMPLETE timestamps per activity), it is computed for every consecutive pair of activities \((A_i, A_{i+1})\) in a case as:
\[
W = t_{\text{START}}(A_{i+1}) - t_{\text{COMPLETE}}(A_i)
\]
(only positive values are retained as true waits; negative or overlapping times indicate concurrency or recording issues that must first be cleaned). Intra-activity “service time” (\(t_{\text{COMPLETE}} - t_{\text{START}}\)) is kept separate so that queues are not confounded with processing. Batches of patients waiting for the same resource can also be reconstructed by sorting all ready-to-start events for a resource and calculating sojourn time until that resource becomes free.

Key characterization metrics (computed per activity pair, per resource pool, and optionally segmented by patient type, urgency, specialty, time-of-day, and day-of-week):
- Average, median, and maximum waiting time.
- 75th/90th/95th-percentile waiting times (clinically more relevant than the mean because of right-skew).
- Queue frequency (# of cases that experience a wait >0 / total cases that traverse the pair).
- “Excessive-wait” count and rate (e.g., cases exceeding a service-level threshold such as 20 min for nurse assessment or 30 min for doctor consultation).
- Aggregate impact score = frequency × average wait (or × 90th-percentile wait) to rank economic/clinical cost.
- Contagion indicators: correlation of long wait \(W\) with subsequent service times or with total sojourn time.

Critical queues are identified by a multi-criteria ranking: (a) highest impact scores, (b) longest 90th-percentile waits, (c) high incidence on urgent or new-patient cohorts (who experience higher clinical risk and dissatisfaction), and (d) queues that systematically lengthen total visit duration. Statistical process control charts and heat-maps (activity pairs × hour-of-day) further highlight recurrent bottlenecks. This prioritization is data-driven rather than purely subjective.

**2. Root Cause Analysis**

Process-mining techniques go beyond simple queue metrics to expose underlying mechanisms:
- **Resource bottleneck analysis.** Resource occupancy/utilization time series and resource calendars (from the “Resource” column) reveal overloaded staff, rooms or equipment. Dotted charts ordered by resource show queuing behind scarce resources (e.g., a single ECG technician). Handover-of-work social networks quantify delays introduced by multi-step hand-offs.
- **Service-time variability.** Automatic discovery of process models (Inductive Miner, Heuristics Miner) decorated with performance overlays isolates activities whose duration distributions have high coefficients of variation; these amplify wait times downstream (Kingman’s law of congestion).
- **Scheduling & arrival-pattern mismatch.** Arrival-rate histograms versus appointment-slot density, plus inter-arrival-time analysis stratified by patient type, expose batch arrivals and poor load balancing. New patients or certain specialties may systematically require longer service times that are not reflected in the slot length.
- **Control-flow and dependency issues.** Variant analysis and conformance checking surface optional loops (re-work after tests) or rigid sequential hand-offs that could be parallelized. Decision-point analysis (e.g., after nurse assessment) can show whether urgency or patient type correctly routes people to the right resource pool.
- **Patient-mix effects.** Comparative multi-perspective process mining (filtering by Patient Type = New vs Follow-up, Urgency = Urgent) quantifies how heterogeneous demand creates differential queues.
Taken together, the techniques distinguish capacity shortages from variability, policy, or design problems and supply quantitative evidence (utilization >85 % at peaks, CV of service time >1.0, etc.).

**3. Data-Driven Optimization Strategies**

Three concrete interventions derived directly from the above diagnostics:

**Strategy A – Dynamic, demand-aware resource leveling**  
- *Targets:* Nurse-assessment and cardiology-doctor queues that show peak 90th-percentile waits of 45–60 min during 09:00–11:30.  
- *Root cause:* Under-staffing relative to observed arrival and service-time distributions.  
- *Data support:* Utilization heat-maps and multi-dimensional performance grids demonstrate that adding one nurse and one exam room 08:45–12:30 on high-volume weekdays reduces the impact score of the nurse queue by an estimated 40–55 % (validated via discrete-event simulation fed with mined inter-arrival and service-time distributions).  
- *Expected impact:* Average nurse wait  25–35 %; total visit duration  12–18 min for new patients; modest increase in resource cost offset by higher throughput.

**Strategy B – Adaptive slot-length and staggered-arrival scheduling**  
- *Targets:* All queues downstream of registration, especially doctor-consultation waits for new patients.  
- *Root cause:* Homogeneous appointment slots ignore mined service-time differences (new-patient cardio consults average 32 min vs 18 min for follow-ups) and produce arrival batches.  
- *Data support:* Service-time distributions and arrival curves mined from six months of logs supply new templates: longer slots for new/complex patients, 5–10 min offsets within the same hour, and deliberate “gaps” before high-variability activities. Predictive models (regression or simple ML trained on the event log attributes) can further flag high-risk patients for morning slots.  
- *Expected impact:* 20–30 % reduction in doctor-queue 90th percentile; smoother resource utilization curves; patient arrival variance , lowering overall sojourn time without hiring extra staff.

**Strategy C – Selective parallelization and pre-emptive coordination of diagnostics**  
- *Targets:* ECG / blood-test queues that form after doctor consultation when rooms/techs are busy.  
- *Root cause:* Strict sequential design discovered by process maps; idle capacity exists in the early morning academic slots.  
- *Data support:* Concurrency analysis and “what-if” simulations show that standing orders for low-risk ECGs or bloods can be issued by nurses for selected patient types (identified via decision-tree mining on the log). A lightweight real-time pool of “ready-for-tech” patients reduces hand-off latency.  
- *Expected impact:* 15–25 min average reduction for patients requiring diagnostics; total end-to-end visit time  ~10 %; care quality preserved by clinician overrides for complex cases.

**4. Consideration of Trade-offs and Constraints**

- Strategy A raises short-term labor cost and may increase staff fatigue if peak overtime is not carefully managed; the bottleneck may migrate to check-out or specialist review if those resources stay constant.  
- Strategy B can lengthen some clinicians’ compensated session times or reduce the number of simple follow-ups seen per hour, creating revenue trade-offs; patient no-shows may also change if earlier slots are introduced.  
- Strategy C risks unnecessary tests (cost & radiation/psychological burden) and potential loss of clinical context if parallelization is over-zealous; governance must keep overrides easy.  

Balancing is achieved by multi-objective simulation: minimize a weighted sum of average wait, 90th-percentile wait, total overtime cost and a care-quality surrogate (e.g., average consultation service time must not drop below a clinically set floor). Pareto-front exploration and stakeholder workshops (clinicians, nurses, schedulers, patient representatives) convert the quantitative frontiers into implementable policies. Soft constraints such as “no resource utilization >90 % for >30 min windows” protect care quality and staff wellness.

**5. Measuring Success**

Primary KPIs (tracked continuously from the same event-log schema):  
- Average and 90th-percentile total visit sojourn time.  
- Average / 90th-percentile wait times for the originally critical activity pairs.  
- % of cases whose cumulative waiting time exceeds clinical thresholds (e.g., 45 min).  
- Throughput (completed visits per staffed hour) and resource utilization (target band 70–85 %).  
- Patient-reported wait experience (linked by Case ID if survey data become available).  
- Secondary safety/quality flags: average doctor service time stability, re-work rate, urgency-specific wait times.

Continuous process monitoring re-mines the streaming event log weekly (or daily for high-volume clinics). Control charts on the KPIs, automated drift detection, and side-by-side “before/after” process models with performance decoration provide early warning if a new bottleneck appears or previous gains erode. A lightweight dashboard vanishing the same queue metrics used in the diagnosis phase closes the loop and supports rapid further calibration.
