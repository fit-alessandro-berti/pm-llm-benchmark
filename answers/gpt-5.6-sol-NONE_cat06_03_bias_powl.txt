The XOR branch creates a potential source of bias because applicants selected for **CheckLocalAffiliation (D)** may receive a score uplift, while applicants routed through the silent skip cannot receive that benefit. Thus, two otherwise comparable applicants may obtain different outcomes based on local residency or membership in a recognized community group.

### Why this is problematic

- **Unequal access to an advantage:** Even if local affiliation is not itself a legally protected characteristic, it may be easier for established, well-connected, or long-term residents to demonstrate.
- **Proxy discrimination:** Residence and community membership can correlate with race, ethnicity, nationality, religion, age, or socioeconomic status. The branch may therefore produce disparate impacts on protected groups without explicitly using protected attributes.
- **Compounding effects:** A small score uplift can move borderline cases into approval, better pricing, a lower interest rate, or less stringent terms. Repeated across many applications, a subtle advantage can generate substantial group-level disparities.
- **Inconsistent treatment:** Fairness also depends on how applicants are routed to D. If routing is discretionary, poorly documented, or based on data available only for some applicants, similarly situated applicants may not receive equal consideration.
- **Historical reinforcement:** “Known community group” criteria may reflect existing institutional networks and historical patterns of inclusion. New residents, migrants, people from less-recognized communities, and socially isolated applicants may be systematically disadvantaged.
- **Reduced transparency:** Because skipping is represented by a silent transition, the source of the differential treatment may be difficult for applicants, auditors, or decision-makers to observe.

### Fairness and governance implications

The relevant question is not only whether the criterion is legally permitted, but whether it is **job-relevant to creditworthiness, consistently applied, explainable, and proportionate**. A non-protected feature can still undermine substantive equity or create unlawful disparate impact when it acts as a proxy for protected status.

Appropriate controls would include:

1. Validating that local affiliation has a legitimate and robust relationship to repayment risk.
2. Testing approval rates, pricing, and terms across protected and intersectional groups, both before and after the uplift.
3. Auditing who is routed to D and whether comparable applicants receive comparable opportunities.
4. Making the branch and its effect explicit in logs and decision explanations rather than treating it as operationally invisible.
5. Limiting or removing the uplift if it lacks clear necessity or creates unjustified disparities.
6. Considering less exclusionary alternatives based on direct, individualized financial evidence.

In short, the XOR is not merely an optional procedural step: because one branch changes an applicant’s prospects, it encodes an asymmetric opportunity. Even a small advantage for a nominally non-protected group can systematically shift final decisions and reproduce broader social inequalities.
